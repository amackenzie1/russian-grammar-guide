# Sentences without "to be"

Here is your first Russian sentence:

:::ex
Я студе́нт. | I am a student. | literally "I student"
:::

Two words, and nothing is missing. Russian has no word for "am", "is" or "are" in the present tense, and it has no words for "a" or "the" at all. You don't drop them, shorten them or imply them; they simply aren't part of the language. To say that one thing is another, you put the two side by side and stop.

That means you can already say a great deal with nothing but nouns. No verb endings, no agreement, nothing to conjugate. This section gets as much mileage out of that as it can.

:::vocab
я | I
ты | you | to one person you know well; more in [Pronouns and possessives](#s-02-04)
он | he
она́ | she
э́то | this is, that is, it is, these are
студе́нт, студе́нтка | student | the second form is for a woman
врач | doctor
друг | friend
брат | brother
сестра́ | sister
ма́ма, па́па | mom, dad
чай | tea
ко́фе | coffee
вода́ | water
здесь | here
там | there
до́ма | at home
сейча́с | now, right now
:::

## X is Y: just say both

:::ex
Я врач. | I'm a doctor.
Он студе́нт. | He's a student.
Она́ студе́нтка. | She's a student.
Ты друг. | You're a friend.
Ива́н врач. | Ivan is a doctor.
:::

Notice that **студе́нт** covered "a student" without any help. It can equally be "the student". Which one you'd say in English depends on context, and Russian leaves it to context too. (When it really matters, Russian shows the difference with word order; that's the subject of [Word order](#s-03-09), much later. For now there is simply nothing to choose.)

The same pattern works for saying *where* something is. Words like **здесь** (here), **там** (there) and **до́ма** (at home) go straight after the subject:

:::ex
Ма́ша до́ма. | Masha is at home.
Па́па там. | Dad is over there.
Я здесь. | I'm here.
Ди́ма сейча́с до́ма. | Dima is at home right now.
:::

:::note Only in the present
"To be" is missing only when you're talking about now. In the past and the future Russian does use a verb (**был** "was", **бу́дет** "will be"); you'll meet them in [The past tense](#s-02-07) and [Adverbs and the future of быть](#s-02-08).
:::

## Э́то: the pointing word

The most useful word in this section is **э́то**. It points at something and announces what it is. One word does the work of "this is", "that is", "it is", "these are" and "those are":

:::ex
Э́то Ма́ша. | This is Masha.
Э́то чай. | This is tea. / It's tea.
Э́то ма́ма и па́па. | These are my mom and dad.
Э́то Москва́. | This is Moscow.
Э́то я. | It's me.
:::

Used like this, as the whole "this is" or "these are" of a sentence, **э́то** never changes, whatever it points at: one thing or several, a person or an object, near or far. Russian is much less fussy than English about "this" versus "that"; unless you're contrasting two things, **э́то** is right.

:::warn Two different words
The unchanging **э́то** is the one that stands on its own and means "this is". When "this" sits directly in front of a noun (*this* book, *these* mistakes), Russian uses a different word, **э́тот**, and that one does change to match its noun: э́та кни́га (this book), э́ти оши́бки (these mistakes). So «Э́то оши́бки» is "These are mistakes", and «э́ти оши́бки» is "these mistakes". The changing word arrives in [Pronouns and possessives](#s-02-04). Until then, every э́то in this guide is the unchanging kind.
:::

In the third example English wanted "my"; Russian usually doesn't bother with it for family members when it's obvious whose they are.

## Вот: here you go

**Вот** also points, but it does something different. **Э́то** identifies ("this thing is tea"). **Вот** presents: it's what you say when you hand something over, find what you were looking for, or wave an arm at something in front of you. Think "here is…" or "there's…", or the French *voilà*.

:::ex
Вот чай. | Here's the tea.
Вот Ма́ша. | Here's Masha. / There's Masha.
Вот он! | There he is!
Вот дом. | Here's the house.
Вот и всё. | And that's all. | a fixed phrase
:::

If someone asks what's in the cup, you answer **Э́то чай**. If you're putting the cup down in front of them, you say **Вот чай**.

## Не: not

To make any of these negative, put **не** directly in front of the thing you're denying:

:::ex
Я **не** врач. | I'm not a doctor.
Э́то **не** ко́фе. | This isn't coffee.
Ма́ша **не** до́ма. | Masha isn't home.
Э́то **не** я. | It's not me. / It wasn't me.
:::

**Не** is always unstressed and leans on the next word, so **не до́ма** sounds like one word. And as you may remember from [Stress and vowel reduction](#s-01-03), an unstressed **е** weakens: **не** sounds closer to "ni" than to "nye".

:::warn не is not нет
**Нет** is the word "no", the opposite of **да** (yes). **Не** is "not". English speakers mix them up because "no" and "not" look alike. You answer with **нет**; you negate a word with **не**: **Нет, э́то не чай.** "No, that's not tea."
:::

## Questions: change the tune, not the words

English turns a statement into a question by shuffling words around ("He is home" → "Is he home?"). Russian leaves every word exactly where it was and changes only the melody.

:::ex
Э́то чай. | This is tea.
Э́то чай? | Is this tea?
Ма́ша до́ма. | Masha is at home.
Ма́ша до́ма? | Is Masha at home?
:::

In writing, the only difference is the question mark. In speech, the difference is this: in a statement your voice drifts downward to the end. In a yes/no question the pitch jumps sharply *up* on the stressed syllable of the word you're asking about, then drops again straight after. It is a much steeper rise than the gentle lift at the end of an English question, and it sits on one syllable rather than at the end of the sentence. Get this wrong and Russians will hear a statement and wait for you to continue.

Because the rise marks the word in question, moving it changes what you're asking:

:::ex
Ма́ша **до́ма**? | Is Masha *at home*? (or somewhere else?)
**Ма́ша** до́ма? | Is it *Masha* who's at home? (or someone else?)
:::

The answers are **да** (yes) and **нет** (no). Russians very often answer by repeating the key word instead of, or as well as, saying yes:

:::dialogue
А: Э́то ко́фе? | Is this coffee?
Б: Да, ко́фе. | Yes, it is.
А: Ма́ша до́ма? | Is Masha home?
Б: Нет, не до́ма. | No, she isn't.
А: Ты студе́нт? | Are you a student?
Б: Студе́нт. | I am.
:::

There is nothing like English "it is" / "she isn't" to hide behind, so you repeat the actual word.

## Кто э́то? Что э́то?

Two question words get you a long way: **кто** (who) and **что** (what). Remember from [How consonants behave](#s-01-04) that **что** is pronounced as if it were written with **ш**: "shto".

:::ex
Кто э́то? | Who is this?
Что э́то? | What is this?
Кто там? | Who's there? | what you say when someone knocks
Кто он? | Who is he? / What does he do?
Что там? | What's over there? / What's going on there?
:::

Questions with a question word don't use the sharp rise; the question word itself gets the emphasis and the voice falls afterwards.

:::dialogue
А: Кто э́то? | Who's this?
Б: Э́то А́ня. | This is Anya.
А: А э́то кто? | And who's this?
Б: Э́то брат. | That's my brother.
А: А что э́то? | And what's this?
Б: Э́то не чай и не ко́фе. Э́то вода́. | It's not tea and it's not coffee. It's water.
:::

Word order in these questions is loose: **Кто э́то?** and **Э́то кто?** are both normal. The second is a little more casual and is common in follow-up questions like the one above.

:::note кто covers animals too
**Кто** is for anything alive: people, dogs, mosquitoes. **Что** is for objects and ideas. Pointing at an unfamiliar animal, a Russian asks **Кто э́то?**, not **Что э́то?**
:::

## И, а, но: three words for "and" and "but"

English has two joining words here; Russian has three, and the one in the middle is the interesting one.

:::vocab
и | and | adds something similar
а | and, whereas, but | sets two things side by side as different
но | but | the second thing goes against what you'd expect
то́же | also, too
:::

**И** simply adds. The two things are on the same side:

:::ex
А́ня и Ма́ша до́ма. | Anya and Masha are at home.
Э́то чай и ко́фе. | This is tea and coffee.
Он врач, и она́ врач. | He's a doctor, and she's a doctor.
:::

**А** compares. It puts two facts next to each other and says: these are different, look. There is no conflict between them, just a contrast. English uses "and" for this, or sometimes "but", or "whereas", or just a pause.

:::ex
Я студе́нт, а он врач. | I'm a student, and he's a doctor.
Ма́ша до́ма, а Са́ша здесь. | Masha is at home, and Sasha is here.
Э́то чай, а э́то ко́фе. | This is tea, and this is coffee.
:::

In all three, **и** would sound wrong to a Russian ear, because **и** would suggest the facts are alike. A quick test: if you could say either "and" or "but" in English without really changing the meaning ("I'm a student and he's a doctor" / "I'm a student but he's a doctor"), Russian wants **а**.

**А** has two more everyday jobs. First, it is the "but" in "not X, but Y":

:::ex
Э́то не чай, а ко́фе. | This isn't tea, it's coffee.
Он не студе́нт, а врач. | He's not a student but a doctor.
Не здесь, а там. | Not here, over there.
:::

Second, it opens a question that shifts the conversation to a new subject, like English "And…?" or "What about…?":

:::ex
Я студе́нт. А ты? | I'm a student. And you?
А э́то кто? | And who's this?
А где Ди́ма? | And where's Dima? | где = where
:::

**Но** is a real "but": the second half pushes against what the first half led you to expect.

:::ex
Он врач, но сейча́с он до́ма. | He's a doctor, but right now he's at home.
Э́то чай, но не мой. | It's tea, but it's not mine. | мой = my, mine
Она́ здесь, но не одна́. | She's here, but not alone.
:::

A doctor ought to be at work; the tea ought to be for you; **но** flags the surprise. If there's no thwarted expectation, just two different facts, it's **а**. Learners overuse **но** because it maps so neatly onto "but". When in doubt between the two, **а** is the better guess.

## То́же: me too

**То́же** means "also" in the sense of "the same goes for this one". It follows the thing you're adding:

:::ex
Я студе́нт. — Я то́же. | I'm a student. — Me too.
Ма́ша то́же здесь. | Masha is here too.
Он врач, и она́ то́же врач. | He's a doctor, and she's a doctor too.
Я то́же не врач. | I'm not a doctor either.
:::

The last example shows that **то́же** works unchanged in negative sentences, where English switches from "too" to "either".

## The dash

When a sentence is just noun + noun, written Russian usually puts a long dash where the "is" would have been:

:::ex
Москва́ — столи́ца. | Moscow is the capital.
Ива́н — врач. | Ivan is a doctor.
Во́лга — река́. | The Volga is a river.
:::

The dash isn't a word and isn't pronounced, though people do make a small pause there. It's a courtesy to the reader, showing where the subject ends and the statement about it begins. Without it, two nouns in a row can look like a single name or a list.

The convention is narrower than it looks. The dash goes between two *nouns*. It is normally not written after a pronoun (**Я студе́нт**, **Он врач**, **Э́то чай**), not before **не** (**Ива́н не врач**), and not before words like **здесь** or **до́ма** (**Ма́ша до́ма**). In text messages people skip it half the time anyway.

## Putting it together

Everything in this conversation uses only what's on this page.

:::dialogue
Ди́ма: Кто э́то? | Who's this?
О́ля: Э́то Са́ша. Он друг. | This is Sasha. He's a friend.
Ди́ма: Он то́же студе́нт? | Is he a student too?
О́ля: Нет, он не студе́нт, а врач. | No, he's not a student, he's a doctor.
Ди́ма: А э́то кто? Сестра́? | And who's this? Your sister?
О́ля: Нет, э́то ма́ма! | No, that's my mom!
Ди́ма: А где она́ сейча́с? | And where is she now?
О́ля: До́ма. А вот и чай. | At home. And here's the tea.
:::

One small thing to notice: Sasha is **он** here, though the name ends in **-а** like **Ма́ша** and **О́ля**. **Са́ша** is short for both Алекса́ндр and Алекса́ндра, so only context tells you. That brings us to the next topic: every Russian noun, not just names of people, is a "he", a "she" or an "it".
