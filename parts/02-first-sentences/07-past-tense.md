# The past tense

After six present-tense endings and a table of irregular verbs, the past tense is a relief. A verb has just four past forms, they are made the same way for almost every verb in the language, and most of the verbs that were irregular in the present are perfectly regular here.

The surprise is what those four forms are. The past tense does not change for person: there is no separate form for "I", "you" or "they". It changes for gender and number, like an adjective. A man and a woman reporting the same thing use different verb forms.

:::vocab
быть | to be
вчера́ | yesterday
сего́дня | today | the г is pronounced "v"
ра́ньше | before, earlier; used to
давно́ | a long time ago; for a long time
неда́вно | recently
тогда́ | then, at that time
у́тром | in the morning
ве́чером | in the evening
весь день | all day
до́лго | for a long time
хо́лодно | cold | as in "it's cold"
:::

## How to make it

Take the infinitive, remove **-ть**, and add:

| | Ending | чита́ть | говори́ть |
|---|---|---|---|
| masculine | **-л** | чита́**л** | говори́**л** |
| neuter | **-ло** | чита́**ло** | говори́**ло** |
| feminine | **-ла** | чита́**ла** | говори́**ла** |
| plural | **-ли** | чита́**ли** | говори́**ли** |

After the **л**, these are the noun endings you know by heart: nothing for masculine, **-о** for neuter, **-а** for feminine, **-и** for plural.

The form is chosen by the subject. If the subject is masculine singular, the verb ends in **-л**, whether that subject is **я**, **ты**, **он** or a noun:

:::ex
Ива́н чита́л. | Ivan was reading.
Ма́ша чита́ла. | Masha was reading.
Они́ чита́ли. | They were reading.
Я чита́л. | I was reading. | a man speaking
Я чита́ла. | I was reading. | a woman speaking
Ты чита́л? | Were you reading? | to a man
Ты чита́ла? | Were you reading? | to a woman
Мы чита́ли. | We were reading.
:::

So with **я** you choose the form that fits yourself, and with **ты** the one that fits the person you are talking to. This has a practical side: to ask a friend **Что ты де́лал вчера́?** or **Что ты де́лала вчера́?** you have to know who you are writing to. And you can often tell whether a Russian message or a first-person novel was written by a man or a woman from the first verb.

**Вы** always takes the plural form, even when it is the polite "you" to one person:

:::ex
Вы чита́ли э́то? | Have you read this? | to one person politely, or to several
Где вы рабо́тали ра́ньше? | Where did you work before?
:::

:::aside Why the past tense behaves like an adjective
Because it used to be one. The **-л** form began as a participle, something like "having read", used with the verb "to be": "I am having-read". Then "to be" dropped out of the present tense, as you know, and the participle was left to carry the meaning alone. It never acquired personal endings, and it kept its adjective-style agreement.
:::

### The verbs that were irregular yesterday

The past is built on the infinitive, not on the present-tense stem. So verbs whose present tense you had to memorize mostly need no special attention here:

| Infinitive | Present (я) | Past |
|---|---|---|
| жить | живу́ | жил |
| писа́ть | пишу́ | писа́л |
| е́хать | е́ду | е́хал |
| ждать | жду | ждал |
| брать | беру́ | брал |
| пить | пью | пил |
| петь | пою́ | пел |
| дава́ть | даю́ | дава́л |
| хоте́ть | хочу́ | хоте́л |
| люби́ть | люблю́ | люби́л |
| спать | сплю | спал |
| ви́деть | ви́жу | ви́дел |

:::ex
Ра́ньше мы жи́ли там. | We used to live there.
Она́ до́лго писа́ла отве́т. | She spent a long time writing a reply.
Я ждал, а ты не звони́л. | I waited, and you didn't call.
Де́ти уже́ спа́ли. | The children were already asleep.
Мы сиде́ли и смотре́ли телеви́зор. | We sat and watched TV.
Я весь день рабо́тала. | I worked all day.
Ба́бушка о́чень хорошо́ пе́ла. | Grandma used to sing very well.
:::

## One past tense for many English ones

English has "I read", "I was reading", "I used to read", "I have read" and "I had been reading". The forms on this page can stand for any of them; context and time words do the sorting.

:::ex
Что ты де́лал вчера́? — Рабо́тал. | What did you do yesterday? — I worked.
Я чита́л, а она́ смотре́ла фильм. | I was reading and she was watching a film.
Ра́ньше я кури́л, а сейча́с не курю́. | I used to smoke, but now I don't.
Ты смотре́л э́тот сериа́л? | Have you seen this series?
Мы давно́ не отдыха́ли. | We haven't had a break in a long time.
:::

:::note The other half of the story
Almost every Russian verb comes as a pair. The verbs you have learned so far are all of one kind, called imperfective, which describe an action as a process, a habit or a bare fact. Each has a partner, the perfective, that presents the action as one completed whole: **чита́л** "was reading, read", against **прочита́л** "read it through, finished it". Perfective pasts are formed exactly as on this page, so nothing here will need unlearning. What the pairs mean and when to use which is the subject of Part 4, beginning with [Aspect: the big idea](#s-04-01).
:::

## Был: "to be" comes back

In the present tense Russian has no "is". In the past it does have a "was": the past of **быть** (to be).

| masculine | neuter | feminine | plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| **был** | **бы́ло** | **была́** | **бы́ли** |

Notice the stress: on the ending in the feminine, on the stem everywhere else.

Take any of the verbless sentences from earlier in this part, put the right form of **был** after the subject, and it moves into the past:

:::ex
Он до́ма. → Он был до́ма. | He's at home. → He was at home.
Ма́ша здесь. → Ма́ша была́ здесь. | Masha is here. → Masha was here.
Ключи́ там. → Ключи́ бы́ли там. | The keys are there. → The keys were there.
Письмо́ здесь. → Письмо́ бы́ло здесь. | The letter is here. → The letter was here.
:::

:::dialogue
А: Где ты была́ вчера́? | Where were you yesterday?
Б: До́ма. А ты где был? | At home. And where were you?
А: Я то́же был до́ма. Весь день. | I was home too. All day.
:::

With **э́то**, the verb agrees with the noun being identified, not with **э́то**:

:::ex
Э́то был Ди́ма. | That was Dima.
Э́то была́ хоро́шая иде́я. | That was a good idea.
Э́то бы́ли мои́ друзья́. | Those were my friends.
Э́то был о́чень интере́сный фильм. | That was a very interesting film.
Кто э́то был? | Who was that?
:::

When there is no noun at all, when **э́то** refers to a situation in general, the neuter **бы́ло** is used. Neuter is Russian's default for anything without a gender of its own:

:::ex
Э́то бы́ло давно́. | That was a long time ago.
Э́то бы́ло о́чень вку́сно. | That was delicious.
Что э́то бы́ло? | What was that?
Всё бы́ло хорошо́. | Everything was fine.
:::

The same **бы́ло** appears in sentences that have no subject at all, such as statements about the weather. English fills the gap with a dummy "it"; Russian leaves it empty:

:::ex
Вчера́ бы́ло хо́лодно. | It was cold yesterday.
Бы́ло уже́ по́здно. | It was already late.
Там бы́ло о́чень интере́сно. | It was really interesting there.
:::

This is only a first look. Subjectless sentences are a big part of Russian, and they are treated in full in [Sentences without a subject](#s-06-09).

:::note Он был врач?
You might want to say "He was a doctor" by adding **был** to **Он врач**. That is possible, but in the past and future Russian usually puts the second noun into another case, the instrumental. It is explained in [The instrumental](#s-03-06). For now, use **был** for where someone was, with **э́то**, and with words like **хо́лодно**.
:::

### [[ns:Не́ был]]: the stress jumps

With **не**, something unusual happens. In three of the four forms the stress moves off the verb and onto **не**:

| masculine | neuter | feminine | plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[[ns:не́ был]]** | **[[ns:не́ было]]** | **не была́** | **[[ns:не́ были]]** |

Only the feminine keeps its stress, on the ending as before. So in **Я там [[ns:не́ был]]** the last two words sound like a single word stressed on its first syllable, with **был** reduced to a weak tail.

:::ex
Я там [[ns:не́ был]]. | I wasn't there. / I've never been there. | a man speaking
Она́ вчера́ не была́ до́ма. | She wasn't home yesterday.
Мы давно́ здесь [[ns:не́ были]]. | We haven't been here in a long time.
Э́то [[ns:не́ было]] смешно́. | That wasn't funny.
:::

## Stress: watch the feminine

For most verbs the past-tense stress sits where it does in the infinitive, in all four forms: **чита́ла**, **говори́ли**, **рабо́тало**.

But **была́** is not alone. A group of short, very common verbs throws the stress onto the ending in the feminine, and only there:

| | masculine | neuter | feminine | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| быть | был | бы́ло | **была́** | бы́ли |
| жить | жил | жи́ло | **жила́** | жи́ли |
| пить | пил | пи́ло | **пила́** | пи́ли |
| спать | спал | спа́ло | **спала́** | спа́ли |
| ждать | ждал | жда́ло | **ждала́** | жда́ли |
| брать | брал | бра́ло | **брала́** | бра́ли |

:::ex
Она́ жила́ здесь, а они́ жи́ли там. | She lived here, and they lived over there.
Я до́лго не спала́. | I was awake for a long time. | a woman speaking
Ма́ма ждала́, а мы не звони́ли. | Mom was waiting, and we didn't call.
:::

All of these have a one-syllable masculine form, but that alone does not predict the shift: **знал, зна́ла**, **пел, пе́ла** and **ел, е́ла** stay put. Learn the six above; other verbs with this habit can be learned as they come.

## A few irregular pasts

Some verbs do not make their past by the simple rule. Most have an infinitive that does not end in the usual vowel + **-ть**.

| | masculine | neuter | feminine | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **идти́** (to go on foot) | шёл | шло | шла | шли |
| **мочь** (to be able) | мог | могло́ | могла́ | могли́ |
| **есть** (to eat) | ел | е́ло | е́ла | е́ли |
| **нести́** (to carry) | нёс | несло́ | несла́ | несли́ |
| **везти́** (to carry by vehicle) | вёз | везло́ | везла́ | везли́ |
| **умере́ть** (to die) | у́мер | у́мерло | умерла́ | у́мерли |
| **привы́кнуть** (to get used to) | привы́к | привы́кло | привы́кла | привы́кли |

What they share: when the stem ends in a consonant, the masculine form has no **-л**. The **л** is present in the other three forms. **Мог** but **могла́**; **нёс** but **несла́**; **у́мер** but **умерла́**. (**Ел** does keep its **л**; it is in the table because you could not have guessed it from **есть**.)

**Идти́** is the odd one out: its past tense comes from a different root altogether, as "went" does in English. **Шёл, шла, шли** are among the most frequent words in Russian, so they are worth drilling.

:::ex
Мы шли домо́й и разгова́ривали. | We were walking home and talking.
Шёл дождь. | It was raining.
Я не мог спать. | I couldn't sleep. | a man speaking
Она́ не могла́ говори́ть. | She couldn't speak.
Ты уже́ е́ла? | Have you eaten yet? | to a woman
Он нёс чемода́н, а я несла́ су́мки. | He was carrying the suitcase and I was carrying the bags.
Де́душка у́мер давно́. | Grandpa died a long time ago.
Я уже́ привы́кла. | I'm used to it by now. | a woman speaking
:::

The two verbs at the bottom of the table belong to the perfective kind mentioned in the note above; they are here for their shape.

## Negation and questions

Nothing new: **не** goes before the verb, and yes/no questions are made with the voice.

:::ex
Я не знал. | I didn't know.
Она́ не звони́ла. | She didn't call.
Мы вчера́ не рабо́тали. | We didn't work yesterday.
Ты ви́дел? | Did you see?
Вы спа́ли? | Were you asleep?
:::

In questions with **кто**, the verb is masculine singular, even if the person asking expects the answer to be a woman or several people. With **что**, it is neuter:

:::ex
Кто звони́л? — Ма́ма. | Who called? — Mom.
Кто э́то писа́л? | Who wrote this?
Что там лежа́ло? | What was lying there?
Что бы́ло вчера́? | What happened yesterday?
:::

**Хоте́ть** and **мочь** take an infinitive in the past just as they do in the present:

:::ex
Я хоте́ла спать. | I was sleepy. | a woman speaking
Он не хоте́л ждать. | He didn't want to wait.
Мы не могли́ рабо́тать. | We couldn't work.
:::

:::note The tense inside "that" clauses
English says "I thought you **were** asleep", moving the second verb into the past to match the first. Russian does not. The inner clause keeps the tense the thought had at the time: **Я ду́мал, что ты спишь**, literally "I thought that you are sleeping". Likewise **Я не зна́ла, что он до́ма**, "I didn't know he was home". More in [That, whether, and reported speech](#s-06-02).
:::

## A conversation

:::dialogue
О́ля: Приве́т! Где ты был вчера́? Я звони́ла. | Hi! Where were you yesterday? I called.
Ми́ша: До́ма. Я спал. | At home. I was asleep.
О́ля: Весь день? | All day?
Ми́ша: Нет, у́тром рабо́тал. А ты что де́лала? | No, in the morning I worked. What did you do?
О́ля: Мы гуля́ли. Бы́ло хо́лодно, но о́чень краси́во. | We went for a walk. It was cold, but really beautiful.
Ми́ша: «Мы» — э́то кто? | Who's "we"?
О́ля: Я и А́ня. Ди́ма то́же хоте́л, но не мог. | Me and Anya. Dima wanted to come too, but he couldn't.
Ми́ша: А ве́чером? | And in the evening?
О́ля: Ве́чером сиде́ли до́ма, пи́ли чай и смотре́ли ста́рый фильм. | In the evening we stayed in, drank tea and watched an old film.
:::

Read it again and check each verb against its subject: **был** and **спал** for Misha, **звони́ла** and **де́лала** for Olya, **гуля́ли** and **пи́ли** for the two friends, **бы́ло** for the weather, which has no subject at all.
