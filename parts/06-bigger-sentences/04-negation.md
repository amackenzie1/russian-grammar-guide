# Negation

You have been saying не since your first Russian sentence, and нет + genitive since the genitive. Still missing: where exactly не goes, the double (triple, quadruple) negative, why direct objects sometimes slip into the genitive, and a second set of negative words — не́кого, не́чего, не́когда — that mean "there is no one / nothing / no time *to*…".

The main point for English speakers: Russian negatives don't cancel out. They agree with each other. "Nobody never said nothing" is the *correct* way to build the sentence.

:::vocab
никто́ | nobody | gen./acc. никого́
ничто́ | nothing | in practice almost always gen. ничего́
никогда́ | never |
нигде́ / никуда́ | nowhere (location / direction) |
ника́к | in no way; just can't |
никако́й | no, not any (kind of) |
ни ра́зу | not once |
ни оди́н | not a single |
совсе́м не | not at all |
не совсе́м | not quite |
да́же не | not even |
не́кого, не́чего | there is nobody / nothing to… | + infinitive
не́когда | there is no time to… |
обраща́ть внима́ние | to pay attention | impf.; pf. обрати́ть
:::

## Не goes right before what it negates

Не is an unstressed word that leans on whatever follows, and it negates exactly that. Usually that is the verb, which negates the sentence as a whole. Aim it at another word and the meaning shifts: the event happened, just not with *that* detail.

:::ex
Я **не** звони́л Ма́ше вчера́. | I didn't call Masha yesterday. | no call happened
**Не я** звони́л Ма́ше вчера́. | It wasn't me who called Masha yesterday. | someone did
Я звони́л **не Ма́ше**. | It wasn't Masha I called. | I called someone else
Я звони́л Ма́ше **не вчера́**. | It wasn't yesterday that I called Masha. | some other day
:::

English needs "it wasn't… that…" for this; Russian just moves не. Often the correct detail follows with **а**: the не X, а Y pattern.

:::ex
Э́то сде́лал **не я, а** Ди́ма. | It wasn't me who did it, it was Dima.
Мы е́дем **не в Со́чи, а** в Каза́нь. | We're going not to Sochi but to Kazan.
Он **не** глу́пый, **а** про́сто лени́вый. | He's not stupid, just lazy.
:::

Не also softens adjectives and adverbs: **не о́чень** ("not very"), **не совсе́м** ("not quite"), **не всегда́** ("not always"), **не все** ("not everyone"). The order of не and the other word matters:

:::ex
Я **не совсе́м** по́нял. | I didn't quite understand.
Я **совсе́м не** по́нял. | I didn't understand at all.
**Не все** пришли́. | Not everyone came.
:::

## Нет: "no" and "there isn't"

**Нет** has two unrelated jobs. One is the answer "no". The other is the negative of есть: "there is no…", "…is not here", "…doesn't have". In that job it puts the missing thing in the **genitive** (see [the genitive section](#s-03-04)).

:::ex
— Ты го́лоден? — **Нет**. | "Are you hungry?" "No."
У меня́ **нет вре́мени**. | I have no time.
В холоди́льнике **нет молока́**. | There's no milk in the fridge.
**Ма́ши** сейча́с **нет**. | Masha isn't here right now.
:::

Нет is present tense only. In the past it becomes **[[ns:не́ было]]**, in the future **не бу́дет** — always these fixed "it" forms (neuter singular in the past, third person singular in the future), whatever is missing, because a genitive can't be a subject and the verb has nothing to agree with.

:::ex
Вчера́ **[[ns:не́ было]]** вре́мени. | Yesterday there was no time.
Ди́мы **[[ns:не́ было]]** на рабо́те. | Dima wasn't at work.
За́втра меня́ **не бу́дет** в о́фисе. | I won't be in the office tomorrow.
Не волну́йся, пробле́м **не бу́дет**. | Don't worry, there won't be any problems.
:::

:::note Where the stress goes
In the past tense of быть, не steals the stress in three of the four forms: [[ns:не́ был, не́ было, не́ были]] — but не была́. That's why you see [[ns:не́ было]] written with an accent on не in this guide.
:::

Compare two ways of saying someone "wasn't" somewhere. With a nominative subject and an agreeing verb, you're talking about the person's life experience. With the genitive, you're talking about their absence from a scene.

:::ex
Я никогда́ [[ns:не́ был]] в Пари́же. | I've never been to Paris.
Меня́ [[ns:не́ было]] в Пари́же, когда́ э́то случи́лось. | I wasn't in Paris when it happened.
:::

## Ни-words: negatives that travel in packs

Take a question word, put **ни** in front, and you get its negative:

| Question word | Negative | |
|---|---|---|
| кто | **никто́** | nobody |
| что | **ничто́** (ничего́) | nothing |
| где | **нигде́** | nowhere (location) |
| куда́ | **никуда́** | nowhere (direction) |
| отку́да | **ниотку́да** | from nowhere |
| когда́ | **никогда́** | never |
| как | **ника́к** | in no way |
| како́й | **никако́й** | no… at all |
| чей | **ниче́й** | nobody's |

Now the rule that matters: **a ни-word always comes with не on the verb.** The ни-word doesn't make the sentence negative by itself; it only *agrees* with a negation that не (or нет) provides. You can stack as many as you like.

:::ex
**Никто́ не** пришёл. | Nobody came.
Я **ничего́ не** зна́ю. | I don't know anything.
Мы ле́том **никуда́ не** е́здили. | We didn't go anywhere this summer.
Я **нигде́ не** могу́ найти́ очки́. | I can't find my glasses anywhere.
У меня́ **нет никаки́х** пла́нов. | I have no plans whatsoever.
Он **никогда́ никому́ ничего́ не** расска́зывает. | He never tells anyone anything.
:::

Where English switches to "any-" after the first negative (*never… any**one**… any**thing***), Russian keeps every word negative. Leaving out не is a typical learner slip, and the result is simply broken: [[ns:Я ничего знаю]] is not a sentence.

The only time a ни-word appears alone is in a short answer, where the verb is understood:

:::dialogue
А: Кто звони́л? | Who called?
Б: Никто́. | Nobody.
А: Что ты ему́ сказа́л? | What did you say to him?
Б: Ничего́. | Nothing.
:::

Никто́ and ничто́ decline like кто and что: никого́, никому́, нике́м; ничего́, ничему́, ниче́м. The nominative ничто́ is uncommon — as the object or with нет you need ничего́ anyway, and even as a subject ничего́ often takes over (Ничего́ не случи́лось — "Nothing happened"). Никако́й declines like како́й and means "none whatsoever": Э́то не име́ет **никако́го** значе́ния — "It makes no difference at all."

**Ника́к** has an everyday use: with не + verb it means "just can't manage to", however hard you try.

:::ex
Я **ника́к не** могу́ запо́мнить э́то сло́во. | I just can't memorize this word.
:::

### Prepositions split them

When a preposition is needed, it goes *inside* the word, between ни and the pronoun, and the whole thing is written as three words: **ни с кем**, **ни о чём**, **ни у кого́**, **ни к кому́**, **ни от кого́**, **ни в чём**.

:::ex
Я **ни с кем** не разгова́ривал. | I didn't talk to anyone.
Он **ни о чём** не ду́мает. | He isn't thinking about anything.
**Ни у кого́** нет заря́дки? | Doesn't anyone have a charger?
Я **ни в чём** не винова́т. | I'm not guilty of anything.
**Ни за что**! | No way! Not for anything! | stress on что
:::

### Ни as "not a single"

By itself, **ни** before a noun — typically with оди́н or a "smallest unit" word — means "not even one". The verb, as always, has не, and the noun is in the genitive where a direct object or нет is involved.

:::ex
Я **ни ра́зу** [[ns:не́ был]] в Сиби́ри. | I haven't been to Siberia even once.
**Ни оди́н** челове́к не пришёл. | Not a single person came.
Он не сказа́л **ни сло́ва**. | He didn't say a word.
У меня́ нет **ни копе́йки**. | I haven't got a kopeck.
В до́ме [[ns:не́ было]] **ни одного́** сту́ла. | There wasn't a single chair in the house.
:::

Doubled, it is "neither… nor" (**ни… ни**), which you saw in [Joining clauses](#s-06-01): Я не ем **ни** мя́са, **ни** ры́бы.

## The genitive of negation

With нет, the genitive is compulsory. With an ordinary negated verb, the direct object *may* go into the genitive instead of the accusative, and here there are only tendencies, not rules. Both of these are correct:

:::ex
Я не чита́л **э́ту кни́гу**. | I haven't read this book. | accusative
Я не чита́л **э́той кни́ги**. | I haven't read this book. | genitive
:::

The feel behind the choice: the **accusative** treats the object as a definite, real thing that the action just didn't reach. The **genitive** makes the negation more sweeping — "none of it", "not any" — and suits things that are abstract, indefinite, or whose very presence is being denied.

In modern everyday speech the accusative has been winning, so when in doubt, use it. But the genitive is still the normal choice in these situations:

- **With ни-words, ни оди́н, никако́й** — the "not any at all" cases: Я не получа́л **никаки́х пи́сем** ("I haven't received any letters").
- **With abstract nouns**: не теря́й **вре́мени** (don't waste time), не ви́жу **ра́зницы** (I see no difference), не име́ю **пра́ва** (I have no right), не вы́звал **интере́са** (aroused no interest).
- **In fixed phrases**: не обраща́й **внима́ния** (pay no attention), не име́ет **значе́ния** (it doesn't matter), не игра́ет **ро́ли** (plays no role).
- **With э́то**: Я **э́того** не говори́л ("I never said that"); Я **э́того** не знал.
- **After verbs of perception and wanting, for something that isn't there**: Я не слы́шал **звонка́** ("I didn't hear the bell ring"); Я не ви́жу здесь **оши́бок** ("I don't see any mistakes here").

And the accusative is the normal choice for a specific person or a specific, identified thing, and for plain everyday objects generally (the first three examples below). The last three show the genitive holding on in set phrases and with an abstract noun:

:::ex
Я давно́ не ви́дел **Ма́шу**. | I haven't seen Masha in ages.
Ты не закры́л **дверь**. | You didn't close the door.
Я не люблю́ **ры́бу**. | I don't like fish.
Не обраща́й на него́ **внима́ния**. | Don't pay any attention to him.
Э́то не име́ет **значе́ния**. | It doesn't matter.
Он не сказа́л нам **пра́вды**. / Он не сказа́л нам **пра́вду**. | He didn't tell us the truth. | both heard
:::

## Не́кого, не́чего, не́когда: nothing to do, nobody to ask

Russian has a second series of negative words, built with **stressed не́-** instead of unstressed ни-. They look similar and mean something quite different.

Я **ничего́ не** де́лаю says that I *am doing* nothing. **Мне не́чего де́лать** says that *there is nothing for me to do* — nothing exists that I could do. The не́-words deny that the thing, person, place or time is available at all.

| | ни- (+ не + verb) | не́- (+ infinitive) |
|---|---|---|
| кого́ | никого́ не спроси́л — asked no one | **не́кого** спроси́ть — no one to ask |
| что / чего́ | ничего́ не де́лаю — I do nothing | **не́чего** де́лать — nothing to do |
| где | нигде́ не сижу́ | **не́где** сесть — nowhere to sit |
| куда́ | никуда́ не иду́ | **не́куда** идти́ — nowhere to go |
| когда́ | никогда́ не отдыха́ю | **не́когда** отдыха́ть — no time to rest |
| заче́м | — | **не́зачем** спеши́ть — no need to hurry |

The construction has its own grammar:

1. The не́-word is followed by an **infinitive**.
2. There is **no second не**.
3. The person for whom the thing is lacking goes in the **dative**.
4. Past and future are shown with **бы́ло** and **бу́дет**.

:::ex
Мне **не́кого** спроси́ть. | I have no one to ask.
Здесь **не́чего** де́лать. | There's nothing to do here.
Извини́, мне сейча́с **не́когда**. | Sorry, I have no time right now.
В авто́бусе **не́где** бы́ло сесть. | There was nowhere to sit on the bus.
Ему́ **не́куда** идти́. | He has nowhere to go.
Тебе́ **не́чего** боя́ться. | You have nothing to be afraid of.
Ско́ро нам **не́чего** бу́дет есть. | Soon we'll have nothing to eat.
:::

The pronouns decline — the infinitive decides the case — but they have no nominative: не́кого, не́кому, не́кем; не́чего, не́чему, не́чем. And prepositions split them, just like the ни-words, with не keeping the stress: **[[ns:не́ с кем]]**, **[[ns:не́ о чем]]**, **[[ns:не́ к кому]]**, **[[ns:не́ на что]]**.

:::ex
Мне **не́чем** писа́ть. | I have nothing to write with. | instr.
**Не́кому** да́же позвони́ть. | There isn't even anyone to call. | dat.
Мне **[[ns:не́ с кем]]** поговори́ть. | I have no one to talk to.
Нам **[[ns:не́ о чем]]** говори́ть. | We have nothing to talk about.
Ей **[[ns:не́ на что]]** жить. | She has nothing to live on.
— Спаси́бо! — **[[ns:Не́ за что]]**. | "Thanks!" "Don't mention it." | literally "there's nothing (to thank me) for"
:::

:::warn Никогда́ vs не́когда
Я **никогда́ не** отдыха́ю — "I never rest." Мне **не́когда** отдыха́ть — "I have no time to rest." One letter and the stress are all that separate them in writing, so listen for the stress: unstressed ни-, stressed не́-.
:::

The positive counterpart — "there *is* someone to ask, something to say" — uses **есть** (past бы́ло, future бу́дет) + the plain question word + infinitive:

:::ex
Мне **есть что** сказа́ть. | I have something to say.
Тебе́ **есть с кем** оста́вить ребёнка? | Do you have someone to leave the child with?
Там **бы́ло где** перекуси́ть. | There was somewhere to grab a bite there.
В ста́рости **бу́дет что** вспо́мнить. | There'll be things to remember in old age.
:::

## Turning it up and down

A few words that adjust the strength of не:

:::ex
Я **совсе́м не** уста́л. | I'm not tired at all.
Э́то **во́все не** так. | That's not so at all. | a bit more bookish than совсе́м не
Э́то **далеко́ не** всё. | That's far from all.
Он **да́же не** извини́лся. | He didn't even apologize.
Я **ничу́ть не** удивлён. | I'm not surprised in the least.
Мне э́то **не о́чень** нра́вится. | I don't like it much.
:::

And two negatives *can* make a positive when each has its own verb: **не мочь не** + infinitive is "can't help but".

:::ex
Я **не могу́ не** согласи́ться. | I can't disagree. I have to agree.
**Нельзя́ не** заме́тить, что он измени́лся. | You can't fail to notice he's changed.
:::

## Не in questions: politeness, not negativity

Russian routinely puts не into a question to make it softer. The не offers the other person an easy way to say no; it doesn't mean the speaker expects a negative answer. English does something similar with "you wouldn't happen to…" and "won't you…".

:::ex
Вы **не** зна́ете, где здесь метро́? | Do you happen to know where the metro is?
Вы **не** подска́жете, кото́рый час? | Could you tell me the time?
**Не** хо́чешь ча́ю? | Would you like some tea?
Ты **не** ви́дел мой телефо́н? | Have you seen my phone, by any chance?
Вы **не** выхо́дите на сле́дующей? | Are you getting off at the next stop? | said on a crowded bus to someone blocking the door
:::

Asking a stranger Вы зна́ете, где метро́? without не is grammatical but sounds like a quiz. With не it's a request.

### Answering a negative question

This is a known rough patch, for Russians too. If someone asks **Ты [[ns:не́ был]] в Москве́?**, a bare да is ambiguous, so people answer with the verb:

:::dialogue
А: Ты [[ns:не́ был]] в Москве́? | You haven't been to Moscow?
Б: Нет, [[ns:не́ был]]. | No, I haven't.
Б: Да, [[ns:не́ был]]. | Right, I haven't. | also possible: confirming the negative
Б: Нет, был. | Yes, I have. | contradicting it: literally "no, (I) was"
:::

**Нет, [[ns:не́ был]]** is the safest "no". To contradict, Russians say **Нет, был**, or more naturally **Почему́? Был** ("Why (do you think so)? I have") or **Был, коне́чно**. After a negative question, don't rely on да or нет alone; repeat the verb.

:::summary
- Не stands directly before the word it negates.
- Нет / [[ns:не́ было]] / не бу́дет + genitive = absence.
- Ни-words **require** не; stack freely; prepositions go in the middle (ни с кем).
- Negated direct objects: accusative by default; genitive for "not any", abstractions and set phrases.
- Не́-words + infinitive (person in dative) = "there is no… to…"; positive version: есть что / есть с кем.
:::
