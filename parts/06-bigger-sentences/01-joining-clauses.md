# Joining clauses

Up to now most of our sentences have been one thought long. Real speech glues thoughts together: *this happened **because** that happened*, *I'll call **when** I arrive*, *it's small **but** it's central*. Russian does this with small joining words — conjunctions — and the good news is that they are just words. They don't decline, they don't conjugate, and each clause on either side of them is built exactly the way you already know.

What takes practice is choosing the right one (Russian splits English "and/but" three ways), a couple of tense habits that differ from English, and the commas. Let's take them by meaning.

:::vocab
зато́ | but on the other hand, but in return |
одна́ко | however | slightly bookish
и́ли / ли́бо | or | ли́бо is a little more formal
потому́ что | because |
поэ́тому | so, that's why |
так что | so (as a result) |
е́сли | if |
пока́ | while; for now |
как то́лько | as soon as |
хотя́ | although |
всё равно́ | anyway, all the same |
успе́ть | to manage in time | pf.; impf. успева́ть
опозда́ть | to be late | pf.; impf. опа́здывать
оста́ться | to stay, remain | pf.; impf. остава́ться
:::

## И, а, но: three words for two English ones

You met these back in [the very first sentences](#s-02-01). Now that you can build proper clauses, here is the full picture.

**И** adds. The second thing goes along with the first: more of the same, or the natural next step.

**Но** cancels an expectation. The first clause makes you expect something, and the second says "nope".

**А** sits in between, and it is the one English lacks. It sets two things side by side and says *they're different* — without any clash between them. Typically the topic switches: *I* do this, **а** *she* does that; *today* this, **а** *tomorrow* that. English uses "and", "but" or "whereas" here, more or less at random.

:::ex
Я гото́влю у́жин, **и** А́ня мне помога́ет. | I'm making dinner, and Anya is helping me. | same direction: addition
Я гото́влю у́жин, **а** А́ня смо́трит сериа́л. | I'm making dinner, and/while Anya is watching a series. | two people, two different activities
Я гото́влю у́жин, **но** А́ня не хо́чет есть. | I'm making dinner, but Anya doesn't want to eat. | expectation defeated
:::

A good test for **а**: could you say "whereas" or "as for…"? Then it's **а**. A good test for **но**: could you say "but despite that"? Then it's **но**.

:::ex
Ди́ма живёт в Москве́, а его́ брат — в Каза́ни. | Dima lives in Moscow, and his brother in Kazan.
Вчера́ бы́ло тепло́, а сего́дня хо́лодно. | Yesterday it was warm, but today it's cold.
Я звони́л три ра́за, но никто́ не отве́тил. | I called three times, but nobody answered.
Он бога́тый, но несча́стный. | He's rich but unhappy.
:::

Three more jobs for **а**, all of them very common:

- **Correcting: не X, а Y.** "Not X but Y" is always **а**, never но.
- **Opening a follow-up question.** *And you? And what about tomorrow?*
- **Mild "and yet"**, when something doesn't fit: this is where а and но overlap, and но is simply stronger.

:::ex
Э́то не ко́фе, **а** чай. | This isn't coffee, it's tea.
Я прие́ду не в пя́тницу, **а** в суббо́ту. | I'm coming not on Friday but on Saturday.
У меня́ всё хорошо́. **А** у тебя́? | I'm fine. And you?
**А** что мы бу́дем де́лать за́втра? | And what are we doing tomorrow?
Уже́ ию́нь, **а** на у́лице хо́лодно. | It's already June, and yet it's cold out.
:::

## More ways to say "and", "but" and "or"

**Зато́** is "but" with a consolation prize: the first clause is a minus, the second is the plus that makes up for it.

**Одна́ко** is a slightly dressier но ("however"). **Да** — the same word as "yes", but unstressed — is a folksy "and" or "but" that lives mostly in proverbs, fairy tales and set pairs. You need to recognize it, not use it.

:::ex
Кварти́ра ма́ленькая, **зато́** в це́нтре. | The apartment is small, but (on the plus side) it's central.
Зарпла́та небольша́я, **зато́** рабо́та интере́сная. | The pay isn't great, but then the work is interesting.
Он обеща́л прийти́, **одна́ко** не пришёл. | He promised to come; however, he didn't.
Мал, **да** уда́л. | Small but mighty. | proverb; да = но
хлеб **да** соль | bread and salt | да = и
:::

**И́ли** is "or". **Ли́бо** means the same and sounds a touch more formal; doubled (**и́ли… и́ли**, **ли́бо… ли́бо**) it is "either… or".

:::ex
Ты бу́дешь чай и́ли ко́фе? | Will you have tea or coffee?
Позвони́ и́ли напиши́. | Call or text.
И́ли ты идёшь с на́ми, и́ли остаёшься до́ма. | Either you come with us or you stay home.
Мо́жно заплати́ть ли́бо ка́ртой, ли́бо нали́чными. | You can pay either by card or in cash.
:::

Doubling works for и as well. **И… и** is "both… and". **Ни… ни** is "neither… nor", and since Russian negatives pile up, the verb still takes не (the full story is in [Negation](#s-06-04)). **Не то́лько… но и** is "not only… but also".

:::ex
Она́ говори́т **и** по-англи́йски, **и** по-неме́цки. | She speaks both English and German.
У меня́ нет **ни** вре́мени, **ни** де́нег. | I have neither time nor money.
Он **не** пьёт **ни** ча́я, **ни** ко́фе. | He drinks neither tea nor coffee.
Мы **не то́лько** колле́ги, **но и** друзья́. | We're not only colleagues but friends too.
:::

And one for rephrasing: **то есть** — "that is, I mean". In messages it is abbreviated [[ns:т. е.]]

:::ex
Я прие́ду в сре́ду, то есть послеза́втра. | I'm coming on Wednesday, that is, the day after tomorrow.
:::

## Why and so: cause and result

The everyday "because" is **потому́ что**. For the reverse direction — cause first, then result — use **поэ́тому** ("that's why, so").

:::ex
Я не пришёл, **потому́ что** заболе́л. | I didn't come because I got sick.
Я заболе́л, **поэ́тому** не пришёл. | I got sick, so I didn't come.
— Почему́ ты не ешь? — **Потому́ что** не хочу́. | "Why aren't you eating?" "Because I don't want to."
:::

A clause with потому́ что doesn't like to come first. If you want to open with the reason, use **так как** ("since, as"), which is happy in either position and sounds a little more written.

:::ex
**Так как** биле́тов на по́езд уже́ [[ns:не́ было]], мы пое́хали на авто́бусе. | Since there were no train tickets left, we went by bus.
:::

Two more "because"s carry an attitude. **Из-за того́ что** blames: the cause is a nuisance. **Благодаря́ тому́ что** thanks: the cause is something good. (They are built from the prepositions из-за + genitive "because of" and благодаря́ + dative "thanks to", which you can also use with plain nouns: из-за дождя́, благодаря́ тебе́.)

:::ex
Мы опозда́ли **из-за того́, что** Ди́ма проспа́л. | We were late because Dima overslept.
**Благодаря́ тому́ что** ты помо́г, мы всё успе́ли. | Thanks to your helping, we got everything done in time.
Из-за про́бок я е́хал два часа́. | Because of traffic I was on the road for two hours.
:::

**Так что** also means "so", but where поэ́тому names a reason, так что draws a conclusion — often a practical one aimed at the listener.

:::ex
Я сего́дня за́нят, **так что** приходи́ за́втра. | I'm busy today, so come tomorrow.
Магази́н уже́ закры́т, **так что** хле́ба не бу́дет. | The store's already closed, so there'll be no bread.
:::

Don't confuse так что with **так…, что** and **тако́й…, что** split between the two clauses. Those mean "so … that": the first clause gives the degree, and the что-clause gives what came of it. Так goes with verbs, adverbs and -о words, тако́й with a long adjective or a noun (the same division you will meet again in [Short adjectives and predicate words](#s-06-08)). **Сто́лько…, что** is "so much, so many … that".

:::ex
Я **так** уста́л, **что** сра́зу засну́л. | I was so tired that I fell asleep at once.
Бы́ло **так** хо́лодно, **что** никто́ не хоте́л выходи́ть. | It was so cold that nobody wanted to go out.
Фильм был **тако́й** ску́чный, **что** мы ушли́. | The movie was so boring that we left.
У меня́ **сто́лько** рабо́ты, **что** я не зна́ю, с чего́ нача́ть. | I have so much work that I don't know where to start.
:::

Finally, **раз** — literally "one time" — works as a conversational "since, seeing that": the reason is a fact both of you already accept.

:::ex
**Раз** ты уже́ здесь, помоги́ мне. | Since you're here already, give me a hand.
**Раз** обеща́л — сде́лай. | If you promised, do it. | literally "since (you) promised"
:::

## If: real conditions

**Е́сли** is "if". For things that can really happen, you just use ordinary tenses on both sides. There is one difference from English, and it is a big one: **when the condition lies in the future, Russian puts it in the future tense.** English says "if it *rains*, we'll stay home" with a present tense doing duty for the future. Russian says what it means.

:::ex
Е́сли **бу́дет** дождь, мы **оста́немся** до́ма. | If it rains, we'll stay home. | literally "if there will be rain"
Е́сли **уви́дишь** Са́шу, скажи́ ему́, что я его́ ищу́. | If you see Sasha, tell him I'm looking for him.
Е́сли ты не **придёшь**, я оби́жусь. | If you don't come, I'll be offended.
Е́сли **бу́дешь** мно́го рабо́тать, всё полу́чится. | If you work hard, it will all work out.
:::

The present tense after е́сли means the present — something true now or in general.

:::ex
Е́сли хо́чешь, пойдём вме́сте. | If you want, let's go together.
Е́сли он так говори́т, зна́чит, э́то пра́вда. | If he says so, then it's true.
Е́сли я не сплю во́семь часо́в, у меня́ боли́т голова́. | If I don't sleep eight hours, I get a headache.
:::

The result clause may be introduced by **то** ("then"), mostly in longer or more careful sentences: Е́сли ты не про́тив, то я откро́ю окно́ — "If you don't mind, (then) I'll open the window."

All of this is about conditions that are open. For the imaginary kind — "if I *were* you", "if I *had known*" — Russian switches to е́сли бы + past tense, which we covered in [бы and что́бы](#s-04-07): Е́сли бы я знал, я бы пришёл.

:::warn Е́сли is not "whether"
English "if" has a second life: "I don't know if he's coming." That is not a condition, and е́сли can't do it. Russian uses the particle ли — see [the next section](#s-06-02).
:::

## When, while, until, before, after

**Когда́** is "when", and like е́сли it takes a real future tense for future events: **Когда́ прие́ду, позвоню́** — "When I arrive, I'll call."

With когда́, aspect does the work that English does with "was doing" versus "did". Two imperfectives run in parallel; two perfectives happen one after the other; one of each means one action was in progress when the other cut in.

:::ex
Когда́ я **гото́вил** у́жин, я **слу́шал** подка́ст. | While I was making dinner, I was listening to a podcast. | impf. + impf.: side by side
Когда́ я **пришёл**, она́ **гото́вила** у́жин. | When I came, she was making dinner. | pf. + impf.: I walked in on it
Когда́ я **пришёл**, она́ **пригото́вила** у́жин. | When I came, she made dinner. | pf. + pf.: first one, then the other
Когда́ **зако́нчишь**, напиши́ мне. | When you finish, text me.
:::

**Пока́** means "while" — for as long as something lasts. Its clause is naturally imperfective.

:::ex
Пока́ ты спал, я всё убра́л. | While you were sleeping, I cleaned everything up.
Пока́ я живу́ здесь, маши́на мне не нужна́. | As long as I live here, I don't need a car.
:::

Add **не** and a perfective verb, and it flips to "until": **пока́ не**. The logic is "while X has not yet happened". The не here isn't really negative — don't translate it.

:::ex
Подожди́, **пока́** я **не** верну́сь. | Wait until I get back.
Я никуда́ не пойду́, **пока́ не** допишу́ отчёт. | I'm not going anywhere until I finish the report.
Мы гуля́ли, **пока́ не** стемне́ло. | We walked until it got dark.
:::

**Как то́лько** is "as soon as".

:::ex
Как то́лько прие́ду, позвоню́. | I'll call as soon as I arrive.
Как то́лько он вошёл, все замолча́ли. | As soon as he walked in, everyone went quiet.
:::

"After" and "before" need a word of warning. **По́сле** and **пе́ред** / **до** are prepositions: they attach to nouns (по́сле рабо́ты, пе́ред сном, до обе́да). To attach them to a whole clause, Russian plugs in the pronoun **то** in the right case, and then **как**: **по́сле того́ как**, **пе́ред тем как**, **до того́ как**. You can't skip it: [[ns:после он уехал]] is not Russian.

:::ex
**По́сле того́ как** он уе́хал, в до́ме ста́ло ти́хо. | After he left, the house got quiet.
Позвони́ мне **до того́, как** при́мешь реше́ние. | Call me before you make a decision.
Мы познако́мились ещё **до того́, как** я перее́хал в Москву́. | We met back before I moved to Moscow.
:::

**Пе́ред тем как** is "right before"; **до того́ как** is "at any time before"; **пре́жде чем** is "before" in the sense of "first do this, then that". When both clauses have the same subject, all three usually take a plain **infinitive** — like English "before leaving".

:::ex
**Пе́ред тем как** уйти́, вы́ключи свет. | Before you leave, turn off the light.
**Пре́жде чем** отвеча́ть, поду́май. | Think before you answer.
Я всегда́ чита́ю о́тзывы, **пе́ред тем как** что-то купи́ть. | I always read reviews before buying something.
:::

**С тех пор как** is "since" in the time sense (never the "because" sense — that was так как and раз).

:::ex
**С тех пор как** мы перее́хали, я хожу́ на рабо́ту пешко́м. | Since we moved, I've been walking to work.
:::

## Although, anyway, in order to

**Хотя́** is "although". The heavier **несмотря́ на то что** is "despite the fact that". In speech, the main clause often gets a **всё равно́** ("anyway, all the same") to hammer the point home — and often всё равно́ does the whole job by itself with а or но.

:::ex
**Хотя́** бы́ло уже́ по́здно, мы пошли́ гуля́ть. | Although it was already late, we went for a walk.
Он помо́г мне, **несмотря́ на то что** о́чень уста́л. | He helped me despite being very tired.
Я ему́ сто раз говори́л, а он **всё равно́** опа́здывает. | I've told him a hundred times, and he's late anyway.
**Хотя́** я и не голо́дный, я **всё равно́** что-[[ns:нибудь]] съем. | Even though I'm not hungry, I'll eat something anyway.
:::

Purpose — "in order to", "so that" — is the job of **что́бы**, which you know from [бы and что́бы](#s-04-07). The short version: same subject → infinitive; different subject → past-tense form.

:::ex
Я пришёл, что́бы помо́чь. | I came to help.
Я пришёл, что́бы ты не скуча́л. | I came so that you wouldn't be bored.
:::

## То́же, та́кже, ещё и

All three can come out as "also" in English, and they are not interchangeable.

**То́же** means "likewise": someone or something *else* matches what was just said. The new information is who or what joins in.

**Та́кже** adds a further item for the *same* subject — "in addition". In speech it sounds formal; it is most at home in **а та́кже** ("as well as").

**Ещё** (often **ещё и**) is the everyday way to add an item: "on top of that".

:::ex
Я уста́л. — Я **то́же**. | "I'm tired." "Me too."
Ма́ша у́чит испа́нский, и О́ля **то́же**. | Masha is learning Spanish, and so is Olya.
Он рабо́тает в ба́нке, **а та́кже** преподаёт в университе́те. | He works at a bank and also teaches at a university.
Она́ врач, а **ещё** она́ пи́шет кни́ги. | She's a doctor, and she also writes books.
Он опозда́л, да **ещё и** забы́л докуме́нты. | He was late, and forgot the documents on top of that.
:::

So "I also speak French" depends on what you mean. *I, too* (like you) → Я то́же говорю́ по-францу́зски. *French too* (besides English) → Я ещё говорю́ по-францу́зски.

## Commas

English commas are a matter of taste. Russian commas are a matter of rules, and the rule here is simple: **a clause boundary gets a comma.**

- Before **а**, **но**, **зато́**, **одна́ко** — always.
- Before a clause-opening **что**, **что́бы**, **потому́ что**, **е́сли**, **когда́**, **пока́**, **хотя́**, **так что**, **как то́лько** — always. If that clause comes first, the comma goes at its end; if it sits in the middle, it gets one on each side.
- Before **и** and **и́ли** — only when they join two full clauses, each with its own subject. No comma when they join two words or two verbs with one subject. But repeated и… и, ни… ни, и́ли… и́ли get a comma between the parts.

:::ex
Он пришёл и сра́зу лёг спать. | He came and went straight to bed. | one subject, two verbs: no comma
Пошёл дождь, и мы верну́лись домо́й. | It started raining, and we went back home. | two clauses: comma
Я зна́ю, что ты прав. | I know you're right.
Челове́к, кото́рый звони́л у́тром, придёт за́втра. | The man who called this morning is coming tomorrow. | commas on both sides
:::

With the multi-word conjunctions (потому́ что, по́сле того́ как, несмотря́ на то что…) the comma normally goes before the whole thing. It can move inside — before что or как — when the first half is stressed: Я пришёл **потому́**, что ты проси́л — "The reason I came is that you asked."

:::note Nobody will fine you
Russians themselves agonize over commas, and in casual messages plenty get dropped. But the basic ones — before что, а, но, е́сли, когда́, потому́ что — are second nature to every literate speaker, and getting them right makes your writing look instantly more Russian.
:::
