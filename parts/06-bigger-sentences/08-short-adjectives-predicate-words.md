# Short adjectives and predicate words

Every adjective you've used so far — но́вый, хоро́ший, больша́я — is what grammars call a *long form*. Many Russian adjectives also have a second, clipped version: **гото́в**, **рад**, **за́нят**, **прав**. You already know two of them well, до́лжен and ну́жен, from [Can, must, need, may](#s-04-08).

Short forms can do exactly one thing: sit in the predicate and say what the subject *is*. Я гото́в — "I am ready." They never stand before a noun, and so they never need cases; they exist only in the nominative.

For most adjectives the short form is optional and sounds literary. But for a few dozen everyday words it is the normal form, or the only one. Those are what this section is really about — along with the neuter short form in -о, which turns out to be the most overworked word type in the language.

:::vocab
рад | glad | no long form
гото́в | ready | к + dat.; + infinitive
за́нят / свобо́ден | busy / free |
прав | right, correct (of a person) |
винова́т | guilty, at fault | в + prep.
согла́сен | in agreement | с + instr.
уве́рен | sure, certain | в + prep.
дово́лен | pleased, satisfied | + instr.
бо́лен / здоро́в | ill / well |
го́лоден / сыт | hungry / full |
знако́м | acquainted, familiar | с + instr.
жена́т | married (of a man) | на + prep.
вели́к / мал | too big / too small |
пора́ | it's time (to) |
жаль | it's a pity; sorry for |
:::

## Forms

Take the long form, remove the ending, and add: nothing for masculine, **-о** for neuter, **-а** for feminine, **-ы** (or **-и** after г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ) for plural.

| Long form | m. | n. | f. | pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| краси́вый | краси́в | краси́во | краси́ва | краси́вы |
| гото́вый | гото́в | гото́во | гото́ва | гото́вы |
| хоро́ший | хоро́ш | хорошо́ | хороша́ | хороши́ |
| ну́жный | ну́ж**е**н | ну́жно | нужна́ | нужны́ |
| больно́й | бо́л**е**н | больно́ | больна́ | больны́ |
| лёгкий | лёг**о**к | легко́ | легка́ | легки́ |
| у́мный | ум**ё**н | умно́ | умна́ | умны́ |

Two complications, both visible in the table.

**The fleeting vowel.** If chopping off the ending leaves two consonants at the end of the masculine, a vowel is usually slipped in: **е** before н (ну́жен, бо́лен, свобо́ден, согла́сен, го́лоден), **о** before к (лёгок, бли́зок, ни́зок). It appears only in the masculine; the other forms have an ending to lean on.

**Moving stress.** Short forms are the most stress-restless corner of Russian. There are three main patterns:

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| fixed on the stem | гото́в, гото́во, гото́ва, гото́вы · краси́в, краси́ва · похо́ж, похо́жа |
| ending-stressed in the feminine only | прав, пра́во, **права́**, пра́вы · жив, жи́во, **жива́**, жи́вы · за́нят, за́нято, **занята́**, за́няты |
| ending-stressed wherever there is an ending | хоро́ш, **хорошо́, хороша́, хороши́** · умён, **умно́, умна́, умны́** · бо́лен, **больна́, больны́** |

The second pattern — everything on the stem except a feminine that jumps to the end — is the most typical. Learn each common short adjective with its feminine and you'll rarely go wrong; the rest are in [Stress patterns](#s-09-07).

Not every adjective has short forms. Those that describe what something is made of or related to (ру́сский, деревя́нный, городско́й) don't; neither does большо́й or ма́ленький — they borrow **вели́к** and **мал** from other words. And **рад** has no long form at all.

## The ones you can't do without

Here are the short adjectives that modern spoken Russian uses all the time. With these, the long form is either impossible in the predicate or means something else.

| m. | f. | pl. | Meaning | Takes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| рад | ра́да | ра́ды | glad | + dat.; + inf.; что… |
| гото́в | гото́ва | гото́вы | ready | к + dat.; + inf. |
| до́лжен | должна́ | должны́ | must; owes | + inf.; + dat. |
| ну́жен | нужна́ | нужны́ | needed | + dat. of the person |
| согла́сен | согла́сна | согла́сны | agrees | с + instr. |
| уве́рен | уве́рена | уве́рены | sure | в + prep.; что… |
| за́нят | занята́ | за́няты | busy | + instr. |
| свобо́ден | свобо́дна | свобо́дны | free | |
| прав | права́ | пра́вы | right | |
| винова́т | винова́та | винова́ты | to blame | в + prep. |
| бо́лен | больна́ | больны́ | ill | + instr. |
| здоро́в | здоро́ва | здоро́вы | well, healthy | |
| похо́ж | похо́жа | похо́жи | similar | на + acc. |
| знако́м | знако́ма | знако́мы | acquainted | с + instr. |
| жена́т | — | жена́ты | married | на + prep. |
| жив | жива́ | жи́вы | alive | |
| дово́лен | дово́льна | дово́льны | pleased | + instr. |
| благода́рен | благода́рна | благода́рны | grateful | + dat.; за + acc. |
| спосо́бен | спосо́бна | спосо́бны | capable | на + acc.; + inf. |
| изве́стен | изве́стна | изве́стны | well known | + instr. (for); + dat. (to) |
| сча́стлив | сча́стлива | сча́стливы | happy | |
| го́лоден | голодна́ | го́лодны | hungry | |
| сыт | сыта́ | сы́ты | full (not hungry) | |
| пьян | пьяна́ | пья́ны | drunk | |

(A woman isn't жена́та; she is **за́мужем** — literally "behind a husband", an adverb: Она́ за́мужем за Ди́мой. The plural жена́ты covers a couple: Они́ жена́ты уже́ де́сять лет.)

:::ex
Я о́чень **рад** тебя́ ви́деть. | I'm very glad to see you.
Ты **гото́ва**? Такси́ уже́ внизу́. | Are you ready? The taxi's downstairs already.
Извини́, я сейча́с **за́нят**. | Sorry, I'm busy right now.
Ты **свобо́дна** в суббо́ту ве́чером? | Are you free Saturday evening?
Ты **прав**, а я был **непра́в**. | You're right, and I was wrong.
Кто **винова́т**? | Who's to blame?
Вы **уве́рены**, что э́то здесь? | Are you sure it's here?
Мы с ним давно́ **знако́мы**. | He and I have known each other a long time.
Ди́ма **жена́т** на мое́й сестре́. | Dima is married to my sister.
Спаси́бо, я **сыт**. | Thanks, I'm full.
Все **жи́вы** и **здоро́вы**. | Everyone's alive and well.
Бу́дьте **здоро́вы**! | Bless you! | said after a sneeze; literally "be healthy"
:::

### They bring their own grammar

Many of these demand a particular case, preposition or infinitive after them — one more reason they prefer the short form, since a long adjective with baggage attached is awkward in the predicate.

:::ex
Я **гото́в к** экза́мену. | I'm ready for the exam.
Я **гото́в** нача́ть хоть за́втра. | I'm ready to start tomorrow if need be.
Я с тобо́й по́лностью **согла́сен**. | I completely agree with you.
Она́ **дово́льна** свое́й но́вой рабо́той. | She's pleased with her new job.
Я **уве́рен в** э́том. | I'm sure of it.
Он **бо́лен** гри́ппом. | He's sick with the flu.
Я вам о́чень **благода́рен за** по́мощь. | I'm very grateful to you for your help.
Каза́нь **изве́стна** свои́м кремлём. | Kazan is known for its kremlin.
Он **спосо́бен на** всё. | He's capable of anything.
Сын о́чень **похо́ж на** отца́. | The son looks a lot like his father.
Я тебе́ **до́лжен** пятьсо́т рубле́й. | I owe you five hundred rubles.
:::

In the past and future, add the right form of быть; the short adjective goes on agreeing with the subject.

:::ex
Вчера́ я **был за́нят** весь день. | Yesterday I was busy all day.
Она́ **была́ больна́** це́лую неде́лю. | She was ill for a whole week.
Мы **бу́дем ра́ды** вас ви́деть. | We'll be glad to see you.
Ты **был прав**. | You were right.
:::

## Short versus long: what's the difference?

When an adjective has both forms available in the predicate, how do they differ? There are three things going on, and they pull in different directions.

**1. State versus characteristic.** For a number of adjectives the short form describes a temporary condition, the long form a lasting quality — what the person is like in general.

:::ex
Он **бо́лен**. | He's ill. | right now; he has a cold
Он **больно́й**. | He's a sick man. | chronically — or, colloquially, "he's nuts"
Она́ сего́дня **занята́**. | She's busy today.
Она́ о́чень **занято́й** челове́к. | She's a very busy person.
Он **жив**! | He's alive!
Он о́чень **живо́й**. | He's very lively.
:::

Sometimes the meanings have split completely: **прав** is "right, correct", while **пра́вый** is "right-hand" or "right-wing". **Хоро́ш** tends to mean "good-looking" or, ironically, "a fine one": Ты то́же **хоро́ш**! — "You're a fine one too!"

**2. Style.** For ordinary descriptive adjectives — у́мный, краси́вый, интере́сный, до́брый — the long form is what people say, and the short form belongs to books, speeches and aphorisms. It sounds more categorical, like a verdict.

:::ex
Она́ о́чень **краси́вая**. | She's very beautiful. | normal
Она́ о́чень **краси́ва**. | She is very beautiful. | literary
Жизнь **прекра́сна**. | Life is beautiful. | a pronouncement
Он **умён** и **тала́нтлив**. | He is intelligent and talented. | written style
:::

So don't over-use short forms. Outside the essential list above, default to the long form in conversation.

**3. "Too…".** With adjectives of size and age, the short form means the thing is *too* big, small, long or young *for someone or something*. This is where вели́к and мал earn their keep; the person affected goes in the dative.

:::ex
Э́ти ту́фли мне **малы́**. | These shoes are too small for me.
Ку́ртка тебе́ **велика́**. | The jacket is too big for you.
Брю́ки мне **длинны́**. | The pants are too long for me.
Он ещё **мо́лод** для тако́й рабо́ты. | He's still too young for a job like that.
:::

Compare Ку́ртка **больша́я** — "The jacket is big" (a fact about the jacket) with Ку́ртка **велика́** — "The jacket is too big" (it doesn't fit).

## With polite вы

When you address one person as вы, verbs and short adjectives go in the **plural**, but long adjectives stay **singular**, in the person's real gender.

:::ex
Вы **пра́вы**. | You're right. | to one person, politely
Вы **гото́вы**? | Are you ready?
Бу́дьте **добры́**, переда́йте соль. | Would you be so kind as to pass the salt.
Вы **така́я краси́вая**! | You're so beautiful! | to one woman
Вы о́чень **до́брый**. | You're very kind. | to one man
:::

## The neuter form in -о: one word, three jobs

The neuter short form — **хорошо́**, **хо́лодно**, **интере́сно** — is the same word as the adverb you learned back in [Adverbs](#s-02-08). That's no coincidence, and it's worth seeing all its jobs together.

**Job 1: predicate of a neuter subject** — typically э́то or всё.

:::ex
Э́то **интере́сно**. | That's interesting.
Всё **гото́во**. | Everything's ready.
Э́то **пра́вильно**. | That's correct.
Всё **я́сно**. | Everything's clear.
:::

**Job 2: adverb** — describing how something is done.

:::ex
Он **интере́сно** расска́зывает. | He tells stories in an interesting way.
Она́ **хорошо́** гото́вит. | She cooks well.
:::

**Job 3: predicate with no subject at all** — describing a situation, the surroundings, or how someone feels (person in the dative).

:::ex
Здесь **хорошо́**. | It's nice here.
На у́лице **хо́лодно**. | It's cold outside.
Мне **интере́сно**, что он ска́жет. | I'm curious what he'll say.
С тобо́й **ве́село**. | It's fun with you.
:::

There's no "it" in these sentences and nothing for the -о form to agree with. It is the whole predicate by itself. This subjectless pattern is so important in Russian that it gets [the next section](#s-06-09) to itself.

### Predicate words that aren't adjectives at all

Alongside the -о words in Job 3, Russian has a small set of words that work the same way — they're predicates without a subject — but don't come from any adjective. You know most of them: **мо́жно**, **нельзя́**, **на́до**, **ну́жно**. Add these:

:::ex
**Пора́** идти́. | It's time to go.
Мне **пора́**. | I have to be going.
**Жаль**, что ты не пришёл. | It's a pity you didn't come.
Мне его́ **жаль**. | I feel sorry for him.
Мне **лень** гото́вить. | I can't be bothered to cook. | лень = laziness
Отсю́да **ви́дно** мо́ре. | You can see the sea from here.
Тебя́ пло́хо **слы́шно**. | I can't hear you well. | literally "you (acc.) is badly audible"
:::

Russian grammars put all of these, together with the Job 3 words, in a class of their own, sometimes called the "category of state". The name doesn't matter. What matters is the shared behavior: no nominative subject, the person in the dative, past with бы́ло, future with бу́дет — Мне **бы́ло** лень; **Бу́дет** хо́лодно.

## Тако́й or так? Како́й or как?

English "so" and "how" each cover two Russian words, and the choice depends on what kind of word follows.

**Тако́й** and **како́й** are adjectives. They go with **long adjectives** and nouns, and agree with them.

**Так** and **как** are adverbs. They go with **short adjectives**, **adverbs** and **verbs**.

:::ex
Он **тако́й у́мный**! | He's so smart!
Она́ **така́я до́брая**. | She's so kind.
Сего́дня **тако́й хоро́ший** день. | It's such a nice day today.
Я **так рад** тебя́ ви́деть! | I'm so glad to see you!
Здесь **так краси́во**! | It's so beautiful here!
Ты **так хорошо́** говори́шь по-ру́сски. | You speak Russian so well.
Я **так уста́л**. | I'm so tired.
:::

Mixing them — [[ns:так умный]], [[ns:такой рад]] — is a very common learner error. Check the next word: long form → тако́й; anything else → так.

Exclamations with "how" and "what a" follow the same split:

:::ex
**Како́й** он **у́мный**! | How smart he is!
**Кака́я краси́вая** му́зыка! | What beautiful music!
**Како́й** у́жас! | How awful! | literally "what horror"
**Как** он **умён**! | How clever he is! | literary
**Как краси́во**! | How beautiful!
**Как** я **рад**! | How glad I am!
**Как** бы́стро лети́т вре́мя! | How time flies!
:::

:::summary
- Short forms: predicate only, no cases; m. zero ending (often with a fleeting е / о), n. -о, f. -а, pl. -ы / -и. Watch the feminine stress.
- Essential in speech: рад, гото́в, до́лжен, ну́жен, согла́сен, уве́рен, за́нят, свобо́ден, прав, винова́т, бо́лен, здоро́в, похо́ж, знако́м, жена́т, жив, дово́лен, благода́рен, го́лоден, сыт.
- Otherwise: short = temporary state, bookish verdict, or "too…" (вели́к, мал).
- The -о form is predicate of э́то / всё, adverb, and subjectless predicate.
- тако́й / како́й + long adjective; так / как + short adjective, adverb, verb.
:::
