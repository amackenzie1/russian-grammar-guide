# Stress and vowel reduction

Every Russian word of more than one syllable has one syllable that dominates: it is louder, noticeably longer, and its vowel is pronounced clearly. That is the **stressed** syllable. All the other syllables are short and weak, and their vowels get blurred. This blurring, called **vowel reduction**, is the reason хорошо́ (good, fine) doesn't sound like *kho-ro-SHO*, and it is the biggest difference between Russian as written and Russian as spoken.

English works the same way, which is good news. In "banana" only the middle *a* is a real *a*; the other two are a neutral "uh". In "photograph" and "photography" the stress moves and the vowels change with it. You already do all this without thinking. You only need to learn where Russian does it.

The bad news is that Russian stress is unpredictable, it moves around within the forms of a single word, and it is not written.

## Stress can fall anywhere

Some languages fix the stress in one place: always the first syllable in Czech, usually the second-to-last in Polish. Russian has no such rule. Stress can land on any syllable, and you can't tell which from the spelling.

:::ex
ма́ма | mom | first syllable
соба́ка | dog | middle
молоко́ | milk | last
ко́мната | room | first of three
учи́тельница | teacher (f.) | second of five
:::

Borrowed words are no safer. Russian took them with its own stress, often from French or German, so your English instincts are frequently wrong:

:::ex
телефо́н | phone
президе́нт | president
компью́тер | computer
шокола́д | chocolate
Аме́рика | America
Вашингто́н | Washington
Флори́да | Florida
:::

The same goes for names. It is Влади́мир, Бори́с, Ива́н, Набо́ков and Шара́пова, whatever sports commentators say.

## Stress can change the word

Because stress is part of a word's identity, two words can be spelled identically and differ only in stress.

:::ex
за́мок — замо́к | castle — lock
мука́ — му́ка | flour — torment
писа́ть — пи́сать | to write — to pee
уже́ — у́же | already — narrower
до́ма — дома́ | at home — houses
сто́ит — стои́т | costs; is worth — is standing
пла́чу — плачу́ | I'm crying — I'm paying
ду́хи — духи́ | spirits, ghosts — perfume
:::

The third pair is the one every teacher warns about. "I like to write" with the wrong stress is a sentence you'll remember getting wrong.

The last few pairs show something further. Stress doesn't only separate unrelated words; it separates *forms of the same word*. Дом is "house"; до́ма means "at home" and дома́ means "houses". In an ordinary Russian text all of these are printed without any marks, so [[ns: Я плачу]] could be "I'm crying" or "I'm paying". Russians tell them apart from context, effortlessly. You'll get there too, but for a long while you will need the marks.

## Stress moves

In many words the stress stays on the same syllable in every form. In many of the most common words it doesn't.

:::ex
рука́ → ру́ки | hand → hands
окно́ → о́кна | window → windows
го́род → города́ | city → cities
письмо́ → пи́сьма | letter → letters
сестра́ → сёстры | sister → sisters
пишу́ → пи́шешь | I write → you write
люблю́ → лю́бишь | I love → you love
был, была́, бы́ли | was (m.), was (f.), were
:::

This is not random; there are a limited number of patterns, and words fall into groups. The individual sections will point out stress shifts as they come up, and the patterns are collected in [Stress patterns](#s-09-07). For now, just know that learning the stress of the dictionary form of a word is necessary but not always sufficient.

## What happens to unstressed vowels

Russian has five vowel sounds under stress. Away from the stress, they collapse into fewer. Three of them, the sounds written у/ю, ы and и, stay recognizably themselves, only shorter. The others change.

From here on, the Latin spellings in this guide show the actual pronunciation, reduction included. In them, *uh* stands for the neutral vowel at the end of English "sofa".

### Unstressed о and а

Unstressed о is never pronounced *o*. It merges with а. How it comes out depends on where it stands relative to the stress:

- **In the syllable right before the stress**, and at the very start of a word, both о and а are a short, clear *a*.
- **Everywhere else** (two or more syllables before the stress, or anywhere after it), both become the weak *uh*.

:::ex
она́ | she | *a-NA*
окно́ | window | *ak-NO*
Москва́ | Moscow | *mask-VA*
оди́н | one | *a-DEEN*
спаси́бо | thank you | *spa-SEE-buh*
э́то | this | *E-tuh*
ма́ло | little, not much | *MA-luh*
рабо́та | work, job | *ra-BO-tuh*
пого́да | weather | *pa-GO-duh*
молоко́ | milk | *muh-la-KO*
хорошо́ | good, fine | *khuh-ra-SHO*
голова́ | head | *guh-la-VA*
магази́н | store | *muh-ga-ZEEN*
ко́мната | room | *KOM-nuh-tuh*
говори́ть | to speak | *guh-va-REET'*
:::

Look at молоко́: three letters о, three different sounds. The last one, under stress, is a real *o*. The one just before the stress is *a*. The first is *uh*. Хорошо́ follows the same pattern. Once you have the rhythm of *uh-a-STRESS* in your ear, a large part of Russian pronunciation falls into place.

The spelling never changes to reflect this. That has two consequences for you. Going from writing to speech, you must know the stress to pronounce a word correctly. Going from speech to writing, you can't tell whether an unstressed *a* or *uh* is spelled о or а; you have to know the word. Russian schoolchildren spend years on exactly this, and the trick they are taught is worth knowing: find a form of the word where the stress falls on the doubtful vowel. Вода́ (water) is *va-DA*, but its plural во́ды is *VO-dy*, so the letter is о.

### Unstressed е and я

After a soft consonant, unstressed е and я are both pronounced as a short, lax *i*, close to the vowel of English "bit". So is unstressed а after ч and щ. Written here as *ee* for want of anything better, but keep it short.

:::ex
сестра́ | sister | *sees-TRA*
река́ | river | *ree-KA*
метро́ | subway | *mee-TRO*
меня́ | me | *mee-NYA*
телефо́н | phone | *tee-lee-FON*
де́сять | ten | *DYE-seet'*
ме́сяц | month | *MYE-seets*
пяти́ | of five | *pee-TEE*
часы́ | clock, watch | *chee-SY*
:::

At the start of a word, where these letters include a *y* sound, the same thing happens to the vowel part:

:::ex
язы́к | language | *yee-ZYK*
янва́рь | January | *yeen-VAR'*
ещё | still, more | *yee-SHCHO*
Еле́на | Elena | *yee-LYE-nuh*
еда́ | food | *yee-DA*
:::

So пять (five) has a clear *a*, but пяти́ sounds like *pee-TEE*. Hearing *ee* in an unstressed syllable, you can't know whether to write и, е or я. Again, the spelling has to be learned with the word.

At the very end of a word, in grammatical endings, unstressed я and е relax a little more toward a weak *yuh*: неде́ля (week) is roughly *nee-DYE-lyuh*, и́мя (name) is *EE-myuh*. Don't fuss over this; if you keep final unstressed vowels short and quiet, they'll come out right.

### After ж, ш and ц

These three consonants are always hard, so the vowel after them can't be the *i* of "bit". Unstressed е after them sounds like a short ы instead:

:::ex
жена́ | wife | *zhy-NA*
цена́ | price | *tsy-NA*
шепта́ть | to whisper | *shyp-TAT'*
:::

In a handful of words, unstressed а after ж or ш does the same in careful standard speech: к сожале́нию (unfortunately) is *k suh-zhy-LYE-nee-yu*. You'll pick these up by ear.

### A summary table

| Written | Under stress | Right before the stress | Elsewhere |
|---|---|---|---|
| а | *a* | *a* | *uh* |
| о | *o* | *a* | *uh* |
| е (after a soft consonant) | *e* | short *i* | short *i* |
| я (after a soft consonant) | *a* | short *i* | short *i*, weak *yuh* at the end of a word |
| е (after ж, ш, ц) | *e* | short ы | short ы |
| и, ы, у, ю | themselves | themselves, shorter | themselves, shorter |

:::note Don't overdo it
Reduction is the natural result of putting all your energy into one syllable. If you hit the stressed syllable hard and long and hurry through the rest, the vowels will reduce on their own. Learners who carefully pronounce every о as *o* sound stilted; learners who stress the right syllable strongly sound good even when the details are off.
:::

:::aside Places where о stays о
A few borrowed words keep an unreduced о in careful speech, mostly at the end of the word: ра́дио (radio), кака́о (cocoa). And some regions of northern Russia traditionally pronounce every о as *o*, a feature Russians call о́канье and find charmingly rustic. The standard language, based on Moscow speech, reduces.
:::

## Ё is always stressed

The letter ё only occurs under stress. This is why it never carries a stress mark in this guide: the two dots already tell you. It also means that when stress moves away, ё can't survive, and е appears in its place:

:::ex
сёстры → сестра́ | sisters → sister
жёны → жена́ | wives → wife
звёзды → звезда́ | stars → star
:::

## Little words that lean on their neighbors

Short function words, above all prepositions and the negative не, normally have no stress of their own. They are pronounced as part of the following word, and their vowels reduce accordingly: не зна́ю (I don't know) is one unit, *nee-ZNA-yu*; до свида́ния (goodbye) is *duh-svee-DA-nee-ya*.

In a small number of fixed combinations the little word steals the stress from its neighbor, which is then left with none: [[ns: не́ был]] (wasn't, *NYE-byl*), [[ns: за́ город]] (out of town, *ZA-guh-rut*). These are learned as set phrases.

## How this guide marks stress

Every Russian word of two or more syllables in this guide carries an acute accent over its stressed vowel: молоко́, соба́ка, говори́ть. Two kinds of words go unmarked: one-syllable words (дом, я, нет), since there is nothing to choose, and words with ё, since ё is always the stressed vowel.

Remember that these marks are a learner's aid. They are not part of Russian spelling, and you shouldn't write them in your own messages. Where you do and don't see them in the real world is covered in [the last section of this part](#s-01-06).

## How to learn stress

There is no shortcut that replaces knowing the word, but some habits make it much easier.

**Learn the stress as part of the word, out loud.** A Russian word without its stress is half-learned. When you meet a new word, say it aloud several times with an exaggerated stressed syllable. Your memory for how a word sounds is far better than your memory for where a little mark sat on the page.

**Listen a lot.** Stress is stored as rhythm. The more Russian you hear, the more often the wrong stress will simply sound wrong, the way "ba-NA-na" said as "BA-na-na" does.

**Use the patterns that exist.** Stress is unpredictable for bare roots, but many endings and suffixes behave consistently. A few reliable ones:

- Words with ё are stressed on the ё.
- Adjectives whose dictionary form ends in -ой are stressed on that ending: большо́й (big), молодо́й (young), плохо́й (bad). Those in -ый and -ий are stressed somewhere on the stem: но́вый (new), ма́ленький (small).
- Nouns in -а́ция are stressed on the а: информа́ция, ситуа́ция.
- Nouns in -и́ст and -и́зм are stressed on the suffix: тури́ст, журнали́ст, тури́зм.
- Verbs that mean a completed action and start with the prefix вы- are stressed on it: вы́пить (to drink up), вы́учить (to learn by heart).

You'll collect more of these as you go.

**Look it up when unsure.** Dictionaries mark stress, and good ones show it for every form of the word. Guessing and repeating a wrong guess twenty times is how mistakes become permanent.

**Don't panic about mistakes.** A wrong stress usually doesn't prevent understanding; it makes the listener work a little harder. Russians get stress wrong too, and argue about it with enthusiasm. The standard form is звони́т (he/she is calling), but a huge number of people say зво́нит, and others wince when they hear it. Тво́рог and творо́г (a kind of fresh curd cheese) are both officially accepted, and people still argue. You are in good company.
