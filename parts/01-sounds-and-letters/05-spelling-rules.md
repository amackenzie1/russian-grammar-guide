# Spelling rules

Russian has three short spelling rules. Each says: after certain consonants, don't write this vowel letter, write that one instead. They matter because Russian grammar works by attaching endings, and the endings begin with vowels. Whenever the "expected" ending would break one of these rules, the spelling changes. Without the rules, you see exceptions scattered through every table of noun, adjective and verb endings. With them, most of those exceptions disappear.

The rules involve two groups of consonants you met in [Hard and soft consonants](#s-01-02):

- **г, к, х**, the three consonants made at the back of the mouth;
- **ж, ч, ш, щ**, the "hushing" sounds, plus **ц**. These are the consonants that have no hard/soft partner.

The section ends with a few writing habits that differ from English: capital letters and punctuation.

## Rule 1: after г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ write и, never ы

This is the rule you'll need first and most often. Seven consonants are never followed by the letter ы. Where grammar calls for ы, you write и.

Take the plural of nouns. Normally the ending is -ы after a hard consonant:

:::ex
ла́мпа → ла́мпы | lamp → lamps
стол → столы́ | table → tables
:::

But after the seven consonants:

:::ex
кни́га → кни́ги | book → books
уро́к → уро́ки | lesson → lessons
му́ха → му́хи | fly → flies
нож → ножи́ | knife → knives
врач → врачи́ | doctor → doctors
каранда́ш → карандаши́ | pencil → pencils
това́рищ → това́рищи | comrade → comrades
:::

The same happens in adjectives. The ordinary dictionary form ends in -ый, and the plural in -ые: но́вый, но́вые (new). After the seven consonants, these become -ий and -ие:

:::ex
ру́сский, ру́сские | Russian
ма́ленький, ма́ленькие | small
ти́хий, ти́хие | quiet
хоро́ший, хоро́шие | good
горя́чий, горя́чие | hot
:::

Why these seven? Different reasons give the same result. For г, к and х, it is a matter of sound: in native Russian words these consonants are always soft before an *i*-type vowel, so you do hear и. For ж and ш, it is pure convention: they are always hard and the vowel you hear is ы (as in жить, *zhyt'*), but the spelling insists on и. Ч and щ are always soft, so и is the natural choice.

:::note What about ц?
Ц is the one consonant where both letters occur. In grammatical endings, write ы: отцы́ (fathers), у́лицы (streets), огурцы́ (cucumbers). Inside roots, it is usually и: цирк (circus), ци́фра (digit), цита́та (quotation). Either way the pronunciation is ы, because ц is always hard.
:::

## Rule 2: after г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ, ц write а and у, never я and ю

The same seven consonants plus ц are not followed by я or ю. Write а and у instead.

The rule shows most in verbs. One large group of verbs has the ending -ю for "I" and -ят for "they":

:::ex
говорю́, говоря́т | I speak, they speak
смотрю́, смо́трят | I watch, they watch
:::

When the stem ends in a hushing consonant, the same endings are spelled -у and -ат:

:::ex
слы́шу, слы́шат | I hear, they hear
учу́, у́чат | I learn, they learn
лежу́, лежа́т | I'm lying down, they're lying down
:::

Nouns show it too. In the hard-and-soft section слова́рь (dictionary) became словаря́ and словарю́, with я and ю to show the soft consonant. The ч of врач (doctor) is just as soft, but the forms are врача́ and врачу́, because ч can't be followed by я or ю. Similarly, А́ня as a direct object is А́ню, but Са́ша is Са́шу.

:::aside Foreign words break this rule
You'll occasionally see ю after these consonants in borrowed words and names: жюри́ (jury), парашю́т (parachute), брошю́ра (brochure), маникю́р (manicure), Цю́рих (Zurich). They are a small, fixed set and have no effect on grammar.
:::

## Rule 3: after ж, ч, ш, щ, ц, an unstressed о in an ending becomes е

This one depends on stress. After the hushing consonants and ц, endings that normally contain о keep it only when the ending is stressed. When the ending is unstressed, write е.

The ending for "with" or "by means of" (the instrumental case, [coming in Part 3](#s-03-06)) is normally -ом or -ой:

:::ex
с бра́том | with (my) brother | the regular ending
с отцо́м | with (my) father | after ц, stressed: о stays
с врачо́м | with the doctor | after ч, stressed: о stays
с му́жем | with (my) husband | after ж, unstressed: е
с това́рищем | with a comrade | after щ, unstressed: е
с Ди́мой | with Dima | the regular ending
с Са́шей | with Sasha | after ш, unstressed: е
:::

In adjectives, compare the neuter forms of "new", "big" and "good":

:::ex
но́вое | new (n.) | the regular ending
большо́е | big (n.) | after ш, stressed: о stays
хоро́шее | good (n.) | after ш, unstressed: е
:::

Большо́й and хоро́ший are two of the commonest adjectives in the language and the standard examples of this rule in later tables: большо́го but хоро́шего, большо́му but хоро́шему.

The reason is vowel reduction. An unstressed о after these consonants wouldn't sound like *o* anyway, so the spelling settles on е; under stress, the *o* is clearly heard and is written.

:::warn This rule is about endings
Inside roots, the spelling of the stressed *o* sound after hushing consonants is a famous mess. It is usually ё: жёлтый (yellow), чёрный (black), шёл (was walking), ещё (still), счёт (bill). But sometimes it is о: шо́рох (rustle), шов (seam), крыжо́вник (gooseberry). Learn each word as you meet it.
:::

## The three rules together

| After | Write | Not |
|---|---|---|
| г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ | и | ы |
| г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ, ц | а, у | я, ю |
| ж, ч, ш, щ, ц | е in an unstressed ending (о only under stress) | unstressed о |

If you memorize one thing, make it the list **г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ** and the first rule. When a later section says "the ending is -ы, or -и by the spelling rule", this is what it means.

## One more habit: ь after hushing consonants

A soft sign after ж, ч, ш or щ has no effect on pronunciation. Russian uses it there as a grammatical label. Two uses:

- Feminine nouns ending in a hushing consonant take ь; masculine ones don't. Ночь (night), дочь (daughter), мышь (mouse), вещь (thing) and по́мощь (help) are feminine. Врач, нож, душ (shower), борщ and това́рищ are masculine.
- The verb form that goes with ты always ends in -шь: зна́ешь (you know), говори́шь (you speak), идёшь (you're going).

## Capital letters

Russian uses capitals more sparingly than English. The first word of a sentence and proper names are capitalized. Almost everything else is lower-case, including several things English capitalizes.

**Days and months** are lower-case:

:::vocab
понеде́льник | Monday |
вто́рник | Tuesday |
среда́ | Wednesday |
четве́рг | Thursday |
пя́тница | Friday |
суббо́та | Saturday |
воскресе́нье | Sunday |
:::

The months are янва́рь, февра́ль, март, апре́ль, май, ию́нь, ию́ль, а́вгуст, сентя́брь, октя́брь, ноя́брь, дека́брь.

**Nationalities and languages** are lower-case, both as nouns and as adjectives: ру́сский (Russian), америка́нец (an American man), англича́нка (an Englishwoman), англи́йский язы́к (the English language). Names of countries and cities are capitalized, but adjectives made from them are not: Москва́, but моско́вский (Moscow, as an adjective).

**The word я** (I) is lower-case unless it starts the sentence.

:::ex
В понеде́льник я рабо́таю. | I'm working on Monday.
У меня́ день рожде́ния в ма́рте. | My birthday is in March.
Она́ америка́нка, но хорошо́ говори́т по-ру́сски. | She's American, but she speaks Russian well.
А́ня у́чит францу́зский. | Anya is learning French.
:::

**In titles and multi-word names**, as a rule only the first word gets a capital, plus any proper names inside:

:::ex
«Война́ и мир» | War and Peace
«Ма́стер и Маргари́та» | The Master and Margarita
«Звёздные во́йны» | Star Wars
Большо́й теа́тр | the Bolshoi Theater
Кра́сная пло́щадь | Red Square
Но́вый год | New Year
:::

**Вы with a capital.** When you write to one person formally, in a letter, an email or an official document, the polite "you" and its forms are traditionally capitalized as a sign of respect: Вы, Вас, Вам, Ваш. When вы means several people, it is never capitalized, and ты never is either.

:::ex
Ива́н Петро́вич, когда́ Вы бу́дете в Москве́? | Ivan Petrovich, when will you be in Moscow?
:::

In casual messages many people skip this; in a job application or a letter to a professor, use it.

## Punctuation

Most punctuation marks work as in English. A few habits differ, and you'll see them in every example from here on.

### Commas follow grammar

English commas are partly a matter of rhythm and taste. Russian commas are placed by rule, and the main rule is that **clauses are always separated by a comma**. Before words like что (that), кото́рый (which, who), потому́ что (because), е́сли (if), когда́ (when), где (where) introducing a clause, the comma is obligatory, even where English would never write one.

:::ex
Я зна́ю, что он до́ма. | I know he's home.
Я не по́мню, где ключи́. | I don't remember where the keys are.
Напиши́, когда́ прие́дешь. | Text me when you get there.
Е́сли хо́чешь, пойдём вме́сте. | If you want, let's go together.
Э́то фильм, кото́рый я смотре́л три ра́за. | That's the movie I've seen three times.
:::

Other fixed habits:

- A comma always stands before а and но (both roughly "but"): Он уста́л, но дово́лен. (He's tired but pleased.)
- There is no comma before и in a simple list: Купи́ хлеб, молоко́ и сыр. (Buy bread, milk and cheese.)
- The name of the person you're addressing is set off by commas: Приве́т, Ма́ша! (Hi, Masha!) Са́ша, ты где? (Sasha, where are you?)
- Sentence-level comment words like коне́чно (of course) are set off too: Коне́чно, приду́. (Of course I'll come.)
- There is no comma after an opening time or place phrase. English often writes "Yesterday, I was home"; Russian writes Вчера́ я был до́ма.

### The dash that stands for "is"

Russian normally leaves out "is" and "are" in the present tense, as the next section of this guide explains. When a sentence says that one noun is another noun, a long dash is written where the missing verb would be:

:::ex
Москва́ — столи́ца Росси́и. | Moscow is the capital of Russia.
Мой брат — врач. | My brother is a doctor.
Вре́мя — де́ньги. | Time is money.
:::

When the subject is a pronoun, the dash is normally left out: Он врач. (He's a doctor.) Я студе́нт. (I'm a student.)

### Dashes for dialogue, angle quotes for quotations

In books, lines of dialogue are not put in quotation marks. Each speaker's line starts on a new line with a dash:

:::ex
— Ты где? | "Where are you?"
— До́ма. А что? | "At home. Why?"
:::

Russian quotation marks are angled, «like this», and are nicknamed ёлочки, "little fir trees". They are used for quoted speech inside a paragraph, and for titles and names of all kinds, where English might use italics or nothing: фильм «Брат» (the movie *Brother*), рестора́н «Пу́шкин» (the restaurant Pushkin), ста́нция «Арба́тская» (Arbatskaya station). A period or comma goes outside the closing mark:

:::ex
Она́ сказа́ла: «Я не приду́». | She said, "I'm not coming."
:::

In chat and informal typing, people usually use straight "quotes".

### Numbers and dates

- The decimal separator is a comma: 3,5 is "three and a half".
- Thousands are separated by a space: 10 000.
- Dates are written day first: 15.11.2026 is the fifteenth of November.
- The sign № means "number": дом № 12 (building no. 12).

:::aside The Russian smiley
In messages, Russians very often end a line with a closing parenthesis and no colon: Приве́т) That is a smile. More parentheses mean a bigger smile: Спаси́бо))) An opening parenthesis is a frown. A message with no parenthesis at all isn't rude, but a period at the end of a short chat message can read as cold.
:::
