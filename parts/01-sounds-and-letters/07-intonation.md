# Intonation: the melody that makes a question

English turns a statement into a question by rearranging it: "This is Masha" becomes "Is this Masha?", or a "do" appears from nowhere. Russian changes nothing. The words stay the same, in the same order, and in speech the only thing that separates a statement from a yes/no question is the tune.

:::ex
Э́то Ма́ша. | This is Masha.
Э́то Ма́ша? | Is this Masha?
:::

So intonation in Russian is not decoration. It does a job that English gives to grammar, and if you get the tune wrong your question will be heard as a statement, or the other way round. There are only a few tunes, and one of them does most of the work.

This guide has no audio, so this section tells you what to listen for. Some of the example sentences use grammar from later parts; here only their tune matters.

## One syllable carries the tune

In every Russian phrase one word is the important one, and on the stressed syllable of that word the pitch moves. That syllable is the **center** of the phrase. Before the center the voice stays around its middle level. On the center it does something: falls, jumps, or dips. After the center it settles low or stays high, depending on the tune, and holds that level to the end.

That gives you three things to hear: where the center is, what the pitch does there, and whether the rest of the phrase ends up low or high. In the examples below the center word is in bold.

## The statement: fall and stay down

In a plain statement the pitch drops on the center and stays low to the end. The center is normally the last word, because that is where Russian puts the new information.

:::ex
Э́то **Ма́ша**. | This is Masha.
Она́ **до́ма**. | She's at home.
Я **студе́нт**. | I'm a student.
:::

Russian statements end lower and more firmly than English ones tend to. To an English ear a row of Russian statements can sound curt or even cross. It isn't; that is just what "I've finished my sentence" sounds like. Going the other way, the English habit of letting statements drift upward at the end makes a Russian listener wait for you to finish.

## The yes/no question: jump up, then drop

This is the one to practice. In a yes/no question the pitch leaps sharply upward on the center syllable, and then falls on whatever follows, down to the bottom of your range.

:::ex
Э́то **Ма́ша**? | Is this Masha?
Она́ **до́ма**? | Is she at home?
Вы **студе́нт**? | Are you a student?
:::

Two things here are unlike English.

First, the rise is on the stressed syllable of the center word, not at the end of the sentence. In «Она́ до́ма?» the voice jumps on **до** and has already come back down by **ма**. An English-style question, where the voice keeps climbing to the last syllable, does not sound like a question to a Russian. It sounds unfinished, as if more were coming.

Second, the jump is big and quick. English speakers almost always make it too small. Exaggerate it until it feels theatrical, and it will be about right.

If the center is the very last syllable, there is nothing after it to fall on, so the voice simply shoots up and stops: «Э́то **метро́**?» (Is this the metro?).

### Moving the center changes the question

Because the jump marks the word you are asking about, you can ask different questions with the same words by moving it. English does this too, with emphasis, but in Russian it is the main mechanism.

:::ex
Ты **идёшь** в кино́? | Are you going to the movies? | or aren't you?
Ты идёшь в **кино́**? | Is it the movies you're going to? | or somewhere else?
**Ты** идёшь в кино́? | Is it you who's going to the movies? | or someone else?
:::

The neutral version, when you just want a yes or a no, puts the center on the verb. And the natural short answer repeats the center word rather than saying only да:

:::dialogue
А: Ты **идёшь**? | Are you coming?
Б: Иду́. | I am. | literally "am-going"
:::

### The same jump also means "I'm not finished"

The rise-then-drop tune has a second use. In a longer sentence, the first part often carries it to show that more is coming, and the final part then falls like any statement. You will hear it at every comma.

:::ex
Е́сли за́втра бу́дет **дождь**, мы оста́немся **до́ма**. | If it rains tomorrow, we'll stay home. | rise on дождь, fall on до́ма
:::

It also softens requests, which is why a polite «Скажи́те, пожа́луйста…» (Tell me, please…) rises on its first word.

## Question-word questions: hit the question word

Questions that begin with a word like кто (who), что (what), где (where) or как (how) do not rise at all. The question word already announces the question, so the melody is close to a statement: the center is often the question word itself, said with extra force, and the pitch falls away after it.

:::ex
**Кто** э́то? | Who is this?
**Где** метро́? | Where is the metro?
**Что** э́то? | What is this?
:::

The center can also sit on a later word when that word carries the point, as in «Как **дела́**?» (How are things?). The tune is the same: no rise, a firm fall on the center, low after it.

Ending these on a rise, as English often does to sound friendly, comes across in Russian as surprise or disbelief: "Wait, *where* is the metro?"

The same forceful falling tune is used for calling to someone and for firm instructions: «**Ма́ша**!», «**Сто́йте**!» (Stop!).

## "And you?": dip and climb

The last everyday tune belongs to follow-up questions that begin with **а** (and, what about). The pitch dips on the center and then climbs, and it stays up to the end.

:::dialogue
А: Как дела́? | How are things?
Б: Хорошо́. А **ты**? | Good. And you?
:::

:::ex
А **Ма́ша**? | And Masha? / What about Masha?
А **вы**? | And you? | polite or plural
:::

You will also hear this dip-and-climb in careful, official speech, on each item of a list and before a pause. In casual talk the sharp jump from the previous section does that job instead.

## Exclamations

In exclamations that begin with како́й (what a…) or как (how…), the voice rises on the first stressed word, stays high, and falls on the last.

:::ex
**Како́й** сего́дня **день**! | What a day it is today!
**Как** здесь **краси́во**! | How beautiful it is here!
:::

The same words can be said in other ways to mean other things. «Како́й он врач!», with a sharp, clipped rise on **како́й** and no admiring fall at the end, does not admire the man; it says he is no doctor at all. Shades like this come with exposure, and you only need to know that they exist.

## Intonation and word order

Later you will learn that Russian word order is free, and that speakers use it to put known information first and news last. Intonation is the other half of that system. In speech you can leave the words where they are and move the center instead, and the meaning shifts just as it would in writing with a different order. [Word order](#s-03-09) picks this up again.

## How to practice

- **Listen for the center first.** In any recording, find the one syllable in each phrase where the pitch moves. Once you can hear that, the rest follows.
- **Drill the yes/no jump in pairs.** Say a statement, then the same words as a question: «Э́то чай. Э́то чай?» Make the question's jump bigger than feels natural.
- **Stop rising at the end.** Record yourself asking a few questions. If your voice is still going up on the last syllable of a long word, that is English leaking through.
- **Shadow real speech.** Play a short clip, pause, and copy the melody exactly, even before you understand all the words. Dialogue from films and shows is ideal, because it is full of questions.

:::summary
- Russian yes/no questions use the same words in the same order as statements. Only the melody differs.
- Each phrase has one center: the stressed syllable of its most important word.
- Statement: fall on the center, stay low. Question-word question: force on the question word, then low.
- Yes/no question: a sharp jump up on the center, then a drop. Not a rise at the end.
- Moving the center changes what is being asked.
:::
