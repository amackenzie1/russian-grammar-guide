# Hard and soft consonants

In the last section we left a puzzle hanging: why does Russian have two letters for each vowel, а and я, у and ю, and so on? The answer has almost nothing to do with vowels. It is about consonants.

Nearly every Russian consonant comes in two versions, called **hard** and **soft**. Russian has a hard *n* and a soft *n*, a hard *t* and a soft *t*, a hard *l* and a soft *l*, and to a Russian ear these are as different as *s* and *sh* are to you. Swapping one for the other can change the word. This distinction runs through the whole language: it decides how words are spelled, and later it will decide which set of endings a noun or adjective takes. If you understand this section, a large amount of Russian grammar that looks like arbitrary variation will turn out to be one pattern written in two ways.

## What "soft" means

A hard consonant is the plain one, pronounced more or less as you'd expect.

A soft consonant (the technical word is *palatalized*) is the same consonant made with the middle of the tongue pushed up toward the roof of the mouth, the position your tongue takes for the *y* in "yes". You don't say the consonant and then a *y*. You say them at the same time, as one sound.

English has traces of this. Compare the *n* in "no" with the *n* in "onion" or "canyon": in the second, your tongue is flatter and higher. Compare the *l* in "pull" with the *l* in "million". Compare the *t* in "tool" with the *t* in British "tune". Those second versions are roughly what Russian soft н, л and т sound like. The difference is that in English it happens by accident next to certain sounds, while in Russian it is deliberate and can occur anywhere, including at the very end of a word.

Fifteen consonants come in such pairs: б, в, г, д, з, к, л, м, н, п, р, с, т, ф, х. The other six consonant letters don't pair up, and we'll deal with them below.

## How softness is written

Russian could have invented fifteen extra consonant letters. Instead it marks softness on the *next* letter. There are two ways.

### Way one: the vowel letter that follows

Russian has five basic vowel sounds, and each is written with two letters. One of the two says "the consonant before me is hard". The other says "the consonant before me is soft".

| Vowel sound | After a hard consonant | After a soft consonant |
|---|---|---|
| *a* | а | я |
| *e* | э | е |
| *i* | ы | и |
| *o* | о | ё |
| *u* | у | ю |

So the pairs а/я, э/е, о/ё, у/ю are the *same vowels*. The letter only tells you about the consonant in front. The syllable на is hard н plus *a*; ня is soft н plus the same *a*. Write them in Latin letters and you get *na* and *nya*, which is fine as a rough guide (the Latin spellings in this section still follow the letters; real vowel reduction comes in the next one), but remember that the *y* is not a separate sound: it is a quality of the н.

The middle row is slightly different. Ы and и really are different sounds to the ear: и is a clear *ee*, ы is that dull, pulled-back vowel from the last section. But they behave as a pair in exactly the same way. Ы only ever follows a hard consonant, и follows a soft one, and when grammar calls for "the *i* ending", you get ы after hard consonants and и after soft ones.

Here are pairs of words that differ only in the hardness of one consonant:

:::ex
лук — люк | onion — hatch, manhole | *look* — *lyook*
нос — нёс | nose — was carrying | *nos* — *nyos*
рад — ряд | glad — row | *rad* — *ryad*
мал — мял | (is too) small — was crumpling | *mal* — *myal*
быть — бить | to be — to hit | *byt'* — *beet'*
мы́ло — ми́ло | soap — sweet, nice | *MY-lo* — *MEE-lo*
:::

In the first row, the vowel is the same *oo* in both words. What changes is the л: hard and dark in лук, soft in люк.

### Way two: the soft sign

When there is no vowel after the consonant, at the end of a word or before another consonant, softness is shown by the soft sign, **ь**. It has no sound. It only means "the consonant before me is soft".

:::ex
мат — мать | checkmate; swearing — mother | *mat* — *mat'*
брат — брать | brother — to take | *brat* — *brat'*
у́гол — у́голь | corner — coal | *OO-gol* — *OO-gol'*
мел — мель | chalk — shoal, sandbank | *myel* — *myel'*
ел — ель | (he) was eating — spruce tree | *yel* — *yel'*
вес — весь | weight — all, the whole | *vyes* — *vyes'*
стал — сталь | became — steel | *stal* — *stal'*
ест — есть | eats — to eat; there is | *yest* — *yest'*
:::

These are everyday words, and the contrast carries real grammar. Он говори́т means "he speaks", and говори́ть is "to speak"; the only difference is whether the final т is hard or soft. If your final consonants all come out hard, you will be understood most of the time, but you'll sound like someone saying "ship" for "sheep".

Look closely at мел and мель. In both words the м is soft (that is what е tells you). Only the л differs. Each consonant in a word has its own hardness or softness, signaled by whatever letter follows it.

The soft sign also appears in the middle of words, before a consonant:

:::ex
письмо́ | letter | *pees'-MO*
то́лько | only | *TOL'-ko*
де́ньги | money | *DYEN'-gee*
ма́льчик | boy | *MAL'-cheek*
большо́й | big | *bol'-SHOY*
:::

## How to actually make the sounds

Start with н, л, т and д, where the difference is easiest to hear and feel.

For the **soft** versions, press the flat front part of your tongue (not just the tip) against the ridge behind your upper teeth and the front of the palate. Say "nyet" but try to make the *n* and *y* one gesture: нет. Soft т and д pick up a faint hiss as they release, so that тётя (aunt) can sound a bit like *tsyo-tsya* and де́ти (children) a bit like *dzye-tsee*. That is normal; don't exaggerate it, but don't fight it.

For the **hard** versions, do the opposite: keep the middle of the tongue low and, if anything, pull the back of the tongue back. Hard л is the clearest case. It is "dark", like the *l* at the end of English "full" or "ball", and it is dark everywhere, including at the start of a word: ла́мпа, лук. The light *l* of English "leaf" doesn't exist in Russian; it falls between hard л and soft ль and sounds foreign as either.

A useful drill is to say the pairs slowly, one after the other, with the same vowel: на — ня, ну — ню, ла — ля, лу — лю, та — тя, да — дя. Then with the consonant at the end, which is harder: он — конь (horse), стол (table) — соль (salt), брат — брать.

де́ти | children | pl.
день | day |
ночь | night | f.

## What я, е, ё, ю do when there is no consonant before them

The letters я, е, ё, ю have a second job. When there is no consonant for them to soften, they are pronounced as two sounds: a full, separate *y* (the sound of й) followed by the vowel. This happens in three positions.

**At the start of a word:**

:::ex
я | I | *ya*
я́блоко | apple | *YAB-lo-ko*
е́сли | if | *YES-lee*
ёлка | Christmas tree, fir tree | *YOL-ka*
ю́мор | humor | *YU-mor*
:::

**After another vowel:**

:::ex
моя́ | my, mine (f.) | *mo-YA*
твоё | your, yours (n.) | *tvo-YO*
зна́ю | I know | *ZNA-yu*
пое́хали! | let's go! | *po-YE-kha-lee*
:::

**After ь or ъ.** Here the sign means: soften the consonant if it can be softened, *and then* say a real, separate *y*.

:::ex
семья́ | family | *syem'-YA*
статья́ | article | *stat'-YA*
пью | I drink | *p'yu*
Татья́на | Tatyana | *tat'-YA-na*
компью́тер | computer | *kom-P'YU-ter*
подъе́зд | building entrance, stairwell | *pod-YEZD*
съесть | to eat up | *s-yest'*
:::

Compare these, where the only difference is that separate *y*:

:::ex
сел — съел | sat down — ate up | *syel* — *s-yel*
полёт — польёт | flight — will pour, will water | *po-LYOT* — *pol'-YOT*
:::

This is all the hard sign ъ ever does: it separates a prefix from a following е, ё, ю or я. The soft sign does the same job inside roots and before endings.

The fifth soft-series letter, и, is the exception. At the start of a word or after a vowel it is a plain *ee* with no *y* before it: и́мя (name) is *EE-mya*, Ива́н is *ee-VAN*, мои́ (my, plural) is *mo-EE*. Only after ь does it get a *y*: чьи (whose, plural) is *ch'yee*.

:::note Spelling the sound "yo"
Native words write the sounds *y* + *o* with ё. A few borrowed words spell it out with й or ь plus о instead: йо́гурт (yogurt), райо́н (district), Нью-Йорк, бульо́н (broth), почтальо́н (mail carrier). Nothing new to learn; just read what you see.
:::

## The six consonants that don't pair

Six consonants come in one version only.

| Always hard | Always soft |
|---|---|
| ж, ш, ц | ч, щ, й |

Because these never change, the vowel letter after them can't signal anything. Spelling after them follows convention instead of sound, and that produces a few mismatches you need to know.

**After ж, ш, ц, the consonant stays hard no matter what is written.** The letter и after them is pronounced ы, and е is pronounced э:

:::ex
жить | to live | *zhyt'*
маши́на | car | *ma-SHY-na*
цирк | circus | *tsyrk*
шесть | six | *shest'*
же́нщина | woman | *ZHEN-shchee-na*
центр | center | *tsentr*
:::

In the last section we wrote маши́на as *ma-SHEE-na*, following the letters. Now you know better: there is no *ee* after ш.

A soft sign after ж or ш softens nothing. It is there for grammatical reasons: it marks certain feminine nouns and certain verb forms, as you'll see later. So мышь (mouse) ends in a perfectly hard ш, and зна́ешь (you know) does too.

**After ч and щ, the consonant stays soft no matter what is written.** Russian writes а and у after them, never я and ю, but the consonant is soft all the same:

:::ex
чай | tea | soft ч, although the letter is а
час | hour | 
чу́до | miracle |
ещё | still, more |
щу́ка | pike (the fish) |
ночь | night | the ь changes nothing; ч is soft anyway
:::

These conventions about which vowel letters may follow which consonants are the basis of the [spelling rules](#s-01-05) that later shape many grammatical endings.

**Й** is simply the consonant *y* on its own. It is soft by nature. It usually comes after a vowel: мой (my), чай, музе́й (museum), трамва́й (tram).

## The exception: е in borrowed words

There is one common situation where the spelling misleads. In many borrowed words, the letter е is written after a consonant that is nonetheless pronounced hard, as if the letter were э.

:::ex
те́ннис | tennis | *TE-nees*, not *TYE-*
интерне́т | internet | both т and н are hard: *een-ter-NET*
компью́тер | computer | hard т: *-ter*
кафе́ | café | *ka-FE*
оте́ль | hotel | *o-TEL'*
сви́тер | sweater | *SVEE-ter*
би́знес | business | *BEEZ-nes*
ме́неджер | manager | *ME-ne-dzher*
:::

There is no rule that tells you which borrowed words do this. Many have been fully absorbed and are pronounced the regular, soft way: те́ма (topic), текст, музе́й, пробле́ма, телефо́н, газе́та (newspaper). The hard pronunciation is typical of newer or more "foreign-feeling" words, and for some words Russians themselves disagree. Good dictionaries mark it. When in doubt about an everyday, long-established word, go soft.

## Why this matters for grammar

Here is the payoff, and the reason this section is long.

Russian words change their endings constantly, and the endings begin with vowels. Since every vowel sound has a hard-series letter and a soft-series letter, every such ending has two spellings: one for words whose stem ends in a hard consonant, one for stems ending in a soft consonant. Grammar books present these as "hard declension" and "soft declension", in two columns, and it looks like twice as much to memorize. It isn't. It is one set of endings, spelled according to the table at the top of this section.

A small preview, with two nouns you'll meet properly in Part 3. Стол (table) ends in hard л. Слова́рь (dictionary) ends in soft р, as its ь shows.

| | Hard stem | Soft stem | The ending is really |
|---|---|---|---|
| basic form | стол | слова́рь | nothing |
| "of" form | стол**а́** | словар**я́** | *-a* |
| "to" form | стол**у́** | словар**ю́** | *-u* |

The ending in the second row is the sound *a* for both words. After the hard л of стол it is written а. After the soft р of слова́рь it has to be written я, because that is how Russian shows that the р is still soft. The same goes for у and ю in the third row.

It works the same way with ы and и. Ла́мпа (lamp) has a hard п, and its plural is ла́мп**ы**. Неде́ля (week) has a soft л, and its plural is неде́л**и**. One plural ending, two spellings.

So whenever a later section shows you two columns of endings, hard and soft, look across the rows. You will see а facing я, у facing ю, ы facing и, о facing ё or е. You already know why.

:::summary The idea in brief
- Most consonants have a hard and a soft version; the difference can change the word (брат / брать).
- Softness is written on the following letter: я, е, ё, ю, и or ь mean "soft"; а, э, о, у, ы (or nothing) mean "hard".
- With no consonant before them (word-initially, after a vowel, after ь or ъ), я, е, ё, ю are *y* plus a vowel.
- ж, ш, ц are always hard and ч, щ, й are always soft, whatever letter follows.
- "Hard" and "soft" endings in grammar tables are the same endings in two spellings.
:::
