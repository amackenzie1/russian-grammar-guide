# Handwriting, typing and the letter ё

You can now read printed Russian. This section is about Russian in the wild: what it looks like when written by hand or set in italics, how to type it, and two things that real texts leave out and this guide puts in, the dots on ё and the stress marks.

## Cursive: what Russians actually write

Russian children are taught joined-up cursive from their first weeks of school, and adults go on using it. A handwritten note, a birthday card, a doctor's prescription, the chalkboard menu outside a café: all cursive. Nobody writes in separate printed letters, and an adult who does looks like a small child or, more likely, a foreigner.

You don't need to write beautifully. But you do need to *read* cursive, and several letters change so much that they are unrecognizable unless you've been warned. Here is the whole alphabet three ways: upright print, printed italic, and a handwritten style.

The handwritten letters here come from a brush-script typeface, so they are tidier and more uniform than anyone's real handwriting, and the capitals are more decorative than the ones taught at school. Every hand is a little different; the lower-case shapes are what to study. This typeface also keeps г close to its printed shape, where most people write the hooked wave described below. The main surprises:

- **т** is written with three downstrokes and looks exactly like a Latin handwritten *m*.
- **и** looks like a Latin *u*. **Й** is the same with a small curved stroke above.
- **п** looks like a Latin *n*.
- **ш** looks like a Latin *w* with a flat bottom (or a *u* with one extra stroke). **Щ** is the same with a tiny tail at the lower right, and **ц** is a *u* shape with the same tail.
- **д** looks like a Latin handwritten *g*, with a loop below the line. Some people write it instead with the loop curling up and back over the top, like the mathematical symbol ∂. Both are common.
- **г** is a small hooked wave, something like a backwards *s*.
- **в** is tall, like a Latin handwritten *b* with its bowl closed.
- **б** resembles the digit 6 with a tail flying off to the right at the top.
- **з** is like the digit 3 with a loop hanging below the line.
- **к** is short, the height of а or о, not tall like Latin *k*.
- **л**, **м** and **я** each start with a tiny hook at the baseline. That hook is important: it is often the only thing separating them from their neighbors.

Among the capitals, the notable ones are **Д**, which looks like a Latin *D*; **Т**, written as three downstrokes under a bar; and **У**, which resembles a Latin *Y*.

Put these together and you get the most famous feature of Russian handwriting: long runs of identical up-and-down strokes. In cursive, и is two strokes, ш is three, л and м differ from them only by their little hooks, and т is three strokes as well. Words such as ши́шка (pine cone), слы́шишь (you hear) and лиши́шь (you'll deprive) turn into a picket fence that even Russians read by counting. To help, many people draw a short horizontal line over т and under ш.

So the handwritten word that looks to you like "mun" is тип (type, guy), and the one that looks like "gemu" is де́ти (children). See for yourself:

:::cursive
тип | type, guy
дети | children
пиво | beer
шишка | pine cone
слышишь | you hear

Привет! Как дела? | Hi! How are you?
Спасибо большое | Thanks a lot
:::

:::note Should you learn to write it?
Learn to read it; that is not optional if you'll ever deal with handwritten Russian. Learning to write it is very much worth a few evenings too: it is faster than drawing printed letters, and forming the letters by hand fixes them in memory. The standard tool is про́писи, the copybooks with dotted letters to trace that Russian first-graders use. They are sold everywhere and are easy to find online to print.
:::

## Italics follow the cursive shapes

Here is something that catches out people who never plan to read handwriting at all. Printed *italic* type in Russian is based on cursive, so several lower-case letters change shape when a text is italicized:

| Upright | In italics it looks like |
|---|---|
| т | Latin *m* |
| д | Latin *g*, or ∂ |
| г | a backwards *s* |
| п | Latin *n* |
| и | Latin *u* |

The middle column of the chart and of the word list above shows it. Here it is again in running text. Upright: тип, де́ти, пи́во. Italic: *тип, де́ти, пи́во*. If the italic versions look like "mun", "gemu" and "nuвo", your font has true Cyrillic italics, as most serif fonts do. Some screen fonts just slant the upright letters, in which case compare with the handwritten column instead.

You will meet italics in book titles, emphasis, captions and on a great deal of packaging and signage, so it is worth a few minutes of practice now.

## Typing

### Two kinds of keyboard layout

To type Russian you add a Russian keyboard layout to your computer or phone and switch between it and English. There are two families of layout.

**The standard Russian layout** is named after the first six letters of its top row, [[ns: ЙЦУКЕН]], just as the English one is called QWERTY. This is what is printed on every keyboard sold in Russia and what every Russian uses. The letters are arranged by frequency, with the most common ones under the strongest fingers, and have no relation to the Latin letters on the same keys:

:::keyboard
ё:`
й:q ц:w у:e к:r е:t н:y г:u ш:i щ:o з:p х:[ ъ:]
ф:a ы:s в:d а:f п:g р:h о:j л:k д:l ж:; э:'
я:z ч:x с:c м:v и:b т:n ь:m б:, ю:.
:::

The small gray character on each key is what the same key types in English.

Because Russian has 33 letters, they spill over onto the keys that hold punctuation in English, so some punctuation moves. On the Windows version, the period is on the key just left of the right Shift, the comma is Shift plus that same key, and ё lives at the top left, under Escape.

**Phonetic layouts** put each Russian letter on the key of the Latin letter it resembles in sound: а on A, б on B, д on D, ф on F, and so on, with the leftover letters (ш, щ, ч, ж, ю, я and the signs) placed wherever there is room. You can start typing almost immediately.

Which should you choose? If you only want to type the odd word, a phonetic layout is fine. If you're serious about the language, learn the standard layout. It takes a couple of weeks of mild frustration, after which you can sit down at any computer in the Russian-speaking world. And you'll be using it on your phone anyway.

### Setting it up

The exact menu names shift between versions, so treat the paths below as approximate; the idea is always "add a language or input source".

- **Windows:** Settings → Time & Language → the language settings (called "Language & region" or just "Language", depending on the version) → Add a language → Russian. The standard layout is installed by default; recent versions also include a phonetic one, called "Russian – Mnemonic", which can be added under the language's keyboard options. Switch layouts with Windows key + Space.
- **Mac:** System Settings (System Preferences on older systems) → Keyboard → Input Sources → add Russian. You'll be offered several variants. "Russian – PC" matches the standard Windows layout; plain "Russian" is Apple's own variant, with ё and some punctuation in different places; the phonetic one is called "Russian – Phonetic" or "Russian – QWERTY", depending on the system version. Switch with the Globe key or, on most setups, Control + Space.
- **iPhone and iPad:** Settings → General → Keyboard → Keyboards → Add New Keyboard → Russian. Switch with the globe button.
- **Android:** open the settings of your keyboard app (Gboard on many phones) and add Russian under Languages.

Phone keyboards use the standard layout. To save space most of them hide two letters: hold down е to get ё, and hold down ь to get ъ.

If your physical keyboard has only Latin letters printed on it, you can buy transparent stickers with the Russian letters, or a silicone cover, for very little. Or put a picture of the layout next to your screen for the first week and learn to touch-type, which is the better deal in the long run.

:::aside Russian in Latin letters
In the days of early cell phones and foreign computers without Cyrillic, Russians often wrote to each other in improvised Latin spelling, known as трансли́т: "privet, kak dela?" Today it is rare, and reads as either an emergency or a joke. Don't use it. Even badly spelled Cyrillic is better.
:::

## The letter ё, and its disappearing dots

Here is a fact about real-world Russian that this guide has been shielding you from: **in most printed and typed Russian, ё is written as plain е**. The two dots are considered optional. Newspapers, novels, websites, subtitles and text messages nearly all leave them out.

So the following are what you'll actually see:

| As usually printed | What it is | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| [[ns: еще]] | ещё | still, more |
| [[ns: ее]] | её | her |
| [[ns: идет]] | идёт | is going |
| [[ns: елка]] | ёлка | Christmas tree |
| [[ns: Петр]] | Пётр | Pyotr (Peter) |
| [[ns: все]] | все or всё | everyone, or everything |

Native speakers don't notice. They know that the word for "still" is ещё, so they read it that way whatever is printed. The pronunciation never changes: a ё without its dots is still pronounced *yo* and is still stressed.

For a learner this is an obvious trap. If you learn words from ordinary texts, you can't tell which е is secretly ё, and you'll mispronounce the word and misplace its stress. Three practical consequences:

**Learn new words from sources that print ё.** Dictionaries always do. So do textbooks for foreigners, children's books, and this guide, which writes ё everywhere it belongs, without exception.

**The last row of the table is a real ambiguity.** Все (everyone, all) and всё (everything) are both extremely common, and only context tells you which is meant when the dots are missing. A few other pairs exist: не́бо (sky) and нёбо (the roof of the mouth); узна́ем (we'll find out) and узнаём (we're finding out). Careful writers and editors are supposed to keep the dots in exactly such cases, and in proper names. In practice it is hit and miss.

**Write ё yourself.** Using ё is always correct. Leaving it out is tolerated in native speakers; in your writing it will just look like you don't know the word. Typing it costs one extra keypress.

:::aside How missing dots changed the words
The habit of dropping the dots has consequences even for native speakers and for other languages. The names Горбачёв, Хрущёв and Потёмкин end in a stressed *-yof* or contain a stressed *yo*, but they reached English from dotless spellings, which is why the world says "Gorbachev", "Khrushchev" and "Potemkin". Within Russian, some words are shifting. The dictionary form of "beet" is свёкла, but so many people have seen it without dots that the pronunciation свекла́ is now widespread.
:::

## Where you'll see stress marks in real life

Almost nowhere, and that is the point to absorb. Ordinary Russian text has no stress marks. Russians know where the stress falls in the same way you know that "record" is stressed differently as a noun and as a verb, without anything on the page telling you.

The places where stress *is* marked:

- **Dictionaries and encyclopedias.** Every dictionary marks stress on the headword, and good ones mark it on other forms too. Encyclopedia articles, Wikipedia included, usually mark the stress on the title word or name in the first line.
- **Books for small children** who are learning to read, starting with the буква́рь, the first-grade primer.
- **Textbooks and readers for foreign learners.**
- **Occasionally, in normal text, to prevent misreading.** If a sentence really could be read two ways, a careful writer may mark one word: бо́льшая часть (the greater part), to keep you from reading больша́я (big). The same is done to show that что is emphasized: Я зна́ю, [[ns: что́]] он сказа́л. (I know *what* he said.)

Dictionaries don't all use the same notation. The acute accent used in this guide is the most common. Others put a straight tick before or after the stressed vowel, or print it in bold or underlined. In chat, where accents are awkward to type, Russians who want to show a stress simply capitalize the vowel: [[ns: зАмок]], [[ns: замОк]].

Should you type stress marks? No. They are a reading aid. Nobody writes them in real communication, and a message full of accents looks very odd.

## Tools worth knowing

You'll need to look up stress and forms constantly, and to hear words spoken. A few free resources cover nearly everything.

**Wiktionary.** The English-language Wiktionary has entries for a huge number of Russian words, with the stress marked, an English definition, often a recording, and, most valuably, complete tables of every form of the word with stress on each one. When you want to know how the stress moves in the plural of a noun or the past tense of a verb, this is the fastest place to look. The Russian-language edition, Викислова́рь, has even more words, explained in Russian.

**Gramota.ru.** Written Гра́мота.ру in Russian, this is the reference site Russians themselves use to settle arguments about spelling and stress. It searches several authoritative dictionaries at once. It is entirely in Russian, but you can type in a word and read off the stress and the main forms long before you can read the definitions.

**Forvo.** A large collection of recordings of individual words, and many names and phrases, pronounced by native speakers. Use it whenever you're not sure how something from this part sounds in practice: listen for the reduction in хорошо́, the *sh* in что, the silent letters in здра́вствуйте.

**Automatic stress markers.** Several websites and browser tools will take a passage of ordinary Russian and add stress marks, and often the dots on ё. They are a real help for reading practice. But they work from a dictionary and can't always tell за́мок from замо́к, or все from всё, so treat their output as usually right, not always right.

**Text-to-speech and machine translation.** The "listen" button in translation apps is good enough these days to check how a sentence roughly sounds. It makes the same kind of mistakes with ambiguous words, so don't treat it as the final authority on stress.

That is everything about how Russian is written and pronounced that you need in order to begin. From here on the Latin spellings are gone: every example is in Cyrillic, with its stress marked. Next, we start building sentences.
