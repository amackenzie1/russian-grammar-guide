# How consonants behave

Russian spelling is consistent about one thing that surprises English speakers: it writes each piece of a word the same way every time, even when the pronunciation shifts. Друг (friend) is written with г because the word is дру́га, дру́гу and so on in its other forms, where you hear a clear *g*. But on its own, друг is pronounced *drook*.

So consonants, like vowels, are not always pronounced as written. Unlike stress, almost all of this follows rules with very few exceptions, and most of the rules are things your mouth would do anyway in fast speech.

As in the previous section, the Latin spellings show real pronunciation, with *uh* for the weak neutral vowel.

## Voiced and voiceless

Put your fingers on your throat and say *zzzz*, then *ssss*. For *z* your vocal cords buzz; for *s* they don't. Otherwise the two sounds are made identically. *Z* is **voiced**, *s* is **voiceless**, and they form a pair.

Russian has six such pairs:

| Voiced | б | в | г | д | ж | з |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Voiceless | п | ф | к | т | ш | с |

The remaining consonants stand outside the system. Л, м, н, р and й are voiced and have no voiceless partner. Х, ц, ч and щ are voiceless with no voiced partner in the alphabet.

Two rules follow.

## Rule one: voiced consonants go voiceless at the end of a word

At the very end of a word, a voiced consonant from the table is pronounced as its voiceless partner.

:::ex
хлеб | bread | *khlyep*
друг | friend | *drook*
год | year | *got*
муж | husband | *moosh*
глаз | eye | *glas*
нож | knife | *nosh*
Ивано́в | Ivanov | *ee-va-NOF*
любо́вь | love | *lyu-BOF'*
:::

The last example shows that a soft sign doesn't protect the consonant: вь is still final, and becomes a soft *f*.

This creates words that sound identical but are spelled differently:

:::ex
код — кот | code — cat | both *kot*
луг — лук | meadow — onion | both *look*
гриб — грипп | mushroom — flu | both *greep*
:::

As soon as an ending is added, the consonant is no longer final and its voice comes back: год is *got*, but го́ды (years) is *GO-dy*; нож is *nosh*, but ножи́ (knives) is *na-ZHY*. This is why the spelling keeps the voiced letter, and it is how you can check which letter to write.

English keeps final voicing ("bed" versus "bet"), so this takes practice: хлеб with a real *b* is one of the more audible features of an English accent.

## Rule two: neighbors agree in voicing

When two consonants from the table stand next to each other, they must match: both voiced or both voiceless. The **second** one decides, and the first one adjusts.

A voiced consonant before a voiceless one becomes voiceless:

:::ex
во́дка | vodka | *VOT-kuh*
ло́жка | spoon | *LOSH-kuh*
за́втра | tomorrow | *ZAF-truh*
авто́бус | bus | *af-TO-boos*
вчера́ | yesterday | *fchee-RA*
всё | everything | *fsyo*
всегда́ | always | *fseeg-DA*
оши́бка | mistake | *a-SHYP-kuh*
вход | entrance | *fkhot*
:::

A voiceless consonant before a voiced one becomes voiced:

:::ex
футбо́л | soccer | *food-BOL*
вокза́л | train station | *vag-ZAL*
экза́мен | exam | *eg-ZA-meen*
сде́лать | to do, to make | *ZDYE-luht'*
про́сьба | request | *PROZ'-buh*
о́тдых | rest, vacation | *OD-dykh*
та́кже | also | *TAG-zhy*
:::

English does a little of this (the *s* of "dogs" is really a *z*), but Russian does it everywhere and in the opposite direction: the later sound controls the earlier one.

### The exceptions: в, and the unpaired consonants

Л, м, н, р and й don't trigger anything. A voiceless consonant before them stays voiceless: слон (elephant) is *slon*, not *zlon*.

The consonant **в** is the odd one. What follows affects it, as in вчера́ and за́втра, but it doesn't affect what comes before it. A voiceless consonant before в stays voiceless:

:::ex
свой | one's own | *svoy*, not *zvoy*
твой | your, yours | *tvoy*
кварти́ра | apartment | *kvar-TEE-ruh*
Москва́ | Moscow | *mask-VA*
:::

## Prepositions are pronounced as part of the next word

Russian prepositions are small, several are a single consonant, and the short ones are not separate words in pronunciation. В (in), с (with; from), к (toward) and the rest attach to the front of the following word with no pause, and all the rules above apply across the join. (The nouns below have endings from Part 3; watch the first consonant.)

:::ex
в шко́ле | at school | *FSHKO-lye*
в кино́ | at the movies | *fkee-NO*
в Москве́ | in Moscow | *vmask-VYE*
с бра́том | with (my) brother | *ZBRA-tuhm*
к до́му | toward the house | *GDO-moo*
из па́рка | out of the park | *ees-PAR-kuh*
под столо́м | under the table | *puht-sta-LOM*
без са́хара | without sugar | *bees-SA-khuh-ruh*
:::

So в is often pronounced *f*, and с is often *z*. The longer prepositions also show that they are unstressed: под becomes *puht*, без becomes *bees*.

A preposition ending in a hard consonant stays hard, so if the next word starts with и, that и is pronounced ы:

:::ex
в Ита́лии | in Italy | *vy-TA-lee-ee*
с Ива́ном | with Ivan | *sy-VA-nuhm*
:::

## Spellings that are simply pronounced differently

A few letter combinations have a fixed pronunciation that doesn't match the letters. They occur in some of the most common words in the language.

### The ending -ого / -его is pronounced with в

In the adjective and pronoun endings -ого and -его, the letter г is pronounced *v*. No exceptions.

:::ex
его́ | his; him | *yee-VO*
кого́ | whom | *ka-VO*
ничего́ | nothing; it's OK | *nee-chee-VO*
сего́дня | today | *see-VOD-nyuh*
но́вого | of the new | *NO-vuh-vuh*
До́брого у́тра! | Good morning! (as a wish) | *DOB-ruh-vuh OOT-ruh*
Всего́ хоро́шего! | All the best! | *fsee-VO kha-RO-shy-vuh*
:::

Сего́дня belongs here because it is historically two words in exactly this form, "of this day".

The rule applies only where -ого or -его is a grammatical ending. Where г is part of the root, it is a normal *g*: мно́го (a lot) is *MNO-guh*, до́рого (expensive) is *DO-ruh-guh*.

### Что is pronounced with ш

The word **что** (what; that) is pronounced *shto*. So are the words built from it:

:::ex
что | what; that | *shto*
что́бы | in order to, so that | *SHTO-by*
что-то | something | *SHTO-tuh*
потому́ что | because | *puh-ta-MOO-shtuh*
ничто́ | nothing | *nee-SHTO*
:::

This is a property of this one word, not of the letters чт: по́чта (mail) and мечта́ (dream) have an ordinary ч. So does the bookish word не́что (something).

### In a few words, чн is pronounced шн

:::ex
коне́чно | of course | *ka-NYESH-nuh*
ску́чно | boring, bored | *SKOOSH-nuh*
наро́чно | on purpose | *na-ROSH-nuh*
яи́чница | fried eggs | *yee-EESH-nee-tsuh*
:::

This is a short, closed list, a leftover of old Moscow pronunciation. Nearly all other words with чн are pronounced as written: то́чно (exactly), отли́чно (excellent), сро́чно (urgently), ночно́й (night, as an adjective).

### Г before к is pronounced х

In two common adjectives and their relatives, гк sounds like *khk*:

:::ex
лёгкий | light; easy | *LYOKH-keey*
мя́гкий | soft | *MYAKH-keey*
легко́ | easily; it's easy | *leekh-KO*
:::

### -тся and -ться are both pronounced "tsa"

Many verb forms end in -тся or -ться. Both are pronounced as a long *ts* plus a weak vowel: *-tsuh*. The soft sign is not heard.

:::ex
учи́ться | to study | *oo-CHEE-tsuh*
у́чится | (he/she) studies | *OO-chee-tsuh*
нра́вится | (it) is pleasing | *NRA-vee-tsuh*
ка́жется | it seems | *KA-zhy-tsuh*
смея́ться | to laugh | *smee-YA-tsuh*
:::

The spelling difference matters grammatically (one is the dictionary form of the verb, the other the "he/she" form), and because the two sound the same, mixing them up is among the most common spelling mistakes Russians make. Which is which is covered in [the section on -ся verbs](#s-04-06).

The same merger happens inside words wherever т or д meets с: де́тский (children's) is *DYETS-keey*, городско́й (urban, city) is *guh-rats-KOY*.

### Сч is pronounced like щ

The combination сч is pronounced as the long soft sound of щ. So is жч.

:::ex
сча́стье | happiness | *SHCHAS-t'ye*
счёт | bill, check; score | *shchot*
счита́ть | to count; to consider | *shchee-TAT'*
мужчи́на | man | *moo-SHCHEE-nuh*
:::

## Silent consonants

When three or more consonants pile up, Russian often drops the middle one in speech while keeping it in spelling.

:::ex
здра́вствуйте | hello (formal) | *ZDRAST-vooy-tye*; the first в is silent
чу́вство | feeling | *CHOOST-vuh*; the first в is silent
со́лнце | sun | *SON-tse*; л is silent
се́рдце | heart | *SYER-tse*; д is silent
по́здно | late | *POZ-nuh*; д is silent
ле́стница | stairs, ladder | *LYES-nee-tsuh*; т is silent
гру́стный | sad | *GROOS-nyy*; т is silent
счастли́вый | happy | *shchees-LEE-vyy*; т is silent
:::

The usual patterns are стн → *sn*, здн → *zn*, and a few one-off cases such as вств, лнц and рдц.

A related word shows which unheard letter to write, just as with final consonants. По́здно has a д because of опозда́ть (to be late), where the д is clearly heard. Гру́стный has a т because of грусть (sadness). Со́лнце has an л because of со́лнечный (sunny), where the л is pronounced.

:::note What fast speech does to long words
Beyond these standard silent letters, very common words get worn down further in everyday speech. Здра́вствуйте often comes out as *ZDRAS-tye*. Пожа́луйста (please) is *pa-ZHAL-stuh*, never five full syllables. Сейча́с (now) is frequently just *shchas*, and ты́сяча (thousand) is *TY-shchuh*. You don't need to produce these yet, but you need to recognize them. Part 7 has more on [colloquial shortcuts](#s-07-07).
:::

## Double consonants

Russian spelling has plenty of double letters. Whether you hear a longer consonant depends on where they are.

**At a join between parts of a word** (prefix plus root, root plus suffix, or preposition plus word), a double consonant is pronounced long, held for an extra beat:

:::ex
отту́да | from there | *at-TOO-duh*, with a held *t*
дли́нный | long | *DLEEN-nyy*, with a held *n*
в воде́ | in the water | *v-va-DYE*, with a held *v*
:::

**Between vowels right after the stress**, doubles in borrowed words also tend to be long:

:::ex
ва́нна | bathtub | long *n*
ка́сса | cash register | long *s*
су́мма | sum, amount | long *m*
:::

**Elsewhere**, especially before the stress, at the end of a word, or next to another consonant, a double letter is usually pronounced as a single consonant:

:::ex
суббо́та | Saturday | *soo-BO-tuh*
ру́сский | Russian | *ROOS-keey*
Росси́я | Russia | *ra-SEE-yuh*
профе́ссор | professor | *pra-FYE-suhr*
класс | class; grade | *klas*
:::

Speakers vary here, and lengthening one that others don't does no harm.

The real difficulty with doubles is spelling, especially in borrowed words, where Russian often disagrees with English about what gets doubled:

:::ex
а́дрес | address | one д, one с
о́фис | office | one ф
коридо́р | corridor | one р
ата́ка | attack | one т
галере́я | gallery | one л
агре́ссия | aggression | one г, two с
:::

There is no rule; each has to be learned.

:::summary The main points
- At the end of a word, б в г д ж з are pronounced п ф к т ш с.
- In a cluster, the last paired consonant sets the voicing for the ones before it; в, л, м, н, р, й don't trigger voicing.
- Prepositions are pronounced as part of the next word, and the same rules apply across the join.
- The endings -ого / -его have *v*; что has *sh*; -тся / -ться is *-tsuh*; сч is щ.
- In clusters like стн and здн, the middle consonant is silent.
:::
