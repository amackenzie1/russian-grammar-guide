# What cases are

Every sentence has to answer a basic question: who is doing what to whom? English answers it with position. In "Masha loves Sasha", the lover comes before the verb and the loved one after it. Swap the names and you have swapped the meaning. Whatever is left over gets a preposition: *to* Masha, *with* Masha, *about* Masha, *of* Masha.

Russian answers the same question differently: it writes the role on the word itself. A noun changes its ending to say "I'm the doer", "I'm the thing acted on", "I'm the receiver", "I'm the tool". These role-marking forms are called **cases**, and Russian has six of them.

:::vocab
люби́ть | to love | люблю́, лю́бишь, лю́бят
ви́деть | to see | ви́жу, ви́дишь, ви́дят
кни́га | book | f.
ру́чка | pen | f.
маши́на | car | f.
сестра́ | sister | f.
паде́ж | case (the grammar term) | m.
:::

## The role is on the word

Here is the whole idea in two sentences:

:::ex
Ма́ша лю́бит Са́шу. | Masha loves Sasha.
Са́ша лю́бит Ма́шу. | Sasha loves Masha.
:::

Look at the endings, not the positions. The name in its dictionary form, ending in -а, is the one doing the loving. The name ending in **-у** is the one being loved. That -у is the entire difference between loving and being loved, and it stays attached to the name wherever the name goes:

:::ex
Ма́ша лю́бит Са́шу. | Masha loves Sasha.
Са́шу лю́бит Ма́ша. | Masha loves Sasha. | the same fact; roughly "the one who loves Sasha is Masha"
Ма́ша Са́шу лю́бит. | Masha loves Sasha. | roughly "Masha does love Sasha"
:::

All three sentences describe the same situation. Masha is the one with the feelings in every one of them, because Ма́ша is in the doer form and Са́шу is in the acted-on form. An English speaker reading Са́шу лю́бит Ма́ша instinctively sees "Sasha loves Masha", and that instinct is the first thing to retrain. In Russian, **you find the subject by its ending, not by its place in line.**

English actually has a fossil of this system, in its pronouns. "He sees her" and "she sees him": *he* turns into *him*, *she* into *her*, depending on the role. That's why "Him I like, her I can't stand" is understandable even though the order is scrambled. Russian does to every noun, adjective and pronoun what English does only to a handful of pronouns.

If you have studied Japanese, think of particles: a little tag after each noun that states its job. A Russian case ending is a particle that has been fused onto the end of the word.

## What a case looks like

A case is not a different word, just a different ending on the same word. Here is кни́га (book) and стол (table) in all six cases. Don't memorize this table. Just look at it to see what kind of thing we're dealing with.

| Case | кни́га | стол |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | кни́г**а** | стол |
| Accusative | кни́г**у** | стол |
| Genitive | кни́г**и** | стол**а́** |
| Dative | кни́г**е** | стол**у́** |
| Instrumental | кни́г**ой** | стол**о́м** |
| Prepositional | (о) кни́г**е** | (о) стол**е́** |

Three things to notice. First, the changes are small: one or two letters at the end. Second, some forms coincide (кни́ге appears twice, стол appears twice); the context always sorts them out. Third, the two words use different sets of endings. Which set a noun uses depends on its gender and on whether its stem ends in a hard or a soft consonant, which is why we spent time on [gender](#s-02-02) and on [hard and soft consonants](#s-01-02). You already know everything you need in order to predict the endings; the rest of this part just shows you the patterns.

The form you find in the dictionary is the **nominative**. Every noun, adjective and pronoun you have met so far in this guide was in the nominative. Everything else is built from it.

## The six cases at a glance

Each case has one core job and a few side jobs. Here is each one's job description in a line, with a single example. The following sections take them one at a time.

**Nominative**: the subject, the one who does or is. Also the "naming" form, used after э́то and in the dictionary.

:::ex
**Ма́ша** чита́ет. | Masha is reading.
:::

**Accusative**: the direct object, the thing the action lands on.

:::ex
Я зна́ю **Ма́шу**. | I know Masha.
:::

**Genitive**: "of". Belonging, relation, and also quantity and absence.

:::ex
Э́то кни́га **Ма́ши**. | This is Masha's book. | literally "book of-Masha"
:::

**Dative**: "to" or "for" someone. The receiver, and the person who experiences a feeling.

:::ex
Я пишу́ **Ма́ше**. | I'm writing to Masha.
:::

**Instrumental**: "with" or "by means of". The tool, the companion, the role you play.

:::ex
Я пишу́ **ру́чкой**. | I'm writing with a pen.
Я рабо́таю с **Ма́шей**. | I work with Masha.
:::

**Prepositional**: used only after a few prepositions, mainly to say *where* and *about what*.

:::ex
Мы говори́м о **Ма́ше**. | We're talking about Masha.
:::

Notice Я пишу́ Ма́ше and Я пишу́ ру́чкой. English needs two little words, "to" and "with", to keep those apart. Russian needs none: the endings -е and -ой already say "receiver" and "tool". That's a case doing a preposition's work.

### The question words

Russians themselves learn the cases in school by asking questions. The words кто (who) and что (what) have case forms too, and each case "answers" its own pair. This is useful: when a Russian teacher or textbook wants to tell you which case a verb requires, they write the question, as in помога́ть **кому́?** (to help *whom?*, so: dative).

| Case | Russian name | who? | what? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | имени́тельный | кто | что |
| Accusative | вини́тельный | кого́ | что |
| Genitive | роди́тельный | кого́ | чего́ |
| Dative | да́тельный | кому́ | чему́ |
| Instrumental | твори́тельный | кем | чем |
| Prepositional | предло́жный | о ком | о чём |

(In кого́ and чего́ the letter г is pronounced like в, as in его́; see [how consonants behave](#s-01-04).)

:::ex
**Кого́** ты ви́дишь? — Ма́шу. | Who do you see? — Masha.
**Кому́** ты пи́шешь? — Ма́ше. | Who are you writing to? — Masha.
**О ком** вы говори́те? — О Ма́ше. | Who are you talking about? — Masha.
:::

Look at those answers. In English all three are just "Masha". In Russian a one-word answer still carries its case, so each answer is a different form. The role never goes unmarked, even when the rest of the sentence has been left out.

## What decides the case

A noun doesn't choose its own case. Something else in the sentence assigns it. There are three kinds of bosses:

1. **The role in the sentence.** Subject? Nominative. Direct object? Accusative. Receiver? Dative. This is the part that works like the Ма́ша лю́бит Са́шу example.
2. **A preposition.** Every Russian preposition demands a particular case from the noun after it. The preposition с meaning "with" always takes the instrumental; для (for) always takes the genitive. You learn the case along with the preposition, as one piece of knowledge. A handful of prepositions take two different cases with two different meanings: в with the prepositional means "in", while в with the accusative means "into".
3. **A particular verb or word.** Some verbs simply require a certain case from their object, in ways English wouldn't predict. Помога́ть (to help) takes the dative: in Russian you help *to* someone. Quantity words such as мно́го (a lot) take the genitive: "a lot *of*".

So the practical question is never "what are all the uses of the dative?" in the abstract. It's "what is the boss of this noun, and what case does that boss demand?"

## Everything attached to the noun changes too

You know from [adjectives](#s-02-05) that an adjective copies the gender and number of its noun: но́вый дом, но́вая кни́га. It copies the case as well. So do possessives like мой and pointing words like э́тот. The whole phrase moves into the case together:

| Case | "my old car" |
|---|---|
| Nominative | моя́ ста́рая маши́на |
| Accusative | мою́ ста́рую маши́ну |
| Genitive | мое́й ста́рой маши́ны |
| Dative | мое́й ста́рой маши́не |
| Instrumental | мое́й ста́рой маши́ной |
| Prepositional | (о) мое́й ста́рой маши́не |

:::ex
Э́то моя́ ста́рая маши́на. | This is my old car.
Я люблю́ мою́ ста́рую маши́ну. | I love my old car.
:::

This looks like a lot of extra work, and at first it is. But it is also what holds a Russian sentence together. Because every word in a phrase carries the same case tag, you can always tell which adjective belongs to which noun, even in a long sentence, even when the words have been moved around. The redundancy is a feature.

For that reason each case section in this part teaches the noun endings, the adjective endings and the endings of мой, твой, наш, ваш and э́тот side by side, so that you learn to decline phrases, not isolated words.

## Why word order is free, and what it's for

Since the endings say who does what, Russian doesn't need word order for that job. So it uses word order for something else: to show what the sentence is *about* and what the *news* is. Compare:

:::ex
Ма́ша лю́бит Са́шу. | Masha loves Sasha. | a neutral statement about Masha
Са́шу лю́бит Ма́ша. | It's Masha who loves Sasha. | we were talking about Sasha; the news is who loves him
:::

English has to reach for stress, or for clumsy frames like "it's ... who", to get the same effect. Russian just rearranges. "Free" doesn't mean random, though. There is a default order (subject, verb, object, as in English), and departures from it mean something. We'll come back to this properly in [word order](#s-03-09), once you have the cases to play with. Until then, stick to the English-like order when you build sentences, and don't be thrown when the Russian you read doesn't.

## How this part is organized

Russian schoolbooks list the cases in a traditional order that has nothing to do with how useful they are. We'll take them in the order that gets you speaking fastest:

1. **[Accusative](#s-03-02)** first, because direct objects are everywhere. You can't say "I'm reading a book" or "I love you" without it. It is also cheap: for most nouns the accusative looks exactly like the nominative, and there is really only one new ending to learn.
2. **[Prepositional](#s-03-03)** next: it has the simplest endings of all and it lets you say where things are.
3. **[Genitive](#s-03-04)** is the big one. It is the most frequent case after the nominative, and it's how Russian says "of", "I have", "there isn't any" and "a lot of". It gets the longest section.
4. **[Dative](#s-03-05)**: receivers, and a very Russian way of talking about feelings and needs ("to me it is cold").
5. **[Instrumental](#s-03-06)**: tools, company, and what you work as.

All five of those sections deal with the **singular only**. Then [cases in the plural](#s-03-07) adds the plural endings in one go (they are simpler than the singular, with one notorious exception), [pronouns and determiners](#s-03-08) gathers all the pronoun forms into full tables, and [word order](#s-03-09) shows you what the freedom is used for.

In tables, the cases always appear in this guide's fixed order: nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, instrumental, prepositional.

## How to learn cases without losing your mind

A complete table of Russian endings is a frightening object, and staring at it is a poor way to learn. Some advice from the other side:

- **Learn each case as a job, plus the few words that demand it.** "Accusative: the thing acted on; also after в and на when going somewhere." "Instrumental: after с 'with'." The endings then have something to attach to in your memory.
- **Learn phrases, not charts.** People remember у меня́ есть (I have), на рабо́те (at work), с удово́льствием (with pleasure), and derive the endings from those. Every example in the following sections is meant to be stolen whole.
- **There are fewer endings than it seems.** Hard and soft variants are the same ending spelled two ways (-у and -ю, -ом and -ем), following the vowel pairs from Part 1. Feminine adjectives use one ending for four different cases. Neuter nouns behave almost exactly like masculine ones. You'll see the system shrink as you go.
- **One case at a time.** When you're working on the accusative, let yourself be wrong about everything else.
- **Mistakes here are survivable.** A wrong ending makes you sound foreign. It rarely makes you incomprehensible, especially if you keep to the neutral word order. Russians hear learners get cases wrong constantly and understand them fine. Aim to get the accusative and the prepositional solid, then widen out.

:::note A word about stress
In some nouns the stress moves when the ending changes: рука́ (hand) but ру́ку in the accusative; стол but стола́ in the genitive. This is the one part of the case system that can't be predicted from the dictionary form. Most nouns keep their stress put, and the common movers come in a few recurring patterns, which are collected in [stress patterns](#s-09-07). In this part every example is stress-marked, and we point out the important movers as they come up.
:::

:::aside Six cases, and some leftovers
You may read that Russian "really" has more than six cases. There is something to this: a few nouns have a special location form (в лесу́, "in the forest") and a special "some of it" form (ча́ю, "some tea"), and there's a new colloquial form for calling people (Маш! for Ма́ша). These are small, closed sets of words, and we treat them as footnotes to the six main cases, where they belong.
:::
