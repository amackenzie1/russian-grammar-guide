# The genitive: of, none, and some

The genitive is the workhorse of the Russian case system. After the nominative it is the most frequent case in the language, and it covers so much ground that it can seem like several unrelated cases sharing one set of endings. They aren't unrelated. Nearly everything the genitive does is something English does with the word **of**:

- **Belonging and relation:** the book *of* my brother, the center *of* the city.
- **Quantity:** a cup *of* tea, a lot *of* work.
- **Absence:** there is none *of* it.
- **Source and separation:** out *of* the house, free *of* sugar.

Along the way the genitive gives you two constructions you can't live without: "I have" and "there isn't any".

This section covers the genitive **singular** only. That is a real limitation, because quantities badly want the plural ("many books", "five friends"), and the genitive plural is the one genuinely messy corner of Russian noun endings. It gets its own treatment in [cases in the plural](#s-03-07). Here we'll stick to singular nouns and to stuff you can't count: water, time, work.

:::vocab
нет | there is no, there isn't any | also "no"
есть | there is; (someone) has | unchanging; not the verb "to eat"
у | at, by; at (someone's) place | + gen.
мно́го | a lot (of) | + gen.
ма́ло | little, not enough (of) | + gen.
немно́го | a little (of) | + gen.
ско́лько | how much, how many | + gen.
вре́мя | time | n.; gen. вре́мени
молоко́ | milk | n.
чай | tea | m.
хлеб | bread | m.

буты́лка | bottle | f.
ча́шка | cup | f.
стака́н | glass | m.
кусо́к | piece | m.; gen. куска́
боя́ться | to be afraid of | бою́сь, бои́шься, боя́тся; + gen.; a verb in -ся, a type covered in Part 4

## The endings

Masculine and neuter nouns share their endings; feminine nouns have their own.

| | Nominative | Genitive | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| m. hard | брат, стол | бра́т**а**, стол**а́** | -а |
| m. soft | музе́й, учи́тель | музе́**я**, учи́тел**я** | -я |
| n. hard | окно́, молоко́ | окн**а́**, молок**а́** | -а |
| n. soft | мо́ре, зда́ние | мо́р**я**, зда́ни**я** | -я |
| f. hard | маши́на, сестра́ | маши́н**ы**, сестр**ы́** | -ы |
| f. soft | неде́ля, Росси́я | неде́л**и**, Росси́**и** | -и |
| f. in -ь | ночь, дверь | но́ч**и**, две́р**и** | -и |

As always, each pair (-а and -я, -ы and -и) is one ending in hard and soft spelling.

The [spelling rule](#s-01-05) "never ы after г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ" hits a great many feminine nouns, so that their genitive ends in **-и** even though they are hard: кни́га → кни́ги, подру́га (female friend) → подру́ги, де́вушка (young woman) → де́вушки, ба́бушка → ба́бушки, Ма́ша → Ма́ши, Са́ша → Са́ши.

Masculine nouns in -а and -я behave like feminines, as usual: па́па → па́пы, Ди́ма → Ди́мы, дя́дя → дя́ди.

A few nouns you already know are irregular. Two feminine nouns grow an extra syllable: мать → **ма́тери**, дочь → **до́чери**. Neuter nouns in -мя do something similar: вре́мя → **вре́мени**, и́мя (name) → **и́мени**. There are only a handful of these, and they're collected in [irregular nouns and loose ends](#s-08-06).

### Three things that will trip you up

**It looks like the plural.** The feminine genitive singular usually looks identical to the nominative plural: кни́ги is both "of the book" and "books". Context always settles it. Sometimes the stress does too: сестры́ (of the sister) but сёстры (sisters), руки́ (of the hand) but ру́ки (hands), страны́ (of the country) but стра́ны (countries). Neuter nouns play the same game in reverse: окна́ (of the window) but о́кна (windows), письма́ (of the letter) but пи́сьма (letters).

**Fleeting vowels.** Some nouns have an о or е in their last syllable that exists only to make the bare nominative pronounceable. As soon as an ending is added, the vowel disappears. This happens in all the cases, but the genitive is where you'll first notice it.

| Nominative | Genitive | |
|---|---|---|
| оте́ц | отца́ | father |
| день | дня | day |
| пода́рок | пода́рка | gift |
| ры́нок | ры́нка | market |
| кусо́к | куска́ | piece |
| ребёнок | ребёнка | child |
| коне́ц | конца́ | end |
| у́гол | угла́ | corner |
| па́рень | па́рня | guy |
| ве́тер | ве́тра | wind |
| сон | сна | sleep, dream |
| рот | рта | mouth |

It's typical of the suffixes -ок and -ец, so you can often see it coming. But not every о or е drops: уро́к → уро́ка, челове́к → челове́ка, биле́т (ticket) → биле́та. A feminine example: любо́вь (love) → любви́.

**Stress that jumps to the ending.** Many masculine nouns, especially short ones, are stressed on the ending whenever they have one: стол → стола́, кот → кота́, врач → врача́, язы́к (language, tongue) → языка́, эта́ж → этажа́, ключ (key) → ключа́, нож (knife) → ножа́, дождь (rain) → дождя́, рубль (ruble) → рубля́, слова́рь → словаря́, янва́рь → января́. If the genitive is end-stressed, the other singular cases are too (столу́, столо́м, на столе́), so the genitive is the form worth learning with the noun. Dictionaries list it for exactly this reason.

Feminine nouns stressed on the ending keep it there: вода́ → воды́, рука́ → руки́, голова́ → головы́. The jump back that you saw in во́ду and ру́ку is, within the singular, a matter for the accusative alone.

### The animate accusative, explained

In [the accusative](#s-03-02) you learned that masculine nouns for people and animals take -а or -я as objects: Я ви́жу бра́та. Now you can see where that came from. It is this genitive, borrowed. So everything in the table above, fleeting vowels and stress shifts included, applies to those accusatives:

:::ex
Я ви́жу отца́. | I see my father.
Мы ждём врача́. | We're waiting for the doctor.
Ты зна́ешь э́того па́рня? | Do you know this guy?
:::

## Adjectives, possessives and э́тот

| | Hard | Soft | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| m. and n. | **-ого** | **-его** | но́вого до́ма, си́него мо́ря |
| f. | **-ой** | **-ей** | но́вой шко́лы, си́ней маши́ны |

The masculine and neuter ending is the one you saw on loan in the animate accusative. Remember that its г is pronounced в.

The feminine ending -ой / -ей should look familiar: it was also the feminine prepositional ending. In fact a feminine adjective has this same ending in the genitive, dative, instrumental and prepositional. Learn it once and you're done with four cases.

| Nominative | m. and n. genitive | f. genitive |
|---|---|---|
| мой | моего́ | мое́й |
| твой | твоего́ | твое́й |
| наш | на́шего | на́шей |
| ваш | ва́шего | ва́шей |
| э́тот | э́того | э́той |
| тот | того́ | той |
| весь | всего́ | всей |
| оди́н | одного́ | одно́й |

Mixed-type adjectives follow the spelling rules as before: хоро́шего, хоро́шей; большо́го, большо́й; ру́сского, ру́сской.

## Pronouns

The genitive of the personal pronouns is the same as their accusative:

| Nominative | Genitive | After a preposition |
|---|---|---|
| я | меня́ | у меня́ |
| ты | тебя́ | у тебя́ |
| он, оно́ | его́ | у **н**его́ |
| она́ | её | у **н**её |
| мы | нас | у нас |
| вы | вас | у вас |
| они́ | их | у **н**их |

After a preposition the third-person forms gain an н-: у него́, для неё, без них. The question words are **кого́** (of whom) and **чего́** (of what).

## Job one: "of"

To connect two nouns the way English does with "of" or with 's, put the second one in the genitive. The owner, or the whole, or the thing something relates to, comes *second*.

:::ex
кни́га бра́та | my brother's book | "book of-brother"
маши́на А́ни | Anya's car |
дом ба́бушки | Grandma's house |
центр го́рода | the city center | "center of-city"
коне́ц фи́льма | the end of the movie |
нача́ло уро́ка | the beginning of the lesson |
но́мер телефо́на | phone number | "number of-phone"
учи́тель му́зыки | music teacher | "teacher of-music"
день рожде́ния | birthday | "day of-birth"
:::

This is one of the most common patterns in the language, partly because Russian uses it where English simply stacks two nouns together. "Bus stop", "phone number", "history teacher": Russian has no way to glue nouns side by side like that, so it says остано́вка авто́буса, но́мер телефо́на, учи́тель исто́рии.

Adjectives and possessives inside the genitive phrase agree with their noun, of course:

:::ex
Э́то маши́на моего́ бра́та. | This is my brother's car.
Как зову́т му́жа твое́й сестры́? | What's your sister's husband's name?
Я не по́мню назва́ние э́того фи́льма. | I don't remember the title of this movie.
Она́ дире́ктор большо́й компа́нии. | She's the director of a big company.
Где ключи́ от маши́ны? — В су́мке Ма́ши. | Where are the car keys? — In Masha's bag.
:::

Genitives can be chained, each one hanging off the noun before it:

:::ex
день рожде́ния сестры́ моего́ дру́га | my friend's sister's birthday | "day of-birth of-sister of-my friend"
:::

The question word for possession is чей (whose), which you met in [pronouns and possessives](#s-02-04). The short answer is a bare genitive:

:::ex
Чей э́то телефо́н? — Ди́мы. | Whose phone is this? — Dima's.
:::

:::note Why его́, её and их never change
For "my book" you use the possessive мой, not the genitive of я: моя́ кни́га, never "кни́га меня́". The same goes for твой, наш and ваш. But look at the third person. Его́, её and их are simply the genitives of он, она́ and они́: его́ кни́га is literally "book of him". That is why they don't agree with anything and never decline: they are already in a case.
:::

## Job two: there isn't any

To say that something *is* somewhere, Russian uses **есть** (there is) or nothing at all. To say that something *isn't* there, it uses **нет**, and the missing thing goes into the genitive.

:::ex
Здесь есть интерне́т. | There's internet here.
Здесь нет интерне́т**а**. | There's no internet here.
В холоди́льнике нет молок**а́**. | There's no milk in the fridge.
В на́шем го́роде нет метро́. | There's no subway in our city.
Ма́ш**и** нет до́ма. | Masha isn't home.
Вре́мен**и** нет. | There's no time.
:::

This нет is not quite the same word as the нет that means "no". Historically it is a squashed-together "не есть", and it means "there is not". The logic of the genitive here is the "none of" idea: zero quantity of something.

Notice Ма́ши нет до́ма. English says "Masha isn't home" with Masha as the subject. Russian treats a person's absence just like the absence of milk: "of Masha there is none at home". With pronouns:

:::ex
Его́ сейча́с нет. | He's not here right now.
Где О́ля? — Её нет, она́ в о́тпуске. | Where's Olya? — She's not in; she's on vacation.
:::

In the past, нет becomes **[[ns:не́ было]]** (stressed on не), and in the future **не бу́дет**. Both are frozen: always neuter, always singular, no matter what or who is missing. There is nothing for the verb to agree with, because the sentence has no nominative subject.

:::ex
Вчера́ А́ни [[ns:не́ было]] на рабо́те. | Anya wasn't at work yesterday.
В магази́не [[ns:не́ было]] хле́ба. | There was no bread at the store.
За́втра меня́ не бу́дет. | I won't be here tomorrow.
Экза́мена не бу́дет. | There won't be an exam.
:::

## Job three: "I have"

Russian has no everyday verb "to have". Instead it says that something exists *by* you, using the preposition **у** (at, by) and the genitive:

:::ex
У меня́ есть маши́на. | I have a car. | literally "by me there is a car"
У Ма́ши есть соба́ка. | Masha has a dog.
У тебя́ есть брат? | Do you have a brother?
У моего́ дру́га есть да́ча. | My friend has a dacha.
:::

Get the roles straight, because they're the reverse of English. The *owner* is in the genitive after у. The *thing owned* is the subject, in the **nominative**. Маши́на and соба́ка above haven't changed at all; no accusative is involved, since nobody is doing anything to them. They simply exist, in someone's vicinity.

Because the thing owned is the subject, the verb "to be" agrees with it in the past and future:

:::ex
У меня́ был кот. | I had a cat.
У меня́ была́ соба́ка. | I had a dog.
У нас бы́ло вре́мя. | We had time.
У него́ за́втра бу́дет экза́мен. | He has an exam tomorrow.
:::

And "don't have" is just the "there isn't any" construction from job two: у + owner, нет, and the thing in the genitive.

:::ex
У меня́ нет маши́н**ы**. | I don't have a car.
У него́ нет бра́т**а**. | He doesn't have a brother.
У нас нет вре́мен**и**. | We don't have time.
У неё [[ns:не́ было]] рабо́т**ы**. | She didn't have a job.
У нас за́втра не бу́дет уро́к**а**. | We won't have a lesson tomorrow.
:::

So the same noun flips between nominative and genitive depending on whether it's there or not: У меня́ есть маши́на, but У меня́ нет маши́ны.

Short answers use the bare есть or нет:

:::ex
У тебя́ есть ру́чка? — Есть. | Do you have a pen? — I do.
У вас есть молоко́? — Нет. | Do you have milk? — No.
У кого́ есть вопро́с? | Who has a question?
:::

### When есть is dropped

You will often see у меня́ with no есть. The rule of thumb: **есть asserts existence.** Use it when the point is whether the thing exists at all. Leave it out when the existence is taken for granted and you're really saying something else about it.

Leave it out when you're describing what the thing is like, or how much of it there is:

:::ex
У неё зелёные глаза́. | She has green eyes. | of course she has eyes; the news is the color
У тебя́ краси́вое и́мя. | You have a beautiful name.
У них больша́я кварти́ра. | They have a big apartment.
У меня́ мно́го рабо́ты. | I have a lot of work.
:::

Leave it out for illnesses, and for things that are happening to you rather than things you possess:

:::ex
У меня́ грипп. | I have the flu.
У меня́ боли́т голова́. | I have a headache. | literally "by me the head hurts"
У меня́ сего́дня встре́ча. | I have a meeting today.
У меня́ вопро́с. | I have a question.
У меня́ иде́я! | I've got an idea!
:::

Compare У тебя́ есть маши́на? (Do you own a car at all?) with У тебя́ но́вая маши́на? (Is your car new? / You've got a new car?).

### У for "at someone's place"

The basic meaning of у is "by, next to", and with a person it means "at that person's place":

:::ex
Стол стои́т у окна́. | The table stands by the window.
Вчера́ я был у врача́. | I was at the doctor's yesterday.
Мы весь день бы́ли у ба́бушки. | We were at Grandma's all day.
Он живёт у сестры́. | He's living at his sister's.
:::

And **у нас**, **у вас** very often mean "at our place, in our family, at our company, in our country", whichever "us" is relevant:

:::ex
У нас в о́фисе хо́лодно. | It's cold in our office.
Как у вас в Аме́рике? | How are things over there in America?
:::

:::aside The verb име́ть
A verb име́ть (to have) does exist, with a normal accusative object. It lives mostly in formal writing and fixed abstract phrases: име́ть пра́во (to have the right), име́ть значе́ние (to matter, literally "to have significance"). For owning a car, a sister or a headache, у меня́ is the only natural choice.
:::

## Job four: some, a lot, a cup of

Any expression of amount is followed by the genitive: an amount *of* something.

:::ex
мно́го рабо́ты | a lot of work
ма́ло вре́мени | little time
немно́го молока́ | a little milk
ско́лько са́хара? | how much sugar?
:::

So are containers and measures:

:::ex
буты́лка воды́ | a bottle of water
ча́шка ча́я | a cup of tea
стака́н молока́ | a glass of milk
кусо́к хле́ба | a piece of bread
таре́лка су́па | a bowl of soup
килогра́мм мя́са | a kilogram of meat
:::

:::ex
У меня́ сего́дня мно́го рабо́ты и ма́ло вре́мени. | I have a lot of work and little time today.
Ско́лько са́хара? — Немно́го. | How much sugar? — A little.
Я хочу́ ча́шку ко́фе и кусо́к то́рта. | I want a cup of coffee and a piece of cake.
О́ля купи́ла буты́лку вина́. | Olya bought a bottle of wine.
:::

In the last two, the container is a direct object and goes into the accusative (ча́шку, буты́лку), while its contents stay in the genitive.

The genitive can even mean "some" all by itself, with no quantity word. As the object of verbs like "want", "buy" and "give", a substance in the genitive means an unspecified amount of it:

:::ex
Хо́чешь ча́я? | Do you want some tea?
Я купи́л хле́ба и молока́. | I bought some bread and milk.
Я купи́л хлеб. | I bought the bread. | accusative: the whole, specific item
:::

:::warn Countable things need the plural
Мно́го рабо́ты, ма́ло вре́мени, ско́лько воды́: all of these work because work, time and water come in amounts, not in units. As soon as you want "many books", "few people" or "how many days", you need the genitive *plural*, which is coming in [cases in the plural](#s-03-07). One plural form is too useful to wait for: де́ньги (money) is a plural-only noun, and its genitive is **де́нег**. So: мно́го де́нег, ма́ло де́нег, ско́лько де́нег, and the timeless У меня́ нет де́нег.
:::

### After two, three and four

Here is a strange and important fact: after the numbers **два** (or **две** with feminine nouns), **три** and **четы́ре**, the noun goes into the genitive *singular*.

:::ex
два бра́та | two brothers | literally "two of-brother"
две сестры́ | two sisters
три дня | three days
четы́ре неде́ли | four weeks
два часа́ | two hours
:::

After оди́н (one) the noun stays nominative, and from five upward it takes the genitive plural. The whole counting system is laid out in [numbers and counting](#s-05-07); for now, enjoy the fact that you can already count to four. (One stress quirk: the genitive of час is normally ча́са, as in о́коло ча́са, but after these numbers it is часа́.)

:::ex
У меня́ два бра́та и одна́ сестра́. | I have two brothers and one sister.
Мы жи́ли там три го́да. | We lived there for three years.
:::

## Prepositions that take the genitive

More prepositions take the genitive than any other case. Here are the ones you need first. Many of them share an idea of *source* or *separation*: from, out of, without.

| Preposition | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| из | out of, from (inside) | из Москвы́, из до́ма |
| с | from, off (a surface or event) | с рабо́ты, со стола́ |
| от | from (a person or point), away from | от ма́мы, от до́ма |
| до | until, up to, as far as, before | до пя́тницы, до це́нтра |
| без | without | без са́хара |
| для | for (the benefit or purpose of) | для тебя́ |
| по́сле | after | по́сле рабо́ты |
| о́коло | near; approximately | о́коло до́ма, о́коло ча́са |
| у | by, at; at someone's place | у окна́, у врача́ |
| кро́ме | except, besides | кро́ме меня́ |
| из-за | because of; from behind | из-за дождя́ |
| вме́сто | instead of | вме́сто са́хара |
| напро́тив | opposite, across from | напро́тив ба́нка |
| во вре́мя | during | во вре́мя уро́ка |

:::ex
Я из Аме́рики, а моя́ жена́ из Росси́и. | I'm from America, and my wife is from Russia.
Отку́да ты? — Из Кана́ды. | Where are you from? — Canada.
Э́то письмо́ от ба́бушки. | This letter is from Grandma.
Я рабо́таю до ве́чера. | I'm working until evening.
Мне ко́фе без молока́, пожа́луйста. | Coffee without milk for me, please. | мне, "for me", is a dative form; see the next section
Э́то пода́рок для ма́мы. | This is a present for Mom.
Что ты де́лаешь по́сле рабо́ты? | What are you doing after work?
Мы живём о́коло па́рка, напро́тив шко́лы. | We live near the park, across from the school.
Все бы́ли там, кро́ме Ди́мы. | Everyone was there except Dima.
Из-за дождя́ мы сиде́ли до́ма. | Because of the rain we stayed home.
Я без тебя́ не могу́. | I can't manage without you.
:::

Some notes on the trickier ones.

**Three words for "from".** Russian splits "from" three ways, and each one is the reverse of a preposition you know. If you are *in* a place with в, you come *out of* it with **из**. If you are *at* a place with на, you come from it with **с**. If you are at a *person's* with у, you come from them with **от**.

:::ex
Я в шко́ле. → Я иду́ из шко́лы. | I'm at school. → I'm coming from school.
Я на рабо́те. → Я иду́ с рабо́ты. | I'm at work. → I'm coming from work.
Я у врача́. → Я иду́ от врача́. | I'm at the doctor's. → I'm coming from the doctor's.
:::

The pairing is strict: a на-noun always takes с, never из. The full system is in [where, where to, where from](#s-05-01).

**С and от also mean "since" and "from" in time**, and pair up with до: с утра́ до ве́чера (from morning till evening), с понеде́льника (since Monday), с де́тства (since childhood). The everyday goodbye **до свида́ния** is literally "until the meeting"; likewise до за́втра (see you tomorrow) and до встре́чи (see you).

**Из-за** names a cause, almost always of something bad: из-за пого́ды (because of the weather), из-за тебя́ (because of you: you're being blamed).

**От** shows up in some unexpected "of" and "for" phrases: ключ от маши́ны (the key to the car), лека́рство от гри́ппа (medicine for the flu), недалеко́ от це́нтра (not far from the center).

The full list of genitive prepositions is in [prepositions and the cases they take](#s-09-05).

## Verbs and wishes

A few verbs take a genitive object. The most common is **боя́ться** (to be afraid of):

:::ex
Я бою́сь темноты́. | I'm afraid of the dark.
Она́ не бои́тся рабо́ты. | She's not afraid of work.
:::

Another is жела́ть (to wish someone something): you wish *of* luck, so to speak. The verb itself is usually left out, which explains why a whole family of everyday formulas are bare genitives:

:::ex
Уда́чи! | Good luck!
Споко́йной но́чи! | Good night!
Прия́тного аппети́та! | Enjoy your meal!
Хоро́шего дня! | Have a nice day!
Всего́ хоро́шего! | All the best!
Счастли́вого пути́! | Have a good trip!
:::

Each one is short for "(I wish you) a peaceful night", "(I wish you) a pleasant appetite" and so on.

## Genitive after "not"

You saw that нет puts the missing thing in the genitive. The same instinct sometimes reaches the direct object of a negated verb: where an affirmative sentence has an accusative, the negative may have a genitive.

:::ex
Я не ви́жу смы́сла. | I don't see the point.
Э́то не име́ет значе́ния. | It doesn't matter.
Он не зна́ет сло́ва «нет». | He doesn't know the word "no".
:::

This used to be close to a rule. In modern Russian it's a tendency: the genitive is normal with abstract nouns and in set phrases like the ones above, while concrete, specific objects usually stay accusative (Я не чита́л э́ту кни́гу, Я не зна́ю Ма́шу). When in doubt, use the accusative; it is rarely wrong. There's more in [negation](#s-06-04).

## Dates

In a date, the month is in the genitive: "the first *of* May".

:::ex
Сего́дня пе́рвое ма́я. | Today is the first of May.
За́втра второ́е января́. | Tomorrow is the second of January.
:::

The genitives of the months are января́, февраля́, ма́рта, апре́ля, ма́я, ию́ня, ию́ля, а́вгуста, сентября́, октября́, ноября́, декабря́. To say that something happens *on* a date, the number goes into the genitive too (пе́рвого ма́я, "on the first of May"); see [time, dates and age](#s-05-08).

## Putting it together

:::dialogue
А: У нас есть молоко́? | Do we have milk?
Б: Нет, молока́ нет. Есть буты́лка со́ка и немно́го сы́ра. | No, there's no milk. There's a bottle of juice and a little cheese.
А: А хлеб? | And bread?
Б: Хле́ба то́же нет. У меня́ [[ns:не́ было]] вре́мени: я весь день была́ у ма́мы. | No bread either. I didn't have time: I was at Mom's all day.
А: Ла́дно. По́сле рабо́ты я иду́ в магази́н. Ско́лько молока́? | OK. I'm going to the store after work. How much milk?
Б: Два ли́тра. И ко́фе для Ди́мы — без кофеи́на. | Two liters. And coffee for Dima: decaf.
А: У Ди́мы нет вку́са. | Dima has no taste.
:::

:::summary
- Nouns: m. and n. **-а / -я**; f. **-ы / -и** (и for soft stems and -ь nouns, and after г, к, х, ж, ч, ш, щ).
- Adjectives: m. and n. **-ого / -его**; f. **-ой / -ей**. Моего́, мое́й; э́того, э́той.
- "Of": кни́га бра́та. The owner comes second.
- Absence: нет / [[ns:не́ было]] / не бу́дет + genitive.
- "Have": у + genitive owner, есть, nominative thing. "Don't have": у + genitive, нет, genitive thing.
- Quantity: мно́го, ма́ло, ско́лько, containers, and the numbers 2–4 (genitive singular).
- Prepositions: из, с, от, до, без, для, по́сле, о́коло, у, кро́ме, из-за, вме́сто, напро́тив.
:::
