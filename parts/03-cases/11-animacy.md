# Animacy: who versus what

English sorts the world into "who" and "what" only when it asks a question. Russian does it for every noun in the dictionary. Each noun is either a **кто** (who) or a **что** (what), and that label is as much a part of the word as its gender. Grammars call the two groups **animate** and **inanimate**; the Russian terms are одушевлённый and неодушевлённый, literally "ensouled" and "un-ensouled".

You have met this in pieces: the masculine "living beings" of [the accusative](#s-03-02), the plural rule in [cases in the plural](#s-03-07), the remark about кто and что in [the pronoun section](#s-03-08). They are one system, and a small one.

## One category, one place

Animacy does one thing. It decides what the **accusative** looks like:

- an animate noun borrows its accusative from the **genitive**;
- an inanimate noun borrows its accusative from the **nominative**.

In the other five cases the two kinds of noun behave identically. So you never have to think about animacy unless a noun is a direct object, or follows a preposition that takes the accusative (Он смо́трит на бра́та).

Whatever agrees with the noun follows it. Adjectives, possessives, э́тот, весь and оди́н all take the genitive form beside an animate noun and the nominative form beside an inanimate one:

:::ex
Я ви́жу **но́вого студе́нта**. | I see the new student.
Я ви́жу **но́вый стол**. | I see the new table.
Ты зна́ешь **э́тих люде́й**? | Do you know these people?
Ты зна́ешь **э́ти дома́**? | Do you know these buildings?
Я зна́ю здесь **всех сосе́дей**. | I know all the neighbors here.
Я чита́л **все его́ кни́ги**. | I've read all his books.
Я ви́жу **одного́ студе́нта** и **оди́н стол**. | I see one student and one table.
:::

The same goes for кото́рый, the "who, which, that" of [relative clauses](#s-06-03). It takes its animacy from the noun it points back to:

:::ex
Э́то челове́к, **кото́рого** я зна́ю. | This is a person I know.
Э́то фильм, **кото́рый** я смотре́л. | This is a movie I've seen.
Э́то лю́ди, **кото́рых** я зна́ю. | These are people I know.
Э́то кни́ги, **кото́рые** я чита́л. | These are books I've read.
:::

## Where it shows and where it doesn't

The rule applies in two zones.

**The masculine singular**, for nouns ending in a consonant, -й or -ь:

:::ex
Я встре́тил бра́та. | I ran into my brother.
Мы ждём учи́теля. | We're waiting for the teacher.
Я купи́л журна́л. | I bought a magazine.
:::

**The plural, in every gender.** This is the part learners forget, because in the singular feminine and neuter nouns never showed anything:

:::ex
Я ви́жу бра́тьев, сестёр и подру́г. | I see my brothers, sisters and friends.
Она́ лю́бит дете́й и живо́тных. | She loves children and animals.
Он рису́ет чудо́вищ. | He draws monsters.
Я ви́жу столы́, кни́ги и о́кна. | I see tables, books and windows.
:::

Everywhere else, animacy is there but invisible:

- **Feminine nouns in -а and -я** have an accusative of their own, -у and -ю, and use it for people and things alike: сестру́, соба́ку, кни́гу.
- **Feminine nouns in -ь** keep the nominative form whatever they name: Я ви́жу мать. Я ви́жу дверь. Only in the plural do they part ways: матере́й, but две́ри.
- **Neuter nouns** keep the nominative form in the singular even on the rare occasions when they name a living thing: Я ви́жу чудо́вище. Я ви́жу э́то живо́тное. The plural tells the truth: чудо́вищ, живо́тных.
- **Masculine nouns in -а and -я** (па́па, дя́дя, де́душка, мужчи́на, Са́ша) decline like the feminine nouns they resemble: Я ви́жу па́пу, дя́дю. But they are still masculine and animate, and the words around them say so: Я ви́жу **моего́** па́пу. Ты зна́ешь **э́того** мужчи́ну? In the plural the noun shows it too: Я зна́ю э́тих мужчи́н.

## Why Russian bothers

Russian word order is free because the endings say who does what to whom. Masculine nouns have a weak spot there: their nominative has no ending, and their own accusative has none either. If Russian said «Брат ви́дит друг», you could not tell the one seeing from the one seen, and you could not move the words around.

With things this hardly matters. In Брат ви́дит стол nobody suspects the table of doing the seeing. With people and animals, either one could be the subject, so for exactly those nouns the language reaches for a form that cannot be mistaken for a subject:

:::ex
Брат ви́дит дру́га. | The brother sees his friend.
Дру́га ви́дит брат. | It's the brother who sees the friend. | same meaning, different emphasis
Брат ви́дит друзе́й. | The brother sees his friends.
:::

Feminine nouns in -а never needed the repair in the singular, since -у already marks the object. In the plural no gender has a separate accusative, so there the rule covers everyone. And in the one corner the repair doesn't reach, feminine nouns in -ь in the singular, word order has to do the job, as you saw with Мать лю́бит дочь in [word order](#s-03-09).

## What counts as animate

For daily purposes the answer is short: **people and animals are animate; everything else is not.** "Animals" means all of them, down to the smallest and least lovable:

:::ex
Я уби́л комара́. | I killed a mosquito.
Де́ти лю́бят соба́к и ко́шек. | Kids love dogs and cats.
Мы лови́ли ра́ков. | We were catching crayfish.
:::

That is all you need. But the line is drawn by grammar, not biology, and it wobbles in places. Treat the rest of this list as curiosities.

**The dead.** Мертве́ц and поко́йник are animate; труп is not. A dead *person* is still a "who"; a body is a "what".

:::ex
Он ви́дел мертвеца́. | He saw a dead man.
Поли́ция нашла́ труп. | The police found a body.
:::

**Groups.** A collective noun is inanimate even when it consists entirely of people: наро́д, толпа́, отря́д, гру́ппа, кома́нда (team), класс, молодёжь. The group is a thing; its members are people.

:::ex
Учи́тель зна́ет **весь класс**. | The teacher knows the whole class.
Учи́тель зна́ет **всех ученико́в**. | The teacher knows all the pupils.
:::

**Things shaped like people.** Ку́кла is normally animate (Ма́ша лю́бит ку́кол), and so is a snowman: Де́ти слепи́ли снеговика́. Ро́бот goes both ways. A humanlike one is animate; a machine on a factory floor is often not; Ди́ма собра́л ро́бота and Ди́ма собра́л ро́бот are both said.

**Game pieces.** Chess pieces and playing cards with masculine names are animate, which makes a chess commentary sound mildly violent:

:::ex
Он взял моего́ ферзя́. | He took my queen.
Она́ поби́ла туза́. | She beat the ace.
:::

**Imaginary beings.** Angels, mermaids, ghosts and gods are grammatically alive: Де́ти рису́ют а́нгелов и руса́лок.

**Plants** are inanimate, however alive they are: Я купи́л цветы́.

**Microbes.** Here even native speakers split. Biology textbooks tend to write изуча́ть бакте́рий, treating them as creatures. Everyone else mostly says Антибио́тики убива́ют бакте́рии. Both are accepted.

**Dinner.** A fish in the water is animate: корми́ть рыб (to feed the fish). Seafood on a plate is less sure of itself. Careful usage keeps it animate (Он ел у́стриц), and casual speech often doesn't (Он ел у́стрицы). The singular ры́бу gives nothing away, being feminine.

**Titles and kites.** A film or book named after its hero is treated like the hero: Ты смотре́л «Термина́тора»? Я чита́л «Га́млета». And the kite, **возду́шный змей** (literally "air serpent"), keeps its serpent's grammar: Де́ти запуска́ют возду́шного зме́я.

:::note В го́сти
A few fixed phrases preserve an older state of the language, in which the plural accusative of people still looked like the nominative. The one you need is **в го́сти**: Мы идём в го́сти (We're going to visit someone), literally "into guests". By today's rule it would be в госте́й, which nobody says.
:::

## Animacy and numbers

Counting is taught in [numbers and counting](#s-05-07), and the way numbers themselves decline in [declining numbers](#s-08-05); this is only a signpost.

A number phrase standing as a direct object obeys the same rule, but only for the small numbers. With **2, 3 and 4**, and with the "both" and group words о́ба, дво́е, тро́е, an animate object puts the whole phrase into the genitive plural, while an inanimate one looks just like the nominative:

:::ex
Я ви́жу **двух студе́нтов**. | I see two students.
Я зна́ю **трёх сестёр**. | I know three sisters.
Я ви́жу **два стола́** и **две кни́ги**. | I see two tables and two books.
:::

From **5** up, and in compound numbers ending in 2, 3 or 4, the distinction is switched off and the phrase stays as it would be in the nominative:

:::ex
Я ви́жу **пять студе́нтов**. | I see five students.
Я ви́жу **два́дцать два студе́нта**. | I see twenty-two students.
:::

## Кто and что

**Кто** is animate, so its accusative is the genitive **кого́**. **Что** is inanimate, so its accusative is the nominative **что** (its genitive is чего́).

:::ex
Кого́ ты ви́дишь? — Бра́та. | Who do you see? — My brother.
Что ты ви́дишь? — Стол. | What do you see? — A table.
:::

The choice between кто and что follows the grammar, not English habit. In particular, animals are кто. Pointing at something furry in a tree, an English speaker asks "What's that?"; a Russian asks who it is:

:::ex
Кто э́то? — Э́то бе́лка. | What's that? — It's a squirrel.
Что э́то? — Э́то но́вый телефо́н. | What's that? — It's a new phone.
:::

The personal pronouns have given up the distinction. Меня́, тебя́, нас and вас can only be people, and **его́, её, их** serve as both accusative and genitive for people and things alike:

:::ex
Где Ди́ма? Я его́ не ви́жу. | Where's Dima? I don't see him.
Где мой телефо́н? Я его́ не ви́жу. | Where's my phone? I don't see it.
:::
