# Being, having and existing

English leans on two verbs harder than on any others. "Be" says what something is (*she's a doctor*), what it's like (*she's tired*), where it is (*she's at home*) and that it exists (*there's a problem*). "Have" covers owning, family, illnesses and appointments. Russian has no present-tense "is" and no everyday "have", so it cuts the same territory along different lines:

- **Saying what something is or is like**: nothing in the present, **был** and **бу́дет** otherwise.
- **Saying where something is**: the same, though Russian often prefers a more specific verb.
- **Saying that something exists**: **есть**.
- **Saying that something is absent**: **нет** and the genitive.
- **Having**: not a separate idea at all. It is existence, located *by* a person.

You have met most of these pieces in Parts 2 and 3. This section puts them on one map and fills the gaps.

:::vocab
есть | there is, there are | never changes; not the verb есть "to eat"
нет | there is no | + gen.
быва́ть | to be (from time to time), to visit, to happen | impf.
находи́ться | to be located | a verb in -ся, covered in Part 4
явля́ться | to be (formal) | + instr.; a verb in -ся
станови́ться / стать | to become | + instr.; past стал, ста́ло, ста́ла, ста́ли
име́ть | to have (formal, abstract) | име́ю, име́ешь, име́ют
пра́во | a right
значе́ние | meaning, significance
возмо́жность | opportunity, possibility | f.
банкома́т | ATM
заря́дка | charger
:::

## X is Y: what something is

In the present, you put the two halves side by side and say nothing in between. In writing, a dash separates two nouns, as you saw in [Sentences without "to be"](#s-02-01).

:::ex
Я врач. | I'm a doctor.
Ди́ма — мой брат. | Dima is my brother.
Фильм интере́сный. | The film is interesting.
Она́ не студе́нтка. | She isn't a student.
:::

In the past and future a verb appears: **был, бы́ло, была́, бы́ли** and **бу́ду, бу́дешь, бу́дет…** And once the verb is there, the noun after it normally goes into the instrumental, the "role" case from [The instrumental](#s-03-06):

:::ex
Он врач. | He's a doctor.
Он был врач**о́м**. | He was a doctor.
Он бу́дет врач**о́м**. | He's going to be a doctor.
А́ня была́ мое́й учи́тельницей. | Anya was my teacher.
Он [[ns:не́ был]] дире́ктором. | He wasn't the director.
:::

The nominative after **был** is not an error. It presents the description as what the person simply was, by nature, where the instrumental presents it as a role held for a time: **Он был хоро́ший челове́к** (He was a good man). That is a tendency, and for jobs and roles the instrumental is the safe choice. After **бу́дет** and after **стать** (to become) it is the instrumental practically always: **Са́ша стал врачо́м** (Sasha became a doctor).

With adjectives, both cases are in everyday use after **был**, and the nominative is very common in speech:

:::ex
Фильм был интере́сный. | The film was interesting.
Когда́ я была́ ма́ленькой, мы жи́ли у ба́бушки. | When I was little, we lived at Grandma's. | a woman speaking
Экза́мен бу́дет тру́дным. | The exam is going to be hard.
:::

### Э́то as the hinge

When the thing you're identifying isn't named, **э́то** stands in for it: **Э́то мой брат**. In the past the verb agrees with the noun that follows, not with **э́то** (**Э́то была́ О́ля**), as explained in [The past tense](#s-02-07).

**Э́то** can also sit between two nouns, after the dash, where English has "is". It is very common in speech and in definitions:

:::ex
Ди́ма — э́то мой брат. | Dima is my brother. | "Dima, that's my brother"
Борщ — э́то суп. | Borscht is a soup.
:::

## X is somewhere

Location works the same way: nothing in the present, **был** and **бу́дет** elsewhere. The place is an adverb or a preposition with its case, mostly в or на with [the prepositional](#s-03-03).

:::ex
Ма́ша до́ма. | Masha is at home.
Ма́ша была́ до́ма. | Masha was at home.
Ма́ша бу́дет до́ма. | Masha will be at home.
Ключи́ в су́мке. | The keys are in the bag.
:::

That is always correct. But where English is content with "is", Russian very often says *how* a thing is there: standing, lying, hanging.

:::ex
Маши́на стои́т у до́ма. | The car is outside the building. | "stands"
Ключи́ лежа́т на столе́. | The keys are on the table. | "lie"
Карти́на виси́т на стене́. | The picture is on the wall. | "hangs"
:::

For buildings, cities and anything else with a fixed address, careful Russian uses **находи́ться**: **Музе́й нахо́дится в це́нтре** (The museum is in the center). All of these are taught in [Standing, lying, sitting, putting](#s-05-05).

## There is: есть

To say that something exists, or is available, Russian uses **есть**. It was once the "is" form of **быть**, and it has fossilized: it never changes, whether one thing exists or twenty.

:::ex
В го́роде есть метро́. | The city has a subway. / There's a subway in the city.
Здесь есть банкома́т? — Есть. | Is there an ATM here? — There is.
В холоди́льнике есть молоко́ и я́йца. | There's milk and eggs in the fridge.
Есть вопро́сы? | Are there any questions?
:::

**Есть** asserts existence, so it is used when existence is the question: is there one or not? When you take the things for granted and are listing or counting what a place contains, it drops out:

:::ex
В го́роде есть университе́т. | There's a university in the city. | it has one
В го́роде два университе́та. | There are two universities in the city. | how many
В ко́мнате стол, крова́ть и шкаф. | In the room there's a table, a bed and a wardrobe. | describing the room
:::

In the past and future there is no such choice. **Есть** and nothing both become **был** or **бу́дет**, agreeing with the thing that exists:

:::ex
Ра́ньше здесь был магази́н. | There used to be a store here.
За́втра бу́дет конце́рт. | There's a concert tomorrow.
:::

### "There is a book" and "the book is there"

English separates "There's a book on the table" from "The book is on the table" with "there" and with articles. Russian has neither, so the whole difference is the order of the words. The principle, set out in [Word order](#s-03-09), is that what's known comes first and the news comes last.

:::ex
На столе́ кни́га. | There's a book on the table. | place known, the book is news
Кни́га на столе́. | The book is on the table. | book known, the place is news
Здесь был магази́н. | There was a store here.
Магази́н был здесь. | The store was here.
:::

So "there is" in Russian is **place first, thing last**, with **есть** in between only if you need to insist that the thing exists.

## There isn't: нет

Existence has its own negative, and it is not **не**. It is **нет**, with the missing thing in the genitive, as in [The genitive](#s-03-04). In the past it is **[[ns:не́ было]]**, in the future **не бу́дет**: always neuter singular, because a sentence like this has no nominative subject for the verb to agree with.

:::ex
Здесь нет банкома́та. | There's no ATM here.
Вчера́ [[ns:не́ было]] интерне́та. | There was no internet yesterday.
Де́нег нет. | There's no money.
За́втра уро́ков не бу́дет. | There won't be any classes tomorrow.
:::

Even with a plural the verb stays put: **Де́нег [[ns:не́ было]]**.

### Нет or не?

"Masha isn't home" has two Russian versions:

:::ex
Ма́ши нет до́ма. | Masha isn't home. | she's absent: "of Masha there is none at home"
Ма́ша не до́ма, а на рабо́те. | Masha isn't at home, she's at work. | she's somewhere else
:::

**Нет** + genitive reports an absence. It is what you say when someone asks for Masha and she isn't there. **Не** negates only the word after it, so **Ма́ша не до́ма** denies one location, usually on the way to naming another. If you just mean "she's out", say **Ма́ши нет**.

The past has the same split, with a twist:

:::ex
Его́ [[ns:не́ было]] на рабо́те. | He wasn't at work. | he was missing from the office
Он [[ns:не́ был]] в Пари́же. | He hasn't been to Paris. | he has never gone there
О́ля не была́ на рабо́те. | Olya didn't go to work.
:::

The first sentence looks at the place and what was missing from it. The others look at the person and what he or she did. In the past, **быть** with a person as its subject often means "to go somewhere and come back", "to visit" (more in [Going](#s-05-02)), and **[[ns:не́ был]]** then means that no such visit happened. So for "I've never been there", it is **Я там [[ns:не́ был]]**, with a nominative **я**; for "I wasn't in when you called", it is **Меня́ [[ns:не́ было]]**.

In the future both are heard with little difference: **Меня́ за́втра не бу́дет** and **Я за́втра не бу́ду на рабо́те** each say you'll be out.

## Having

Russian says "I have a car" as **У меня́ есть маши́на**: "by me there is a car". It is an existence sentence. **У меня́** is the place, exactly like **в го́роде** above, and the car is the thing that exists there. That explains everything else about the construction:

- The owner follows **у** in the genitive: **у меня́, у тебя́, у него́, у неё, у нас, у вас, у них, у Ма́ши**.
- The thing owned is the subject, in the **nominative**. Nobody is doing anything to it.
- **Есть**, **был**, **бу́дет** and **нет** all behave as they do in any other existence sentence.

### Есть or no есть

Keep **есть** when the news is that the thing exists at all:

:::ex
У тебя́ есть заря́дка? — Есть. | Do you have a charger? — I do.
У Ми́ши есть соба́ка. | Misha has a dog.
У вас есть де́ти? | Do you have children?
:::

Drop it when the thing is taken for granted and you are saying what it's like or how many there are:

:::ex
У неё голубы́е глаза́. | She has blue eyes.
У меня́ два бра́та. | I have two brothers.
У них больша́я кварти́ра. | They have a big apartment.
:::

**У неё есть голубы́е глаза́** would mean that somewhere among her possessions there are blue eyes. And drop it for illnesses, events and anything that is happening to you, not owned by you:

:::ex
У меня́ грипп. | I have the flu.
У Ива́на температу́ра. | Ivan has a fever.
У нас сего́дня го́сти. | We have guests today.
:::

### Past, future and negative

In the past and future the difference between **есть** and nothing disappears, as it did above. The verb agrees with the thing possessed:

:::ex
У меня́ был кот. | I had a cat.
У меня́ была́ соба́ка. | I had a dog.
У неё бы́ли голубы́е глаза́. | She had blue eyes.
У нас бу́дет ребёнок. | We're going to have a baby.
У нас бу́дут го́сти. | We'll have guests.
:::

One wrinkle: when the thing possessed is a quantity (a number, **мно́го**, **ма́ло**), the past is usually the neuter **бы́ло**: **У нас бы́ло мно́го рабо́ты** (We had a lot of work), **У него́ бы́ло два бра́та** (He had two brothers). More in [Numbers and counting](#s-05-07).

Not having is absence:

:::ex
У меня́ нет маши́ны. | I don't have a car.
У нас [[ns:не́ было]] вре́мени. | We didn't have time.
У тебя́ не бу́дет пробле́м. | You won't have any problems.
:::

:::warn Two mistakes English pushes you into
The thing you have is never accusative: **У меня́ есть маши́на**, not "маши́ну". And the verb agrees with the thing, not with you: a man who had a dog says **У меня́ была́ соба́ка**.
:::

### У меня́ beyond owning

Since **у** + person really names a location, it reaches well past possession. It means "at someone's place": **Мы бы́ли у ба́бушки** (We were at Grandma's). It also marks the person something is happening to, where English would use "my":

:::ex
У меня́ боли́т голова́. | I have a headache. | "by me the head hurts"
У меня́ ма́ма врач. | My mom's a doctor.
У него́ ско́ро экза́мен. | He has an exam soon.
:::

And "the car is with me", as opposed to "I have a car", is the same words in the other order: **Маши́на у меня́**.

### Wholes and their parts: в + prepositional

When the "owner" is a thing and what it "has" are its parts or contents, Russian usually locates them *in* it:

:::ex
В кварти́ре три ко́мнаты. | The apartment has three rooms.
В неде́ле семь дней. | A week has seven days.
В сло́ве «метро́» пять букв. | The word "метро́" has five letters.
:::

### Име́ть

There is a verb "to have", **име́ть**, with an ordinary accusative object. It belongs to formal writing and to abstract things, above all in a handful of fixed phrases that you will hear every day:

:::ex
Ка́ждый челове́к име́ет пра́во на о́тдых. | Everyone has the right to rest.
Что ты име́ешь в виду́? | What do you mean? | "what do you have in view?"
Э́то не име́ет значе́ния. | It doesn't matter. | "has no significance"; genitive after не
Мы име́ем возмо́жность рабо́тать до́ма. | We have the opportunity to work from home. | formal
:::

For possessions, relatives and illnesses it is the wrong tool. **Я име́ю маши́ну** is grammatical, and it sounds like a customs form or a bad translation. With a person as its object the verb also has a crude sexual sense, so build "I have a sister" with **у меня́**.

## The other "be" verbs

### Быва́ть: to be, now and then

**Быва́ть** is **быть** spread over many occasions. It conjugates like **чита́ть** (**быва́ю, быва́ешь, быва́ет…**) and covers "is" in the sense of "tends to be" or "is from time to time".

With a place, it means to be there regularly, to visit:

:::ex
Я ча́сто быва́ю в Москве́. | I'm often in Moscow.
Мы ре́дко быва́ем у них. | We rarely go to see them.
Ты быва́л в Пари́же? | Have you ever been to Paris?
:::

With a description, "can be", "is sometimes":

:::ex
Зимо́й здесь быва́ет о́чень хо́лодно. | It can get very cold here in winter.
Он быва́ет гру́бым. | He can be rude.
:::

And on its own it is how Russian says that things of some kind exist or happen:

:::ex
Так быва́ет. | It happens. / That's how it goes. | a plain Быва́ет is a sympathetic reply to bad news
Быва́ет, что по́езд опа́здывает. | Sometimes the train is late. | "it happens that…"
Чуде́с не быва́ет. | There's no such thing as miracles. | genitive, as after нет
:::

### Явля́ться: the formal "is"

Official, scientific and news language fills the gap in the present tense with **явля́ться** and the instrumental. You need to read it; in conversation it sounds like a press release.

:::ex
Ру́сский язы́к явля́ется госуда́рственным языко́м Росси́и. | Russian is the state language of Russia.
Э́то не явля́ется пробле́мой. | This does not constitute a problem.
:::

### Есть and суть as "is"

**Есть** survives as a real "is" in two corners. One is formulas of the type "X is X", said with a shrug. The other is bookish definitions.

:::ex
Зако́н есть зако́н. | The law is the law.
Что есть и́стина? | What is truth?
:::

The old "they are" form, **суть**, turns up almost only in nineteenth-century prose, very pompous modern writing and a fixed phrase or two. In today's Russian **суть** is a noun meaning "the essence, the point".

### Станови́ться / стать: to become

Changing into something is **станови́ться / стать** with the instrumental. You'll meet the past **стал** constantly; the choice between the two verbs is a matter of aspect, which starts in [Part 4](#s-04-01).

:::ex
Са́ша стал врачо́м. | Sasha became a doctor.
Ста́ло хо́лодно. | It's gotten cold.
Стано́вится темно́. | It's getting dark.
:::

| X is (adjective) | Фильм интере́сный. | Фильм был интере́сный. | Фильм бу́дет интере́сным. |
| X is at a place | Ма́ша до́ма. | Ма́ша была́ до́ма. | Ма́ша бу́дет до́ма. |

| I don't have an X | У меня́ нет маши́ны. | У меня́ [[ns:не́ было]] маши́ны. | У меня́ не бу́дет маши́ны. |
