# Can, must, need, may

English has a tidy little set of verbs for possibility and obligation: can, could, may, might, must, should, have to, need to. Russian has nothing like a matching set. Instead it has a mixed bag: a couple of real verbs, a few unchanging words that take the dative, and one adjective. They slice up the territory differently, so "can" might be any of three Russian words, and one Russian word might come out as "can't", "mustn't" or "shouldn't".

Rather than hunting for the translation of each English verb, learn what each Russian tool is *for*. At the end there's a table that maps the English ones onto them.

:::vocab
мочь / смочь | to be able, can | могу́, мо́жешь… мо́гут; past мог, могла́
уме́ть | to know how to | уме́ю, уме́ешь
мо́жно | it's allowed; it's possible | + dative
нельзя́ | it's not allowed; it's impossible | + dative
на́до, ну́жно | it's necessary, need to | + dative
до́лжен | must, ought to, is supposed to | agrees with the subject
приходи́ться / прийти́сь | to have to (because of circumstances) | impersonal, + dative
ну́жен | needed | agrees with the thing needed
сто́ит | it's worth, it's a good idea to
мо́жет быть | maybe
наве́рное | probably
пла́вать | to swim
сове́т | advice, a piece of advice | m.
обяза́тельно | definitely, without fail; не обяза́тельно = not necessarily
:::

## Мочь and уме́ть: two kinds of "can"

**Уме́ть** is "can" in the sense of a learned skill: you know how. **Мочь** is "can" in the sense that nothing prevents you: you have the physical ability, the time, the opportunity, the permission.

:::ex
Я **уме́ю** пла́вать, но сего́дня не **могу́**: у меня́ боли́т плечо́. | I can swim, but today I can't: my shoulder hurts.
Ты **уме́ешь** води́ть маши́ну? | Can you drive?
Она́ **уме́ет** слу́шать. | She knows how to listen.
Ты **мо́жешь** мне помо́чь? | Can you help me?
Я не **могу́** прийти́ за́втра. | I can't come tomorrow.
:::

Мочь is the irregular -чь verb from the [conjugation section](#s-04-04): могу́, мо́жешь, мо́жет, мо́жем, мо́жете, мо́гут; past мог, могла́, могло́, могли́. Its perfective partner **смочь** is what you use for the future (there is no бу́ду мочь) and for managing, or failing, on one particular occasion.

:::ex
Ты **смо́жешь** прийти́ в суббо́ту? | Will you be able to come on Saturday?
Я не **смог** откры́ть файл. | I couldn't open the file. | I tried, it didn't work
В де́тстве я **мог** спать по двена́дцать часо́в. | As a child I could sleep twelve hours at a time. | general ability
:::

Мочь also covers "may" and "might" as a guess about what's possible:

:::ex
Он **мо́жет** опозда́ть. | He may be late.
Она́ **мо́жет** не знать. | She might not know.
:::

## Мо́жно and нельзя́: allowed and not allowed

**Мо́жно** means that something is possible or permitted. **Нельзя́** is its opposite. Both are unchanging words used without a subject; if you need to say *who* may or may not, that person goes in the dative, exactly like мне хо́лодно.

:::ex
**Мо́жно** войти́? | May I come in?
Здесь **мо́жно** кури́ть? | Is smoking allowed here?
Здесь **нельзя́** кури́ть. | You can't smoke here.
Ему́ **нельзя́** волнова́ться. | He mustn't get upset. | doctor's orders
Биле́ты **мо́жно** купи́ть онла́йн. | You can buy tickets online.
:::

Мо́жно is the everyday word for asking permission, and it's wonderfully economical. On its own, with a gesture, Мо́жно? covers "May I?", "Is this seat free?" and "Can I take this?". It's also how you ask for things:

:::ex
**Мо́жно** мне ещё ко́фе? | Could I have some more coffee?
**Мо́жно** я откро́ю окно́? | Is it OK if I open the window? | colloquial: мо́жно + я + perfective future
:::

:::dialogue
А: Мо́жно? | May I?
Б: Да, коне́чно. | Yes, of course.
:::

For the past and future, add бы́ло or бу́дет after the word:

:::ex
Вчера́ **нельзя́ бы́ло** купа́ться. | Swimming wasn't allowed yesterday.
**Мо́жно бы́ло** про́сто спроси́ть. | You could have just asked.
За́втра **мо́жно бу́дет** отдохну́ть. | Tomorrow we'll be able to relax.
:::

Remember from [the aspect section](#s-04-03) that нельзя́ changes its meaning with the aspect of the infinitive: нельзя́ входи́ть is "you may not go in", нельзя́ войти́ is "it's impossible to get in".

:::warn There is no "не мо́жно"
The opposite of мо́жно is нельзя́. Russians don't say не мо́жно.
:::

## На́до and ну́жно: need to

The standard, neutral way to say that something needs doing is **на́до** or **ну́жно** + infinitive. The two are interchangeable; на́до is a touch more conversational. Again the person goes in the dative, and if you leave the person out, the statement is general ("one needs to", "we need to").

:::ex
Мне **на́до** идти́. | I have to go.
Тебе́ **ну́жно** бо́льше спать. | You need to sleep more.
**На́до** купи́ть хлеб. | We need to buy bread.
Вам **на́до** поговори́ть с дире́ктором. | You need to talk to the director.
:::

Past and future, once more, with бы́ло and бу́дет:

:::ex
Мне **на́до бы́ло** позвони́ть ра́ньше. | I should have called earlier. / I needed to call earlier.
За́втра **на́до бу́дет** ра́но встать. | We'll have to get up early tomorrow.
:::

This is the workhorse. When English says "have to", "need to", "got to" or a mild "should", Russian most often says на́до.

### Not needing to is not the same as not being allowed to

English "must not" forbids; "don't have to" releases you. Russian keeps them just as far apart, with different words:

:::ex
Тебе́ **нельзя́** приходи́ть. | You mustn't come.
Тебе́ **не на́до** приходи́ть. | You don't need to come. / Don't come.
Тебе́ **не обяза́тельно** приходи́ть. | You don't have to come. | it's optional
**Мо́жно не** приходи́ть. | You don't have to come. | you're allowed not to
:::

Не на́до sits in between. Literally it's "there's no need", but said to someone directly, it is the normal polite way to ask them not to do something. On its own, Не на́до! means "Don't!"

## Ну́жен: needing a thing

When what you need is a *thing* rather than an action, Russian turns the sentence around. The thing needed is the subject, in the nominative; the word **ну́жен** agrees with it like a short adjective; and the person who needs it is in the dative. Literally: "to me a car is necessary".

| The thing needed is… | Form |
|---|---|
| masculine | ну́жен |
| neuter | ну́жно |
| feminine | нужна́ |
| plural | нужны́ |

:::ex
Мне **ну́жен** твой сове́т. | I need your advice.
Мне **нужна́** маши́на. | I need a car.
Мне **ну́жно** вре́мя. | I need time.
Нам **нужны́** де́ньги. | We need money.
Ты мне **ну́жен**. | I need you. | said to a man
Что вам **ну́жно**? | What do you need?
:::

In the past and future, быть agrees with the thing as well:

:::ex
Мне **нужна́ была́** по́мощь. | I needed help.
Нам **ну́жен бу́дет** перево́дчик. | We'll need an interpreter.
:::

So ну́жно turns up in two roles: as the neuter of ну́жен (Мне ну́жно вре́мя) and as the unchanging "need to" before an infinitive (Мне ну́жно идти́). The structure is the same pattern as нра́виться: the English subject becomes a Russian dative.

## До́лжен: duty and expectation

**До́лжен** is different from everything above. It isn't impersonal: it has a normal nominative subject and agrees with it in gender and number.

| Subject | Form |
|---|---|
| masculine | до́лжен |
| neuter | должно́ |
| feminine | должна́ |
| plural | должны́ |

It has three meanings.

**1. Obligation.** До́лжен is stronger and more personal than на́до. На́до says the situation requires something. До́лжен says it's *your* duty, promise or responsibility.

:::ex
Я **до́лжен** рабо́тать. | I have to work.
Она́ **должна́** позвони́ть в пять. | She's supposed to call at five.
Мы **должны́** ему́ помо́чь. | We must help him.
Ты **не до́лжен** так говори́ть. | You shouldn't talk like that.
:::

**2. Expectation.** "Should" in the sense of "if all is as I think it is".

:::ex
Он **до́лжен** быть до́ма. | He should be at home.
По́езд **до́лжен** прийти́ в шесть. | The train is due at six.
:::

**3. Owing.** With no infinitive, and with the person you owe in the dative.

:::ex
Ты мне **до́лжен** ты́сячу рубле́й. | You owe me a thousand rubles.
Ско́лько я вам **должна́**? | How much do I owe you?
:::

Past and future are made with быть, which agrees too. In the past, до́лжен был very often means "was supposed to (but didn't)".

:::ex
Я **до́лжен был** сказа́ть тебе́ ра́ньше. | I should have told you earlier.
Она́ **должна́ была́** прийти́ час наза́д. | She was supposed to come an hour ago.
Ты **до́лжен бу́дешь** всё объясни́ть. | You'll have to explain everything.
:::

## Прихо́дится: no choice

When you have to do something because circumstances force you, and you'd rather not, Russian uses the impersonal verb **приходи́ться / прийти́сь** with the dative. It has just three forms worth knowing:

- present: **прихо́дится** — (I) have to, keep having to
- past: **пришло́сь** — had to, ended up having to
- future: **придётся** — will have to

:::ex
Мне **прихо́дится** мно́го рабо́тать. | I have to work a lot. | not my idea
Мне **пришло́сь** ждать два часа́. | I had to wait two hours.
Нам **пришло́сь** взять такси́. | We ended up having to take a taxi.
Тебе́ **придётся** подожда́ть. | You'll have to wait.
:::

Compare the three shades of "I had to": Мне на́до бы́ло позвони́ть (it was necessary, and perhaps I didn't), Я до́лжен был позвони́ть (it was my duty; I was supposed to), Мне пришло́сь позвони́ть (I was forced to, and I did).

## Сто́ит: worth doing

**Сто́ит** + infinitive says that something is worth doing or is a good idea. It's a gentle way to recommend. The negative, **не сто́ит**, advises against, and as you know, it takes the imperfective.

:::ex
Э́тот фильм **сто́ит** посмотре́ть. | This movie is worth seeing.
Тебе́ **сто́ит** поговори́ть с врачо́м. | You'd do well to talk to a doctor.
**Не сто́ит** волнова́ться. | There's no point worrying.
:::

This is the verb сто́ить (to cost). Mind the stress: сто́ит is "costs, is worth", while стои́т is "stands".

## Three formal ones

You'll meet these in documents, announcements and careful speech. One line each:

- **обя́зан** (обя́зана, обя́заны) — "obliged", a formal or emphatic до́лжен. Ты не обя́зан отвеча́ть (You're not obliged to answer) is common in ordinary speech too.
- **вы́нужден** (вы́нуждена, вы́нуждены) — "forced, compelled", a formal пришло́сь. Я вы́нужден отказа́ться (I am forced to decline).
- **сле́дует** — "one should", an official на́до, with the dative. Вам сле́дует обрати́ться к врачу́ (You should consult a doctor).

## Maybe, probably, must be

For guessing, Russian mostly uses little parenthetical words rather than verbs. They don't change the grammar of the sentence at all; you just drop them in, set off by commas.

:::ex
**Мо́жет быть**, он придёт. | Maybe he'll come.
**Возмо́жно**, я опозда́ю. | I may possibly be late.
**Наве́рное**, она́ уже́ до́ма. | She's probably home already.
Он, **должно́ быть**, уста́л. | He must be tired.
:::

Мо́жет быть is literally "(it) can be"; in conversation it's regularly cut down to мо́жет: Мо́жет, зака́жем пи́ццу? (Maybe we should order pizza?) Наве́рное (also наве́рно) is by far the most common way to say "probably", and it's what you should reach for where English uses "must be" as a guess. Должно́ быть means the same but is more bookish. The whole toolkit of hedges is laid out in [Hedging, seeming and hearsay](#s-07-05).

And the fixed exclamation of disbelief:

:::ex
Не мо́жет быть! | No way! / That can't be!
:::

## Could and should

These two English words do so many jobs that they need a paragraph of their own.

**"Could"** as the past of "can" is мог or смог. As "would be able to", it's мочь with бы, from the [previous section](#s-04-07):

:::ex
Я **мог бы** помо́чь. | I could help.
Ты **мог бы** позвони́ть! | You could have called! | a reproach
:::

**"Should"** has no single equivalent. Choose by how strong you want to be:

:::ex
Тебе́ **на́до** отдохну́ть. | You should rest. | plain, most common
**На́до бы** позвони́ть ба́бушке. | I really ought to call Grandma. | бы softens it: a good intention
Тебе́ **сто́ит** отдохну́ть. | It would be a good idea for you to rest.
Тебе́ **лу́чше** уйти́. | You'd better leave. | literally "for you it's better to leave"
Ты **бы отдохну́л**. | You should get some rest. | the gentlest
Ты **до́лжен** отдохну́ть. | You must rest. | strong: almost an order
:::

"Should have" is на́до бы́ло, or до́лжен был when it was someone's duty; "shouldn't have" is не на́до бы́ло:

:::ex
**На́до бы́ло** взять зонт. | We should have taken an umbrella.
**Не на́до бы́ло** ему́ говори́ть. | You shouldn't have told him.
:::
