# Possibility and necessity: мо́жно, на́до, до́лжен, мочь

English handles ability, permission, obligation and need with one tidy class of little verbs: can, may, must, should, need. Russian has no such class. The same meanings are spread across three different kinds of word: words that never change, short adjectives, and ordinary verbs. Each kind has its own grammar: who appears in which case, what agrees with what, how the past and future are made.

So the useful question about any of these words is "which kind is it?" Once you know that, its behavior stops being arbitrary. An index from the English verbs is at the end.

:::vocab
мо́жно | it's allowed; it's possible | unchanging, + dative
нельзя́ | it's not allowed; it's impossible | unchanging, + dative
на́до, ну́жно | it's necessary | unchanging, + dative
обяза́тельно | definitely, without fail; не обяза́тельно = not necessarily
до́лжен | owing, obliged, supposed to | short adjective; agrees with the subject
ну́жен | needed | short adjective; agrees with the thing needed
мочь / смочь | to be able, can | могу́, мо́жешь… мо́гут; past мог, могла́
уме́ть | to know how to | уме́ю, уме́ешь
приходи́ться / прийти́сь | to have to (because of circumstances) | impersonal, + dative
удава́ться / уда́ться | to manage, succeed | impersonal, + dative
сто́ит | it's worth, it's a good idea to
мо́жет быть | maybe
наве́рное | probably
пла́вать | to swim
сове́т | advice, a piece of advice | m.
:::

## Words that never change: мо́жно, нельзя́, на́до, ну́жно

The first kind is a small family of words with exactly one form each, neither verbs nor adjectives. They belong with хо́лодно in мне хо́лодно: words that state a fact about the situation. Here the fact is that something is permitted, impossible or necessary. They share one grammar:

- There is no subject. Nothing stands in the nominative, and nothing agrees with anything.
- The person concerned, if mentioned at all, is in the dative. Leave the person out and the statement is general: "one can", "we need to".
- What is allowed or needed is an infinitive.
- Past and future add бы́ло or бу́дет after the word: always neuter бы́ло, since there is no subject to agree with.

**Мо́жно** says that something is possible or permitted; **нельзя́** is its opposite.

:::ex
**Мо́жно** войти́? | May I come in?
Здесь **нельзя́** кури́ть. | You can't smoke here.
Ему́ **нельзя́** волнова́ться. | He mustn't get upset. | doctor's orders
Биле́ты **мо́жно** купи́ть онла́йн. | You can buy tickets online.
:::

Мо́жно is the everyday word for asking permission, and it's wonderfully economical. On its own, with a gesture, Мо́жно? covers "May I?", "Is this seat free?" and "Can I take this?". It's also how you ask for things:

:::ex
**Мо́жно** мне ещё ко́фе? | Could I have some more coffee?
**Мо́жно** я откро́ю окно́? | Is it OK if I open the window? | colloquial: мо́жно + я + perfective future
:::

**На́до** and **ну́жно** say that something needs doing. They are interchangeable; на́до is a touch more conversational. This is the workhorse: where English says "have to", "need to" or a mild "should", Russian most often says на́до.

:::ex
Мне **на́до** идти́. | I have to go.
Тебе́ **ну́жно** бо́льше спать. | You need to sleep more.
**На́до** купи́ть хлеб. | We need to buy bread.
:::

Past and future:

:::ex
Вчера́ **нельзя́ бы́ло** купа́ться. | Swimming wasn't allowed yesterday.
**Мо́жно бы́ло** про́сто спроси́ть. | You could have just asked.
За́втра **мо́жно бу́дет** отдохну́ть. | Tomorrow we'll be able to relax.
Мне **на́до бы́ло** позвони́ть ра́ньше. | I should have called earlier. / I needed to call earlier.
За́втра **на́до бу́дет** ра́но встать. | We'll have to get up early tomorrow.
:::

### Negation

The negative of мо́жно is нельзя́, not "не мо́жно", which Russians don't say. As you saw in [the aspect section](#s-04-03), нельзя́ changes meaning with the aspect of the infinitive: нельзя́ входи́ть is "you may not go in", нельзя́ войти́ is "it's impossible to get in".

Not being allowed and not being required are kept well apart:

:::ex
Тебе́ **нельзя́** приходи́ть. | You mustn't come.
Тебе́ **не на́до** приходи́ть. | You don't need to come. / Don't come.
Тебе́ **не обяза́тельно** приходи́ть. | You don't have to come. | it's optional
**Мо́жно не** приходи́ть. | You don't have to come. | you're allowed not to
:::

Не на́до is literally "there's no need", but said to someone directly it is the normal polite way to ask them not to do something. On its own, Не на́до! means "Don't!"

### The same family

Other words behave exactly this way, among them **пора́** (it's time), **жаль** (it's a pity) and **лу́чше** (it's better).

:::ex
Мне **пора́** идти́. | It's time for me to go.
Мне **жаль** уезжа́ть. | I'm sorry to be leaving.
Тебе́ **лу́чше** уйти́. | You'd better leave. | literally "for you it's better to leave"
:::

The whole family is treated in [short adjectives and predicate words](#s-06-08) and [sentences without a subject](#s-06-09).

## Short adjectives that agree: до́лжен, ну́жен

The second kind is built the opposite way. **До́лжен** and **ну́жен** are short adjectives: they need a subject in the nominative and agree with it in gender and number. Past and future are made with был or бу́дет, agreeing with that same subject.

| Subject | до́лжен | ну́жен |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | до́лжен | ну́жен |
| neuter | должно́ | ну́жно |
| feminine | должна́ | нужна́ |
| plural | должны́ | нужны́ |

### До́лжен: the subject owes

The root is долг (debt, duty), and the subject is the one who owes. Most literally, money, with the person owed in the dative:

:::ex
Ты мне **до́лжен** ты́сячу рубле́й. | You owe me a thousand rubles.
Ско́лько я вам **должна́**? | How much do I owe you?
:::

With an infinitive, what is owed is an action. На́до says the situation requires something; до́лжен says it is *this person's* duty or promise, so it is stronger and more personal.

:::ex
Я **до́лжен** рабо́тать. | I have to work.
Она́ **должна́** позвони́ть в пять. | She's supposed to call at five.
Мы **должны́** ему́ помо́чь. | We must help him.
Ты **не до́лжен** так говори́ть. | You shouldn't talk like that.
:::

The same construction states what is expected, if all is as you think it is:

:::ex
Он **до́лжен** быть до́ма. | He should be at home.
Она́ уже́ **должна́** быть здесь. | She should be here by now.
По́езд **до́лжен** прийти́ в шесть. | The train is due at six.
:::

In the past, до́лжен был very often means "was supposed to (but didn't)".

:::ex
Я **до́лжен был** сказа́ть тебе́ ра́ньше. | I should have told you earlier.
Она́ **должна́ была́** прийти́ час наза́д. | She was supposed to come an hour ago.
Ты **до́лжен бу́дешь** всё объясни́ть. | You'll have to explain everything. | the future: до́лжен + бу́ду, бу́дешь…
:::

### Ну́жен: the subject is the thing needed

When what you need is a thing rather than an action, the thing is the nominative subject, ну́жен agrees with it, and the person who needs it is in the dative: "to me a car is necessary". It is the arrangement you know from нра́виться.

:::ex
Мне **ну́жен** твой сове́т. | I need your advice.
Мне **нужна́** маши́на. | I need a car.
Мне **ну́жно** вре́мя. | I need time.
Нам **нужны́** де́ньги. | We need money.
Ты мне **ну́жен**. | I need you. | said to a man
Что вам **ну́жно**? | What do you need?
Мне **нужна́ была́** по́мощь. | I needed help.
Нам **ну́жен бу́дет** перево́дчик. | We'll need an interpreter.
:::

:::warn Ну́жно or ну́жен?
Ну́жно is two words that look alike. Before an infinitive it is the unchanging word from the first family: Мне ну́жно идти́, Мне ну́жно бы́ло идти́. With a noun it is merely the neuter of ну́жен, and it changes as soon as the noun does: Мне ну́жно вре́мя, but Мне нужна́ маши́на, Мне нужна́ была́ маши́на.

So ask what is needed. An action: ну́жно (or на́до), and nothing agrees. A thing: ну́жен, agreeing with the thing. And don't start with я: Я ну́жен means "I am needed".
:::

### More short adjectives

Four more work the same way:

- **обя́зан** (обя́зана, обя́заны): "obliged", a formal or emphatic до́лжен. Ты не обя́зан отвеча́ть (You're not obliged to answer) is common in ordinary speech too.
- **вы́нужден** (вы́нуждена, вы́нуждены): "forced, compelled"; formal. Я вы́нужден отказа́ться (I am forced to decline).
- **гото́в** (гото́ва, гото́вы): "ready, willing". Я гото́в помо́чь (I'm ready to help).
- **спосо́бен** (спосо́бна, спосо́бны): "capable". Он спосо́бен на всё (He's capable of anything).

## Real verbs: мочь, уме́ть and the impersonal ones

The third kind is ordinary verbs, with conjugation, tenses and aspect like any other.

### Мочь and уме́ть

These two take a normal nominative subject. **Мочь** says that nothing prevents you: you have the physical ability, the time, the opportunity, the permission. **Уме́ть** says you have a learned skill: you know how.

:::ex
Я **уме́ю** пла́вать, но сего́дня не **могу́**: у меня́ боли́т плечо́. | I can swim, but today I can't: my shoulder hurts.
Ты **уме́ешь** води́ть маши́ну? | Can you drive?
Она́ **уме́ет** слу́шать. | She knows how to listen.
Ты **мо́жешь** мне помо́чь? | Can you help me?
Я не **могу́** прийти́ за́втра. | I can't come tomorrow.
:::

Мочь is the irregular -чь verb from the [conjugation section](#s-04-04): могу́, мо́жешь, мо́жет, мо́жем, мо́жете, мо́гут; past мог, могла́, могло́, могли́. Its perfective partner **смочь** is what you use for the future (there is no бу́ду мочь) and for managing, or failing, on one particular occasion.

:::ex
Ты **смо́жешь** прийти́ в суббо́ту? | Will you be able to come on Saturday?
Я не **смог** откры́ть файл. | I couldn't open the file. | I tried, it didn't work
В де́тстве я **мог** спать по двена́дцать часо́в. | As a child I could sleep twelve hours at a time. | general ability
:::

Мочь also states what may happen:

:::ex
Он **мо́жет** опозда́ть. | He may be late.
Она́ **мо́жет** не знать. | She might not know.
:::

### Impersonal verbs: the person is dative again

The remaining verbs have no subject. They stand in the third person singular (neuter in the past), and the person is in the dative, as with на́до.

**Приходи́ться / прийти́сь** is for what circumstances force on you, usually against your wishes. Three forms do the work: прихо́дится (have to, keep having to), пришло́сь (had to), придётся (will have to).

:::ex
Мне **прихо́дится** мно́го рабо́тать. | I have to work a lot. | not my idea
Мне **пришло́сь** ждать два часа́. | I had to wait two hours.
Тебе́ **придётся** подожда́ть. | You'll have to wait.
:::

That gives three shades of "I had to", one from each kind of word: Мне на́до бы́ло позвони́ть (it was necessary, and perhaps I didn't), Я до́лжен был позвони́ть (it was my duty; I was supposed to), Мне пришло́сь позвони́ть (I was forced to, and I did).

**Удава́ться / уда́ться** is the opposite experience: something works out for you. Удаётся, удало́сь, уда́стся.

:::ex
Нам **удало́сь** купи́ть биле́ты. | We managed to get tickets.
:::

**Сто́ит** + infinitive says that something is worth doing; it's a gentle way to recommend. **Не сто́ит** advises against, and as you know, it takes the imperfective. This is the verb сто́ить (to cost). Mind the stress: сто́ит is "costs, is worth", while стои́т is "stands".

:::ex
Э́тот фильм **сто́ит** посмотре́ть. | This movie is worth seeing.
Тебе́ **сто́ит** поговори́ть с врачо́м. | You'd do well to talk to a doctor.
**Не сто́ит** волнова́ться. | There's no point worrying.
:::

**Сле́дует** is an official на́до, "one ought to", found in documents, announcements and careful speech: Вам сле́дует обрати́ться к врачу́ (You should consult a doctor).

## Guessing and softening

**Guessing.** When "may" or "must" expresses how likely something is, Russian mostly uses parenthetical words. They don't touch the grammar of the sentence; you drop them in, set off by commas.

:::ex
**Мо́жет быть**, он придёт. | Maybe he'll come.
**Возмо́жно**, я опозда́ю. | I may possibly be late.
**Наве́рное**, она́ уже́ до́ма. | She's probably home already.
Он, **должно́ быть**, уста́л. | He must be tired.
:::

Two of them are frozen forms of words from this section: мо́жет быть is literally "(it) can be", and должно́ быть is the neuter of до́лжен. In conversation мо́жет быть is regularly cut down to мо́жет: Мо́жет, зака́жем пи́ццу? (Maybe we should order pizza?) Наве́рное (also наве́рно) is by far the most common way to say "probably", and it's what to reach for where English uses "must be" as a guess; должно́ быть means the same but is more bookish. The whole toolkit is laid out in [Hedging, seeming and hearsay](#s-07-05). And the fixed exclamation of disbelief is Не мо́жет быть! (No way!)

**Softening.** English "could" and "should" are often just "can" and "must" made less blunt. Russian does that with бы, from the [previous section](#s-04-07), added to whichever word you would have used anyway:

:::ex
Я **мог бы** помо́чь. | I could help.
Ты **мог бы** позвони́ть! | You could have called! | a reproach
**На́до бы** позвони́ть ба́бушке. | I really ought to call Grandma. | a good intention
Тебе́ **сто́ило бы** отдохну́ть. | You really should get some rest.
Ты **бы отдохну́л**. | You should get some rest. | the gentlest: бы alone
:::

"Should have" needs no бы, only the past: на́до бы́ло, or до́лжен был when it was someone's duty.

:::ex
**На́до бы́ло** взять зонт. | We should have taken an umbrella.
**Не на́до бы́ло** ему́ говори́ть. | You shouldn't have told him.
:::

## An index for English speakers

This table is a way in from English, not a picture of how Russian is organized: each English verb is scattered across the kinds of word above.

| English | Russian |
|---|---|
| can (ability, opportunity) | мочь / смочь |
| can (know how) | уме́ть |
| can, may (permission) | мо́жно |
| could | мог, смог (past of "can"); мог бы (would be able to; could have) |
| may, might (possibility) | мо́жет + infinitive; мо́жет быть, возмо́жно |
| must, have to | на́до, ну́жно; до́лжен (duty); прихо́дится (forced) |
| must be (a guess) | наве́рное; должно́ быть |
| should (advice) | на́до; сто́ит; тебе́ лу́чше; softened with бы |
| should (expectation) | до́лжен |
| should have | на́до бы́ло; до́лжен был |
| need + verb | на́до, ну́жно + infinitive |
| need + thing | ну́жен, ну́жно, нужна́, нужны́, agreeing with the thing |
| don't have to | не на́до; не обяза́тельно; мо́жно не |
| mustn't, not allowed | нельзя́ + imperfective |
| can't (impossible) | нельзя́ + perfective; не могу́ |
| managed to | смог; мне удало́сь |
| owe | до́лжен + dative |

:::summary
| Kind of word | Members | Person | Past and future |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unchanging words | мо́жно, нельзя́, на́до, ну́жно | dative | на́до бы́ло, на́до бу́дет |
| Short adjectives | до́лжен, ну́жен | nominative subject, agreeing (with ну́жен, the thing; the person is dative) | должна́ была́, должна́ бу́дет |
| Personal verbs | мочь / смочь, уме́ть | nominative subject | мог, смогу́ |
| Impersonal verbs | прихо́дится, удаётся, сто́ит, сле́дует | dative | пришло́сь, придётся |
:::
