# Aspect: the big idea

Here is a fact that has been hiding in plain sight: almost every Russian verb you have met is one half of a pair. For "to read" Russian has чита́ть *and* прочита́ть. For "to do" it has де́лать *and* сде́лать. For "to say" it has говори́ть *and* сказа́ть. Both members of a pair name the same action. What differs is how the speaker chooses to *look at* that action, and this choice is called **aspect**.

Aspect is the heart of the Russian verb. English spreads its verb meanings over a dozen tenses (I read, I was reading, I have read, I had been reading…). Russian has only three tenses, and it does the rest of the work with aspect. Get comfortable with this one idea and the whole verb system falls into place.

:::vocab
де́лать / сде́лать | to do, to make
чита́ть / прочита́ть | to read
писа́ть / написа́ть | to write
гото́вить / пригото́вить | to cook, to prepare
звони́ть / позвони́ть | to call (on the phone) | + dative
сдава́ть / сдать | to take / to pass (an exam)
реша́ть / реши́ть | to work on / to solve; to decide
покупа́ть / купи́ть | to buy
говори́ть / сказа́ть | to speak, to say / to say, to tell
брать / взять | to take
иска́ть | to look for | impf. only
находи́ть / найти́ | to find
экза́мен | exam | m.
зада́ча | problem (in math etc.), task | f.
отчёт | report | m.
наконе́ц | finally, at last
:::

## Two ways of looking at one action

The two aspects are called **perfective** and **imperfective**. Forget whatever those names suggest; here is what they do.

The **perfective** presents an action as *one complete whole*: a single event with its end point included. It happened (or will happen), it's finished, and usually there is a result on the table. Think of a dot on a timeline, or a box that has been ticked.

The **imperfective** is everything else. It does not package the action as a single finished whole. That leaves it free to show the action as a process under way, as something repeated, or as a bare fact with no interest in the outcome.

:::figure aspect
The imperfective shows an action from the inside or as a series; the perfective wraps it up as a single finished whole.
:::

Look at the same sentence in both aspects:

:::ex
Я **чита́л** кни́гу. | I was reading a book. / I read a book (for a while). / I've read the book (at some point).
Я **прочита́л** кни́гу. | I read the book (all of it). / I've finished the book.
:::

Чита́л says that reading went on. Maybe I finished, maybe not; that's not what I'm telling you. Прочита́л says one thing loudly: the job is done, start to finish.

The imperfective is the wide, general-purpose aspect, and the perfective is the specialized one. So rather than asking "is this imperfective?", it's easier to ask "is this one complete, bounded event?" If yes, perfective. If no, or if you don't care, imperfective.

## What the perfective says

A perfective verb reports a single event as completed. In practice you'll feel three flavors, which are really the same thing.

**1. A result that matters now.** The action is over and its consequence is what the speaker is pointing at.

:::ex
Я **пригото́вил** у́жин. | I've made dinner. | so dinner is ready
Она́ **написа́ла** отчёт. | She has written the report. | the report exists
Я **купи́л** биле́ты! | I bought the tickets! | we have tickets
Он **сдал** экза́мен. | He passed the exam.
:::

**2. The next step in a story.** Each perfective moves the plot forward by one event: this happened, then this, then this.

:::ex
Я **встал**, **вы́пил** ко́фе и **написа́л** Са́ше. | I got up, had a coffee and texted Sasha.
:::

**3. A single moment that changes the situation.**

:::ex
Что ты **сде́лал**?! | What have you done?!
Он **сказа́л** «нет». | He said no.
:::

## What the imperfective says

Because the imperfective is "everything that isn't one bounded whole", it covers several situations that English treats quite differently. There are four worth knowing by name.

**1. Process.** The action is shown in mid-flow. We're inside it, not looking back at it as a finished thing.

:::ex
Вчера́ ве́чером я **чита́л** кни́гу. | Last night I was reading a book.
Когда́ ты позвони́ла, я **гото́вил** у́жин. | When you called, I was making dinner.
Я весь день **писа́л** отчёт. | I spent the whole day writing the report.
:::

**2. Repetition and habit.** If something happens many times, it isn't *one* event, so it's imperfective, even if each individual occasion was completed.

:::ex
Я ка́ждый день **покупа́ю** ко́фе по доро́ге на рабо́ту. | I buy a coffee on the way to work every day.
В шко́ле мы ча́сто **писа́ли** те́сты. | At school we often wrote tests.
Ба́бушка всегда́ **звони́ла** по воскресе́ньям. | Grandma always called on Sundays.
:::

**3. The bare fact.** Sometimes you just want to know whether an action took place at all: ever, at any point. You're not following a story, and you're not asking about a result. Russian uses the imperfective here, even though the action was obviously completed.

:::ex
Ты **чита́л** э́ту кни́гу? | Have you read this book? | ever, at all
Я уже́ **смотре́л** э́тот фильм. | I've seen this movie already.
Что ты **де́лал** вчера́? | What did you do yesterday? | how did you spend the day
:::

Compare the last one with Что ты сде́лал?! above. Что ты де́лал? is a friendly question about how your day went. Что ты сде́лал? asks about one specific deed and its consequences, and it's what you say when you find the cat dyed green.

**4. The attempt.** Many actions have a goal that you may or may not reach. The imperfective names the trying; the perfective names the succeeding. English often needs two different verbs for this.

:::ex
Он **сдава́л** экза́мен. | He took the exam. | we don't know how it went
Он **сдал** экза́мен. | He passed the exam.
Я до́лго **реша́л** зада́чу, но не **реши́л**. | I worked on the problem for a long time but didn't solve it.
Я **звони́л** тебе́! | I called you! / I tried calling you! | perhaps nobody answered
Я **позвони́л** ма́ме и всё рассказа́л. | I called Mom and told her everything.
:::

Я звони́л тебе́ is what you say to defend yourself: the calling happened. Whether we actually talked is left open. Я позвони́л presents the call as a done deal, one event in a chain.

## Why the perfective has no present tense

If the perfective shows an action as a finished whole, then it cannot describe what is going on right now. "Right now" is a moment you are inside of, and anything happening at this moment is by definition in progress. So Russian simply has no perfective present.

Perfective verbs still have a set of forms that *look* exactly like the present tense: прочита́ю, прочита́ешь, прочита́ет… Since they can't mean the present, they mean the **future**: "I will read (it, to the end)".

| | Imperfective | Perfective |
|---|---|---|
| Past | чита́л — was reading, used to read, read | прочита́л — read, finished reading |
| Present | чита́ю — read, am reading | — |
| Future | бу́ду чита́ть — will read, will be reading | прочита́ю — will read, will finish |

Five forms in all. You already know how to make every one of them: past in -л, present endings, бу́ду + infinitive. The only new rule is this:

:::warn Бу́ду only goes with imperfectives
Бу́ду + infinitive works **only** with imperfective verbs: бу́ду чита́ть, бу́ду де́лать. You can never say бу́ду прочита́ть or бу́ду сде́лать. The perfective makes its future all by itself: прочита́ю, сде́лаю.
:::

:::ex
Я сейча́с **пишу́** сообще́ние. | I'm writing a message right now. | impf. present
Я **напишу́** тебе́ за́втра. | I'll write to you tomorrow. | pf. future
За́втра я **бу́ду писа́ть** отчёт. | Tomorrow I'll be writing the report. | impf. future
:::

The [next section](#s-04-02) goes through the past and the future in detail.

## Aspect is not the English continuous

It is tempting to decide that imperfective = "was doing" and perfective = "did". It works about half the time, and the other half will hurt you. Look again at the examples above:

- Ты чита́л э́ту кни́гу? is imperfective, but the English is "Have you read…?", not "Were you reading…?"
- Я ка́ждый день покупа́ю ко́фе is "I buy", not "I am buying".
- Он сдава́л экза́мен is "he took the exam", plain past.

Going the other way, English "I read the book yesterday" could be чита́л or прочита́л, and English doesn't tell you which. You have to ask the Russian question: am I presenting this as one completed whole, with its result, or not?

So don't translate tenses. Decide what you mean, then pick the aspect.

## How pairs are formed

There are three ways a pair can be related. None of them lets you predict the partner with certainty, but the patterns make pairs much easier to remember.

### 1. A prefix makes the perfective

The basic verb is imperfective, and adding a prefix gives the perfective partner.

| Imperfective | Perfective | |
|---|---|---|
| де́лать | **с**де́лать | do, make |
| писа́ть | **на**писа́ть | write |
| чита́ть | **про**чита́ть | read |
| ви́деть | **у**ви́деть | see / catch sight of |
| слы́шать | **у**слы́шать | hear |
| гото́вить | **при**гото́вить | cook, prepare |
| смотре́ть | **по**смотре́ть | watch, look |
| звони́ть | **по**звони́ть | call |
| пить | **вы́**пить | drink |
| есть | **съ**есть | eat |
| учи́ть | **вы́**учить | learn |
| плати́ть | **за**плати́ть | pay |

Which prefix a verb takes has to be learned; по-, с-, на- and про- are the most common. One thing is regular: the prefix вы́- always takes the stress in a perfective verb (вы́пить, вы́учить).

Prefixes are a big topic of their own, because most of the time a prefix adds meaning as well as changing aspect: писа́ть is "to write", but подписа́ть is "to sign" and переписа́ть is "to rewrite". The table above lists the cases where the prefix adds (almost) nothing except "done". The rest of the story is in [Verbal prefixes beyond motion](#s-05-06).

### 2. A suffix makes the imperfective

Here it's the other way round: the perfective is the shorter verb, and the imperfective is built from it with a longer or different suffix.

| Imperfective | Perfective | |
|---|---|---|
| реша́ть | реши́ть | solve, decide |
| получа́ть | получи́ть | receive, get |
| отвеча́ть | отве́тить | answer |
| изуча́ть | изучи́ть | study (a subject) |
| объясня́ть | объясни́ть | explain |
| расска́зывать | рассказа́ть | tell (a story), recount |
| пока́зывать | показа́ть | show |
| спра́шивать | спроси́ть | ask |
| опа́здывать | опозда́ть | be late |
| дава́ть | дать | give |
| встава́ть | встать | get up |
| открыва́ть | откры́ть | open |
| закрыва́ть | закры́ть | close |
| забыва́ть | забы́ть | forget |
| понима́ть | поня́ть | understand |
| начина́ть | нача́ть | begin |
| помога́ть | помо́чь | help |
| покупа́ть | купи́ть | buy |

A useful rule of thumb: if two verbs share a root and the same prefix, **the longer one is the imperfective**, the one with -а-, -ва- or -ыва-/-ива- before the ending. And a verb with -ыва-/-ива- is practically always imperfective.

Покупа́ть / купи́ть is the odd one out: here the perfective is the one *without* a prefix. Don't let the по- fool you.

### 3. Two unrelated verbs

A handful of very common pairs are made of two completely different words (the technical name is *suppletive*, the same thing as English "go / went").

| Imperfective | Perfective | |
|---|---|---|
| говори́ть | сказа́ть | say, tell |
| брать | взять | take |
| класть | положи́ть | put (lay down) |
| лови́ть | пойма́ть | catch |
| сади́ться | сесть | sit down |
| ложи́ться | лечь | lie down |

Говори́ть also means "to speak, to talk" in general, and in that sense it has its own perfective, поговори́ть (to have a talk).

:::note Looking and finding
You might expect иска́ть (to look for) and найти́ (to find) to be a pair. They aren't. Иска́ть is an imperfective verb with no perfective partner: searching is pure process. Найти́ has its own, perfectly regular imperfective, находи́ть, which is what you use for repeated finding: Я всегда́ нахожу́ вре́мя (I always find the time). Still, the two verbs constantly appear together and behave exactly like an attempt and its result:

Я до́лго иска́л ключи́ и наконе́ц нашёл. — I looked for my keys for ages and finally found them.
:::

### Verbs without a partner

Not every verb comes in two. Verbs that name a state rather than an action tend to be imperfective only: быть (to be), жить (to live), знать (to know), зна́чить (to mean), сто́ить (to cost). There is no "completed whole" of knowing. A few verbs, mostly bookish or borrowed ones, use one form for both aspects: испо́льзовать (to use), организова́ть (to organize). You'll meet both kinds as you go; no need to hunt for them.

## Learn every verb as a pair

There is no shortcut here. When you learn a new verb, learn both partners together, the way you learn a noun together with its gender: not "to answer — отвеча́ть" but "to answer — отвеча́ть / отве́тить". This guide always writes pairs in that order, imperfective / perfective, and the most common ones are collected in [Common aspect pairs](#s-09-04).

Russian is at least kind about one thing. The two partners are separate verbs, but each one conjugates in a perfectly ordinary way, with the endings you already know. Aspect costs you vocabulary, not grammar tables.

## Which one do I use?

When you're about to say a verb, run through these questions in order. The first "yes" decides.

1. **Is it happening right now?** Imperfective. There is no other option. — Я чита́ю.
2. **Is it repeated, habitual, or a general truth?** Imperfective. Words like ча́сто (often), всегда́ (always), обы́чно (usually), ка́ждый день (every day) are reliable signals. — Я ча́сто опа́здываю.
3. **Are you describing the process, or saying how long it went on?** Imperfective. — Мы два часа́ говори́ли о рабо́те.
4. **Are you only asking or stating whether it ever happened, with no interest in the result?** Imperfective. — Ты смотре́л э́тот фильм?
5. **Was it only an attempt, with the outcome failed or unknown?** Imperfective. — Я звони́л, но он не отве́тил.
6. **Is it one specific action, done (or to be done) from start to finish, where the result or the next step is the point?** Perfective. — Я сде́лал. Я позвоню́.

Notice that five of the six answers are "imperfective". That is the real proportion of meanings, though not of how often each form turns up: in storytelling and in everyday promises and requests the perfective is everywhere.

A short conversation with both aspects doing their jobs:

:::dialogue
А: Что ты де́лал вчера́? | What did you do yesterday?
Б: Писа́л отчёт. Весь день! | Wrote the report. All day!
А: Написа́л? | Did you get it written?
Б: Нет ещё. За́втра напишу́. | Not yet. I'll finish it tomorrow.
А: А хлеб ты купи́л? | And did you buy bread?
Б: Ой. Забы́л. Сейча́с куплю́. | Oops. I forgot. I'll go buy it now.
:::

Де́лал and писа́л: how the day was spent. Написа́л?: is there a finished report? Напишу́, куплю́: single results promised for the future. Забы́л: one event, with a consequence we can all see, namely no bread.

:::summary
- Every action usually has two verbs: an imperfective and a perfective.
- **Perfective** = one complete, bounded event: a result, the next step in a story. No present tense; its present-looking forms are the future.
- **Imperfective** = everything else: process, repetition, the bare fact that something happened, an attempt.
- Pairs are formed by prefix (де́лать / сде́лать), by suffix (реша́ть / реши́ть) or from two different verbs (говори́ть / сказа́ть). Learn them as pairs.
- Don't translate English tenses. Ask: one completed whole, or not?
:::
