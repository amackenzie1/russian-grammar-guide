# Colloquial shortcuts, fillers and slang

Everything so far in this guide has been real Russian, but it has been Russian with its shirt tucked in. The moment you open a messenger or sit down in a kitchen with friends, the language loosens: words lose syllables, verbs go missing, sentences start with «коро́че» and end with «вот», and a message reads «ща бу́ду, 5 мин)».

None of this is "bad Russian". It's the relaxed register every native speaker moves into among their own, with rules of its own. The goal here is mostly **recognition**: understand all of it, use some of it with people who use it with you, and keep it out of anything addressed to a stranger, an elder or an employer.

Each item below carries a rough label: *speech* means it's how people talk but not normally how they write; *chat* means you'll see it typed in messages; *slang* means it marks the speaker as young, or casual, or both.

## Worn-down words

The commonest words in any language get ground down by use. English has "gonna" and "dunno"; Russian has these:

| Full form | Worn-down form | Where |
|---|---|---|
| сейча́с (now) | **щас**, **ща** | speech and chat; nearly universal |
| что, чего́ (what) | **чё** (also typed чо), **шо** | speech and chat |
| ничего́ (nothing; it's fine) | **ничё** | speech and chat |
| ты́сяча (thousand) | **ты́ща** | speech; almost everyone says it |
| вообще́ (at all, in general) | **ваще́** | speech and chat; jokey when typed |
| сего́дня (today) | **сёдня** | speech |
| ско́лько (how much) | **ско́ка** | speech; typed only as a joke |
| то́лько (only) | **то́ка** | speech; typed only as a joke |
| когда́ (when) | **када́** | speech |
| здра́вствуйте (hello) | **здра́сте** | speech; very common |
| пожа́луйста (please) | **пожа́лста** | speech; the normal pronunciation |
| говори́т (says) | **грит** | speech, when reporting what someone said |
| тебя́, тебе́ (you) | **тя**, **те** | speech and chat |

Some of these are simply how the word is pronounced at normal speed. Nobody outside a language classroom says all four syllables of пожа́луйста, and ты́сяча in three clear syllables sounds like a news anchor. You don't have to imitate them deliberately. Speak faster and they'll happen by themselves.

:::ex
**Ща** приду́. | I'll be right there. |
**Чё** де́лаешь? | Whatcha doing? |
Да **ничё**, норма́льно. | It's nothing, I'm fine. |
Э́то сто́ит три **ты́щи**. | It costs three thousand. |
Я **ваще́** не по́нял. | I didn't get it at all. |
:::

:::warn Чё is not for everyone
Among friends **чё** is perfectly ordinary. But a bare «Чё?» in reply to a stranger or an older person sounds boorish, a bit like "Whaddya want?". If you didn't catch something, say «Что-что?», «Прости́те?» or «Как вы сказа́ли?». And **шо** is a southern and Ukrainian-flavored variant that most Russians use only for comic effect.
:::

### Abbreviations in messages

Typed Russian has a second set of shortcuts that exist only on a screen. Most drop the vowels.

| Typed | Stands for | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **спс**, **паси́б** | спаси́бо | thanks |
| **пж**, **пжлст** | пожа́луйста | please; you're welcome |
| **нзч** | [[ns:не́ за что]] | don't mention it |
| **норм** | норма́льно | fine, OK (also said aloud) |
| **ок**, **о́ки** | оке́й | OK |
| **лан** | ла́дно | all right |
| **мб** | мо́жет быть | maybe |
| **кст** | кста́ти | by the way |
| **оч** | о́чень | very |
| **крч** | коро́че | long story short |
| **прив** | приве́т | hi |
| **др** | день рожде́ния | birthday |
| **хз** | (a crude phrase) | no idea, who the heck knows |

The last one is everywhere in casual chats and mildly vulgar in origin; don't send it to your landlord.

Chat spelling has other habits too. People skip capital letters and commas, write е for ё, and leave off the final period, since a period at the end of a short message can come across as curt. A closing parenthesis stands for a smile, which we'll get to below.

:::dialogue
А: [[ns:ты где]] | where are you
Б: [[ns:ща буду, 5 мин)]] | be right there, 5 min :)
А: [[ns:ок жду. купи хлеба пж]] | ok waiting. get some bread pls
Б: [[ns:лан]] | k
А: [[ns:спс))]] | thx :))
:::

## Leaving words out

Spoken Russian drops whatever the listener can recover, and thanks to cases and direction words, that's a lot. Verbs of motion are the first to go: if there's a **куда́** or an accusative of direction, "going" is understood.

:::ex
Ты куда́? — В магази́н. | "Where are you off to?" "To the store." |
Я домо́й. | I'm going home. |
Ты отку́да? | Where are you coming from? Where are you from? |
Мы к ба́бушке. | We're going to Grandma's. |
Я за хле́бом. | I'm popping out for bread. |
Ты с на́ми? | Are you coming with us? |
:::

Verbs of wanting, giving and saying vanish just as easily, leaving a dative or an accusative to carry the meaning:

:::ex
Мне ко́фе, пожа́луйста. | A coffee for me, please. |
Вам кого́? | Who are you looking for? | at a door or on the phone
Ты чего́? | What's with you? What's the matter? |
Ты как? | How are you doing? |
А он что? | And what did he say? What did he do? |
Я ему́: «Подожди́!» А он — в маши́ну и уе́хал. | I go, "Wait!" And he's into the car and off. |
:::

Subject pronouns drop when the verb ending makes them obvious, and a short answer usually repeats the verb where English would say "yes":

:::dialogue
А: Ма́ме звони́л? | Did you call Mom?
Б: Звони́л. | I did.
А: И что? | And?
Б: Не зна́ю. Не берёт. | Don't know. She's not picking up.
:::

### Бу́дешь + a noun

One ellipsis has hardened into a pattern of its own. **Бу́дешь** with a noun in the accusative, and no infinitive, means "will you have...?", with есть or пить understood. It is the normal way to offer food and drink, and waiters use it too.

:::ex
Чай **бу́дешь**? | Want some tea? |
Ты суп **бу́дешь**? | Are you having soup? |
Я **не бу́ду**, спаси́бо. | Not for me, thanks. |
Кто **бу́дет** пи́ццу? | Who wants pizza? |
Что вы **бу́дете**? | What will you have? | a waiter
:::

The enthusiastic answer is «**Бу́ду!**», or the wonderfully brief «**А то!**» ("You bet!").

## Loose word order and repetition

You know that Russian word order is flexible and that new information tends to come last. Unplanned speech stretches this further. Speakers blurt out the most important word first and then tack on the rest as an afterthought:

:::ex
Хоро́ший он па́рень, Ди́ма. | He's a good guy, Dima. |
Краси́во тут у вас. | It's lovely here at your place. |
Он где рабо́тает? | Where does he work? | the question word isn't first
А Ма́ша — она́ что сказа́ла? | And Masha, what did she say? | the topic first, then a pronoun to pick it up
Ты э́то, не обижа́йся. | Hey, um, don't take offense. |
:::

**Repetition** is the spoken substitute for "very" and for "kept on":

:::ex
**Да-да**, я слу́шаю. | Yes, yes, I'm listening. |
**Дава́й-дава́й**, быстре́е! | Come on, come on, faster! |
**Иду́-иду́**! | Coming, coming! |
Он **шёл-шёл** и наконе́ц пришёл. | He walked and walked and finally got there. |
Мо́ре **си́нее-си́нее**. | The sea is the bluest blue. |
Э́то бы́ло **давны́м-давно́**. | It was a long, long time ago. |
:::

## Fillers and conversation steering words

Some words hold the floor while you think; others manage the listener's attention.

| Word | What it does |
|---|---|
| **ну** | "well"; the universal starter |
| **э́то**, **э́то са́мое** | "um", "the thingy"; a placeholder while you search for a word |
| **как его́**, **как её** | "what's-it-called", "what's-his-name" |
| **коро́че** | "so basically", "long story short"; opens stories, especially among the young |
| **ти́па**, **как бы** | "like", "sort of" |
| **вот** | "so, yeah"; closes a thought |
| **в о́бщем** | "anyway" |
| **зна́чит**, **зна́чит так** | "so", "right then" |
| **так** | "right", "OK"; taking stock |
| **слу́шай**, **слу́шайте** | "listen"; opens a request or a piece of news |
| **смотри́**, **смотри́те** | "look"; opens an explanation |
| **зна́ешь**, **понима́ешь** | "you know", "you see" |
| **представля́ешь**, **прики́нь** | "can you imagine", "get this"; the second is slang |
| **в смы́сле** | "I mean"; as a question, "what do you mean?" |
| **то есть** | "that is", "I mean"; as a question, "meaning?" |
| **ла́дно** | "OK"; closes a topic |

Here they are in their natural habitat, a story told in a hurry:

«**Коро́че**, иду́ я вчера́ домо́й, и тут, **прики́нь**, звони́т Ди́ма. **Ну**, я **ти́па** удиви́лась, он же никогда́ не звони́т. А он тако́й: „**Слу́шай**, тут тако́е де́ло… **В о́бщем**, мне ну́жен **э́тот… как его́…** перево́дчик“. Я говорю́: „**В смы́сле**? Я же не перево́дчик“. А он: „**Ну** ты же зна́ешь англи́йский“. **Вот**.»

"So basically, I'm walking home yesterday, and then, get this, Dima calls. Well, I was, like, surprised, he never calls. And he's like, 'Listen, here's the thing... Anyway, I need a, um, what's it called, a translator.' I say, 'What do you mean? I'm not a translator.' And he goes, 'Well, you know English, don't you.' So, yeah."

Notice the present tense for a past story (иду́, звони́т), and the verb before the subject (иду́ я, звони́т Ди́ма): both are typical of lively storytelling.

A few of these in use:

:::ex
**Слу́шай**, а у тебя́ есть заря́дка? | Listen, do you have a charger? |
**Смотри́**, тут всё про́сто. | Look, it's all quite simple. |
Дай мне **э́то… как его́…** пульт. | Hand me the, um, what's it called, the remote. |
— Я не приду́. — **В смы́сле**? | "I'm not coming." "What do you mean?" |
— Он уе́хал. — **То есть** как уе́хал? | "He's left." "What do you mean, left?" |
**Прики́нь**, он опя́ть опозда́л! | Can you believe it, he was late again! |
:::

## Reacting: good, bad and "wow"

One-word reactions, with their registers.

**Approval**

| Word | Roughly | Register |
|---|---|---|
| **отли́чно**, **здо́рово** | great | neutral; safe anywhere |
| **класс**, **кла́ссно** | great, awesome | colloquial; safe with anyone you say ты to |
| **кру́то** | cool | colloquial; very common |
| **су́пер** | super | colloquial |
| **прико́льно** | fun, neat, amusing | colloquial, youngish |
| **ого́нь** | fire, amazing | current youth slang |
| **обалде́ть**, **офиге́ть** | wow, no way, I'm floored | colloquial; the second is cruder |
| **ничего́ себе́** | wow, well I never | neutral |

Watch the stress in **здо́рово**: with the stress on the first syllable it means "great", but **здоро́во** is a hearty "hi" between men.

**Disapproval**

| Word | Roughly | Register |
|---|---|---|
| **у́жас**, **кошма́р** | awful, nightmare | neutral; everyone says them, often about trifles |
| **жесть** | brutal, insane, that's rough | slang; very widespread |
| **капе́ц** | disaster; I'm done for | colloquial |
| **ерунда́**, **чепуха́** | nonsense, rubbish | neutral |
| **фигня́** | crap, rubbish | crude colloquial |
| **[[ns:та́к себе]]**, **не о́чень** | so-so, not great | neutral; не о́чень is an understatement for "bad" |
| **ну тако́е** | meh | current slang |

**Mild expletives**

| Word | Roughly | Register |
|---|---|---|
| **блин** | darn, damn | literally "pancake"; ubiquitous and mild, but not for formal settings |
| **чёрт** | damn | literally "devil"; mild |
| **ёлки-па́лки** | good grief | literally "fir trees and sticks"; quaint and harmless |
| **ё-моё** | oh man | harmless |

:::ex
**Блин**, я забы́л ключи́! | Damn, I forgot my keys! |
— Я сдала́ экза́мен! — **Кру́то**! Поздравля́ю! | "I passed the exam!" "Cool! Congratulations!" |
— Он рабо́тает по шестна́дцать часо́в. — **Жесть**. | "He works sixteen-hour days." "That's brutal." |
— Как фильм? — **Ну тако́е**. | "How was the movie?" "Meh." |
**Офиге́ть**, ско́лько наро́ду! | Wow, look at the crowd! |
**Кошма́р**, опя́ть про́бки. | What a nightmare, traffic again. |
:::

A few verbs in the same spirit:

:::ex
Не **па́рься**! | Don't sweat it! | па́риться: to stress about something

Slang dates quickly, and using last decade's slang sounds worse than using none. When in doubt, use the neutral word.

## English in Russian

Russian has always borrowed, and right now it borrows from English at speed, especially online and at work. The borrowed root then gets Russian suffixes and prefixes and behaves like a native word.

| Loan | Meaning | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **оке́й**, **ок** | OK | universal |
| **лайк**, **ла́йкать / ла́йкнуть** | a like, to like (a post) | |
| **гу́глить / загу́глить** | to google | гу́глю, гу́глишь |
| **зум** | Zoom; a video call | office talk |
| **дедла́йн** | deadline | office talk |
| **хайп** | hype | |
| **кринж** | cringe | youth slang |
| **вайб** | vibe | youth slang |
| **краш** | a crush (the person) | youth slang |
| **чи́ллить** | to chill | youth slang |
| **со́рри** | sorry | light, for small things |
| **го** | let's go | youth slang: «Го в кино́?» |

:::ex
**Загу́гли**, во ско́лько они́ закрыва́ются. | Google what time they close. |
Она́ **ла́йкнула** мою́ фо́тку. | She liked my photo. |
Дава́й за́втра **созвони́мся** в зу́ме. | Let's have a call on Zoom tomorrow. |
У меня́ **дедла́йн** в пя́тницу. | I've got a deadline on Friday. |
Приложе́ние опя́ть **кра́шится**. | The app keeps crashing. | IT slang, from "crash"
:::

Загу́глить has a perfectivizing prefix, ла́йкнуть has the one-time suffix -ну-, and both conjugate like any Russian verb.

## Laughing and smiling in text

The Russian smiley is a bare closing parenthesis: **)**. It comes from :) with the colon dropped; the usual explanation is that the colon takes an awkward key combination on a Russian keyboard. More parentheses mean a bigger smile, and an opening parenthesis is a frown.

:::ex
[[ns:Спасибо)]] | Thanks :) | friendly
[[ns:Спасибо)))]] | Thanks :D | very warm
[[ns:Не получится(]] | It won't work out :( |
:::

The convention is so strong that among younger people a message with no parenthesis at all can read as serious or even annoyed. Older people and work emails use it less.

Laughter is typed as [[ns:**хаха**]], [[ns:**ахах**]], [[ns:**ахахах**]], in any length and often with the letters deliberately scrambled. Stronger amusement is **ржу** (literally "I'm neighing") or the more recent **ору́** ("I'm screaming"). The borrowed **лол** exists and feels a little dated.

The small noises are worth learning, because the English ones sound foreign in a Russian sentence.

| Sound | When |
|---|---|
| **ой** | surprise, a small mishap, sudden pain: "oh!", "oops!", "ouch!" |
| **ай** | sharp pain: "ow!" |
| **ох** | a sigh of weariness or worry |
| **ах** | delight or dismay: "ah!" |
| **ух ты** | admiration: "wow!" |
| **ого́** | amazement at size or amount: "whoa!" |
| **фу** | disgust: "yuck!", "ugh!" |
| **тьфу** | annoyance, as if spitting: "pah!" |

| **ага́** | a casual "yeah", "uh-huh"; also "aha!" |
| **угу́** | "mm-hm" |
| **не-а** | "nope" |
| **мда** | unimpressed: "hmm, well..." |
| **ура́** | "hooray!" |
| **алло́** | "hello?" on the phone only |

:::ex
**Ой**, извини́те! | Oh, I'm sorry! | you've stepped on someone's foot
**Ай**, бо́льно! | Ow, that hurts! |
**Ух ты**, как краси́во! | Wow, how beautiful! |
**Ого́**, ско́лько сне́га! | Whoa, look at all that snow! |
**Фу**, кака́я га́дость! | Yuck, how disgusting! |
**Эх**, жаль, что ле́то ко́нчилось. | Ah, what a shame summer's over. |

## A sober note on мат

Russian has a layer of obscene vocabulary with its own name: **мат**. The verb is **матери́ться**, "to swear using мат".

**What it is.** Мат is built on a very small number of roots, traditionally counted as four: words for the male and female sexual organs, for intercourse, and for a promiscuous woman. Russian word formation then goes to work on them. With prefixes and suffixes, those few roots yield hundreds of nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs that can stand in for nearly any word in the language. Fluent swearers can build whole sentences from them.

**How strong it is.** Much stronger than its English counterparts. English four-letter words now appear in newspapers, award speeches and office small talk. Мат does not. It is beeped out of television and masked with asterisks in print, Russian law restricts it in the media, in films and in public performances, and swearing in a public place can be fined as petty hooliganism. The social rule most Russians grew up with is that you do not use it in front of women, children, older people or strangers, or at work. Practice varies a great deal: many people swear freely among close friends, and for some it is punctuation. But the taboo is real, and a word that passes unnoticed in one company will freeze the room in another.

**Why to be careful.** A learner cannot easily judge that line. On top of that, мат in a foreign accent lands badly: it comes out either comic or much more aggressive than you intended. Until you know a group well, you gain little by using it and can lose a lot. Understanding it is another matter. You will hear it in the street and see it in comment sections. The particulars, for recognition only, are in [the section on мат](#s-07-09).

**What people say instead.** Because the real words are off limits so much of the time, Russian has a rich supply of stand-ins, and these you *will* need to recognize. Many are ordinary words chosen because they begin with the same sound as an obscene one:

| Euphemism | Literal meaning | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **блин** | pancake | Блин! (Damn!) |
| **ёлки-па́лки**, **ё-моё** | fir trees and sticks; nonsense syllables | exclamations of dismay |
| **чёрт** | devil | Чёрт! Чёрт возьми́! (Damn it!); an old curse of its own, not a stand-in |
| **фиг** | fig | фиг зна́ет (who knows), ни фига́ (not a thing), по́фиг (don't care), офиге́ть (wow) |
| **хрен** | horseradish | хрен зна́ет (who the hell knows), ни хрена́ (not a damn thing), хрено́во (lousy) |

In strength, they run roughly: чёрт and блин (mild; you'll hear them from schoolteachers) → фиг (crude but common) → хрен (cruder) → the real thing. The same frame takes each in turn. «Чёрт зна́ет», «фиг зна́ет» and «хрен зна́ет» all mean "who knows", at rising levels of rudeness, and there is an obscene version one step above.

If you want an expletive of your own, **блин** is the one. It is mild, it is everywhere, and it will never get you into trouble among friends.
