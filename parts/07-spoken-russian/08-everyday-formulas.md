# Everyday formulas

A good share of daily speech isn't built from grammar on the spot. It's pulled out ready-made: hello, thanks, sorry, happy birthday, who's last in line. These formulas are where a learner's Russian is most exposed, because there's no credit for creativity. Either you say what people say, or you say something odd.

This section is a catalog, organized by situation. Each formula has a register label where it matters: **formal** (strangers, officials, вы), **neutral** (safe anywhere), **casual** (friends, ты). The grammar inside the formulas is worth a look as well. Two patterns, a dropped **жела́ю** and a dropped **поздравля́ю**, explain dozens of phrases that otherwise look like random case endings.

## Hello

| Formula | Register | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Здра́вствуйте!** | formal, neutral | to anyone you call вы; the first в is silent |
| **Здра́вствуй!** | neutral | to one person you call ты; a little weighty, typical of an older person to a younger one |
| **До́брое у́тро! До́брый день! До́брый ве́чер!** | neutral | polite and warm at once; the standard opening in shops and work emails |
| **Приве́т!** | casual | the default among friends |
| **Приве́тик!** | casual, cute | |
| **Здоро́во!** | casual | mostly between men |
| **Здра́сте!** | casual-neutral | здра́вствуйте at normal speed |

You greet a person once a day. Saying здра́вствуйте a second time to someone you've already seen that morning is a small gaffe, and they may answer «Мы уже́ здоро́вались» ("We've already said hello").

Some extras for particular moments:

:::ex
Добро́ пожа́ловать! | Welcome! |
С прие́здом! | Welcome! Glad you made it! | to someone who's just arrived from a trip
Каки́е лю́ди! | Look who's here! | casual, delighted
Ско́лько лет, ско́лько зим! | Long time no see! | literally "how many summers, how many winters"
:::

:::note До́брой но́чи and споко́йной но́чи
**Споко́йной но́чи** ("peaceful night") is what you say to someone going to bed. **До́брой но́чи** is a polite goodbye late in the evening, and some people also use it as a late-night greeting. Neither is a way to say hello at 9 p.m.; that's до́брый ве́чер.
:::

## How are you?

:::ex
Как дела́? | How are things? | the standard; neutral to casual
Как у вас дела́? | How are things with you? | to вы
Как ты? | How are you doing? | casual; warmer, asks about the person
Как жизнь? | How's life? | casual
Что но́вого? | What's new? | casual
Как пожива́ете? | How are you keeping? | polite, somewhat old-fashioned
:::

The important thing here is cultural. **In Russian this is a real question.** You don't say «Как дела́?» to a cashier or toss it at a colleague as you pass in the corridor. You ask people you actually know, and you wait for an answer. The answer, in turn, is expected to be at least slightly informative. A beaming "Great!" every time sounds hollow.

| Answer | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Норма́льно.** | Fine, OK. The default, and positive: not "mediocre". |
| **Хорошо́, спаси́бо.** | Good, thanks. |
| **Всё хорошо́. Всё в поря́дке.** | Everything's fine. |
| **Отли́чно!** | Great! |
| **Ничего́.** | Not bad. All right. |
| **Непло́хо.** | Not bad. |
| **Потихо́ньку.** | Getting along, little by little. |
| **Да всё по-ста́рому.** | Oh, same as ever. |
| **[[ns:Та́к себе.]]** | So-so. |
| **Не о́чень.** | Not great. |
| **Быва́ло и лу́чше.** | I've been better. |
| **Лу́чше всех!** | Better than everyone! A cheerful joke. |

Then you return the question: **А у тебя́? А ты как? А у вас?**

:::dialogue
А: Приве́т! Как дела́? | Hi! How are things?
Б: Да норма́льно, рабо́таю мно́го. А ты как? | Fine, working a lot. How about you?
А: То́же ничего́. Вот, в о́тпуск собира́юсь. | Not bad either. Getting ready to go on vacation, actually.
:::

## Goodbye

| Formula | Register | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **До свида́ния!** | formal, neutral | literally "until the meeting" |
| **Всего́ до́брого! Всего́ хоро́шего!** | neutral, polite | "all the best"; a pleasant way to end a phone call or leave a shop |
| **Хоро́шего дня! Хоро́шего ве́чера! Хоро́ших выходны́х!** | neutral | "have a nice day / evening / weekend" |
| **До за́втра! До понеде́льника! До встре́чи!** | neutral | "see you tomorrow / Monday / soon" |
| **До свя́зи!** | neutral | "talk soon"; when the next contact will be by phone or online |
| **Пока́!** | casual | the default among friends; doubled, **Пока́-пока́!**, it's breezier |
| **Дава́й!** | casual | "OK, bye"; very common, especially at the end of calls |
| **Уви́димся! Созвони́мся! Спи́шемся!** | casual | "we'll see each other / call / text" |
| **Счастли́во!** | casual, warm | "all the best" |
| **Уда́чи!** | casual-neutral | "good luck" |
| **Береги́ себя́!** | warm | "take care of yourself"; meant sincerely |

To get out of a conversation you announce it first: «Ну ла́дно, мне пора́» (Right, I have to go), «Ну всё, я побежа́л» / «я побежа́ла» (OK, I'm off, literally "I've run"). A Russian goodbye often runs through several formulas in a row: «Ну всё, дава́й, пока́, созвони́мся!»

One to avoid: **проща́й** / **проща́йте** means "farewell", for partings you expect to be permanent. Said at the end of an ordinary evening, it would be alarming.

## Thank you, and you're welcome

:::ex
Спаси́бо! | Thank you! | neutral, for everything
Спаси́бо большо́е! Большо́е спаси́бо! | Thank you very much! |
Огро́мное спаси́бо! | Thanks a million! |
Спаси́бо за по́мощь. | Thank you for your help. | за + acc.
Спаси́бо, что пришли́. | Thank you for coming. |
Благодарю́ вас. | I thank you. | formal, a little elevated
Я вам о́чень благода́рен. Я вам о́чень благода́рна. | I'm very grateful to you. |
:::

The replies:

| Reply | Use |
|---|---|
| **Пожа́луйста.** | the universal "you're welcome" |
| **[[ns:Не́ за что.]]** | "don't mention it", literally "there's nothing to thank for" |
| **Да [[ns:не́ за что]]. Да ла́дно.** | casual: "oh, it's nothing" |
| **Рад помо́чь. Ра́да помо́чь.** | "glad to help" |
| **Обраща́йтесь! Обраща́йся!** | "any time!", literally "do turn to me" |
| **Не сто́ит.** | formal: "it's not worth thanks" |
| **На здоро́вье!** | "to your health": mainly for food, drink or a treat |

**На здоро́вье** is what a host says when you thank her for dinner, or what you say when someone thanks you for the candy you shared. It is not a reply to "thanks for the report".

The word **пожа́луйста** itself has four jobs: "please" with a request, "you're welcome" after thanks, "here you are" when handing something over, and "go ahead" when granting permission.

:::dialogue
А: Мо́жно? | May I?
Б: Пожа́луйста. | Go ahead.
А: Спаси́бо. | Thank you.
Б: Пожа́луйста. | You're welcome.
:::

## Sorry

| Formula | Register | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Извини́! Извини́те!** | neutral | for small offenses, and to get someone's attention |
| **Прости́! Прости́те!** | neutral | the same, and also for real guilt; a shade more heartfelt |
| **Прошу́ проще́ния.** | formal | "I beg your pardon" |
| **Винова́т. Винова́та.** | neutral | "my fault"; slightly old-fashioned, with a military ring |
| **Со́рри!** | casual | for trifles, among friends |

To say what you're sorry for, use **за** with the accusative, or a **что** clause:

:::ex
Извини́те за опозда́ние. | Sorry for being late. |
Извини́те за беспоко́йство. | Sorry to bother you. |
Прости́, что не позвони́л. | Sorry I didn't call. |
Прости́, я не хоте́ла тебя́ оби́деть. | I'm sorry, I didn't mean to hurt you. |
Я неча́янно! | I didn't do it on purpose! |
:::

Remember that «Мне о́чень жаль» is sympathy or regret, not an apology. If you step on someone's foot, it's «Извини́те!».

The replies:

:::ex
Ничего́. Ничего́ стра́шного. | It's nothing. No harm done. |
Быва́ет. | It happens. |
Всё в поря́дке. Всё норма́льно. | It's all right. |
Да ла́дно, ерунда́. | Oh, forget it, it's nothing. | casual
Прое́хали. | Water under the bridge. Let's move on. | casual; literally "we've driven past"
:::

## Asking for things

English softens requests with "could you" and "would you mind". Russian's basic tool is simpler: **an imperative plus пожа́луйста is already polite.** «Дай, пожа́луйста, ру́чку» is not a command barked at a friend; it's the ordinary courteous way to ask. English speakers tend to over-engineer here.

Above that baseline, there is a ladder:

:::ex
Дай, пожа́луйста, ру́чку. | Give me a pen, please. | to ты; normal
Переда́йте, пожа́луйста, соль. | Pass the salt, please. | to вы; normal
Мо́жно меню́? | Could I have the menu? | мо́жно + the thing you want
Мо́жно мне ко́фе? | Can I get a coffee? |
Мо́жно войти́? | May I come in? | мо́жно + infinitive
Мо́жно вас попроси́ть закры́ть окно́? | Could I ask you to close the window? |
Ты не помо́жешь мне? | You couldn't help me, could you? | a negated question is a polite one
Ты не мог бы мне помо́чь? | Could you help me? | to a man; не могла́ бы to a woman
Не могли́ бы вы говори́ть ме́дленнее? | Could you speak more slowly? | to вы; quite polite
Е́сли вам не тру́дно, закро́йте дверь. | If it's no trouble, close the door. |
:::

For asking strangers, three openers cover nearly everything:

:::ex
**Скажи́те, пожа́луйста**, где здесь туале́т? | Excuse me, where is the restroom here? |
**Вы не подска́жете**, как пройти́ к вокза́лу? | Could you tell me how to get to the train station? |
**Вы не зна́ете**, где здесь банкома́т? | Do you happen to know where there's an ATM around here? |
:::

**Вы не подска́жете** is the classic. The verb подсказа́ть means "to give a hint", so you are asking for only a tiny favor, and the не makes it politer still.

In shops, cafés and on the phone you'll also hear **Бу́дьте добры́** ("be so kind"): «Бу́дьте добры́, два биле́та до Каза́ни.» It's polite and slightly old-school. To order, the simplest formula is the dative with no verb: «**Мне, пожа́луйста,** капучи́но и круасса́н.»

Typical answers to a request: **Коне́чно** (of course), **Да, пожа́луйста** (yes, certainly), **Сейча́с** (right away), **Мину́ту** (just a minute), **Без пробле́м** (no problem; casual), **К сожале́нию, нет** (I'm afraid not).

## Wishes: the genitive after a silent жела́ю

The verb **жела́ть / пожела́ть** (to wish) takes the dative of the person and the **genitive** of the thing wished:

:::ex
Жела́ю тебе́ сча́стья. | I wish you happiness. |
Жела́ю вам успе́хов. | I wish you success. |
:::

In everyday wishes жела́ю is simply dropped, and the genitive is left standing on its own. That's the whole secret behind a long list of phrases you may have learned as unexplained chunks:

| Wish | Meaning | Nominative it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Прия́тного аппети́та!** | Enjoy your meal! | прия́тный аппети́т |
| **Споко́йной но́чи!** | Good night! | споко́йная ночь |
| **Счастли́вого пути́!** | Have a good trip! | счастли́вый путь |
| **Уда́чи!** | Good luck! | уда́ча |
| **Всего́ до́брого!** | All the best! | всё до́брое |
| **Всего́ хоро́шего!** | All the best! | всё хоро́шее |
| **Хоро́шего дня!** | Have a nice day! | хоро́ший день |
| **Хоро́ших выходны́х!** | Have a good weekend! | хоро́шие выходны́е |
| **Прия́тного о́тдыха!** | Enjoy your vacation! | прия́тный о́тдых |
| **Сла́дких снов!** | Sweet dreams! | сла́дкие сны |
| **Здоро́вья!** | Good health to you! | здоро́вье |
| **Скоре́йшего выздоровле́ния!** | Get well soon! | скоре́йшее выздоровле́ние |

Compare the greetings **До́брое у́тро** and **До́брый день**, which are in the nominative: they are statements ("[it is a] good morning"), not wishes. That is why it's до́брый ве́чер when you arrive but до́брой но́чи when you leave.

Once you know the pattern you can make your own: «Хоро́шей доро́ги!», «Хоро́шего полёта!», «Хоро́шего настрое́ния!»

To wish someone an action, use an infinitive or что́бы: «Жела́ю хорошо́ отдохну́ть!» (Have a good rest!), «Жела́ю, что́бы всё получи́лось!» (I hope it all works out!).

The standard replies to a wish are **Спаси́бо!**, **И тебе́!** / **И вам!** (You too!), **И вам того́ же!** (The same to you!) and **Взаи́мно!** (Likewise!).

:::note Ни пу́ха ни пера́!
Before an exam, an interview or a performance, the traditional thing to say is not a plain "good luck", which might tempt fate, but **«Ни пу́ха ни пера́!»**, an old hunters' wish of "neither fur nor feather", wishing you the opposite of what you want so as to fool the evil spirits. The one correct reply is **«К чёрту!»** ("To the devil!"). Don't say спаси́бо; it spoils the charm.
:::

## Congratulations: the instrumental after a silent поздравля́ю

The verb **поздравля́ть / поздра́вить** (to congratulate) takes the accusative of the person and **с** with the **instrumental** of the occasion:

:::ex
Поздравля́ю тебя́ с днём рожде́ния! | Happy birthday! | literally "I congratulate you with the day of birth"
:::

Drop поздравля́ю, and you have the Russian formula for every holiday and happy event:

| Formula | Occasion |
|---|---|
| **С днём рожде́ния!** | birthday |
| **С Но́вым го́дом!** | New Year |
| **С Рождество́м!** | Christmas (January 7 in the Orthodox calendar) |
| **С Восьмы́м ма́рта!** | March 8, International Women's Day |
| **С Па́схой!** | Easter |
| **С Днём Побе́ды!** | Victory Day, May 9 |
| **С пра́здником!** | any holiday at all: the all-purpose one |
| **С наступа́ющим!** | "happy upcoming (holiday)": said in the days before, above all before New Year |
| **С годовщи́ной!** | anniversary |
| **С новосе́льем!** | moving into a new home |
| **С повыше́нием!** | a promotion |
| **С рожде́нием сы́на! С рожде́нием до́чки!** | the birth of a son, of a daughter |
| **С прие́здом! С возвраще́нием!** | arrival; coming back |

**Поздравля́ю!** on its own is "Congratulations!".

A full birthday greeting combines both patterns, the instrumental and then the genitive: «С днём рожде́ния! Жела́ю сча́стья, здоро́вья и всего́ са́мого лу́чшего!» (Happy birthday! I wish you happiness, health and all the very best!)

:::aside The calendar
Но́вый год is the big family holiday, with the decorated tree, the presents and Дед Моро́з (Grandfather Frost); Christmas comes a week later and is quieter. On March 8 women get flowers from every man they know, and saying «С Восьмы́м ма́рта!» to female colleagues, friends and relatives is close to obligatory. One superstition to respect: don't congratulate anyone on a birthday *before* the day. It's bad luck.

One more formula with the same grammar is **«С лёгким па́ром!»** ("with light steam"), said to someone who has just come out of the bathhouse, or these days the shower.
:::

## Toasts

A toast is **за** plus the **accusative**: you drink *for* something.

:::ex
За тебя́! За вас! | To you! |
За здоро́вье! | To health! |
За встре́чу! | To our getting together! | a natural first toast
За хозя́йку! | To our hostess! |
За роди́телей! | To our parents! |
За любо́вь! | To love! |
За дру́жбу! | To friendship! |
За именни́нника! | To the birthday boy! | за именни́нницу for a woman
За то, что́бы все бы́ли здоро́вы! | To everyone staying healthy! | за то, что́бы + past form, for a whole wish
:::

At a proper Russian table a toast is a short speech, and nobody drinks until one has been said. After it, everyone clinks glasses (**чо́каться**) and drinks. Among friends who have already said everything, toasts shrink to almost nothing:

:::ex
Бу́дем! | Cheers! | short for бу́дем здоро́вы, "let us be healthy"
Ну, дава́йте! | Well, here goes! |
Ну, за нас! | Well, to us! |
:::

:::warn «На здоро́вье» is not a toast
Every foreign film has a Russian character raise a glass and say «На здоро́вье!». Russians don't. As you saw above, на здоро́вье is a reply to thanks for food. The toast is **За здоро́вье!** or **Ва́ше здоро́вье!** (The confusion probably comes from Polish, where *na zdrowie* really is "cheers".)
:::

## At the table

:::ex
Проходи́те, раздева́йтесь! | Come in, take your coat off! | the standard welcome at the door
Чу́вствуйте себя́ как до́ма. | Make yourself at home. |
Угоща́йтесь! Угоща́йся! | Help yourself! |
Прия́тного аппети́та! | Enjoy your meal! |
Бери́те ещё! | Have some more! |
Вам положи́ть ещё? | Can I give you some more? |
Спаси́бо, я уже́ сыт. Я сыта́. | Thank you, I'm full. |
О́чень вку́сно! | It's delicious! |
Спаси́бо, всё бы́ло о́чень вку́сно. | Thank you, everything was delicious. | on getting up from the table
:::

The host's answer to that last line is, at last, **«На здоро́вье!»**

Expect to be offered food several times, and expect your first "no, thank you" to be ignored. Refusing takes persistence; praising the food is never wrong.

## On the phone

:::ex
Алло́? | Hello? | the phone-only word; often pronounced алё
Да? Слу́шаю. | Yes? Speaking. | brisker
Ма́ша, приве́т, э́то А́ня. | Masha, hi, it's Anya. |
Здра́вствуйте, вас беспоко́ит Дми́трий из ба́нка. | Hello, this is Dmitry calling from the bank. | formal; literally "is disturbing you"
А Ма́шу мо́жно? | Is Masha there? | accusative: "may I [have] Masha?"
Мину́ту, сейча́с позову́. | One moment, I'll get her. |
Её сейча́с нет. [[ns:Что́-нибудь]] переда́ть? | She's not here right now. Can I take a message? |
Тебе́ удо́бно говори́ть? | Is this a good time to talk? |
Я тебя́ пло́хо слы́шу. | I can't hear you well. |
Ты пропада́ешь. | You're breaking up. |
Перезвони́ мне, пожа́луйста. | Call me back, please. |
Я перезвоню́ че́рез пять мину́т. | I'll call back in five minutes. |
Вы оши́блись но́мером. | You have the wrong number. |
:::

Calls end with a familiar chain: «Ну ла́дно, дава́й, пока́!» To family and close friends people add **Целу́ю!** (Kisses!) or **Обнима́ю!** (Hugs!).

## Messages and email

With friends, a chat opens with «Приве́т!» and gets to the point, often via «Слу́шай, …». A considerate opener before a longer request is «Приве́т! Не отвлека́ю?» (Hi! Am I interrupting?) or «Есть мину́тка?» (Got a minute?).

To someone you call вы, start with a greeting and the name: «Здра́вствуйте, А́нна Серге́евна!» or «До́брый день, А́нна!» Truly formal letters open with **Уважа́емый** / **Уважа́емая** ("respected") and the name and patronymic, and close with **С уваже́нием** ("with respect") and your name, which is the equivalent of "Best regards".

Short replies you'll type every day:

:::ex
Хорошо́. Ок. | OK. |
Договори́лись! | Deal! Agreed! |
По́нял. Поняла́. | Got it. |
Спаси́бо, сейча́с посмотрю́. | Thanks, I'll have a look now. |
Уже́ выхожу́. | Leaving right now. |
Опа́здываю на де́сять мину́т, извини́! | Running ten minutes late, sorry! |
Я на ме́сте. | I'm here. |
Напиши́, когда́ дое́дешь. | Text me when you get there. |
Зара́нее спаси́бо! | Thanks in advance! |
:::

## Reactions and sympathy

Small responses keep a conversation alive. These are the ones to have on the tip of your tongue.

:::ex
Будь здоро́в! Будь здоро́ва! Бу́дьте здоро́вы! | Bless you! | after a sneeze; the reply is спаси́бо
Выздора́вливай! Выздора́вливайте! | Get well soon! |
Сочу́вствую. | I sympathize. That's tough. |
Держи́сь! | Hang in there! |
Соболе́зную. Прими́те мои́ соболе́знования. | My condolences. Please accept my condolences. |
Молоде́ц! | Well done! Good for you! | to a man or a woman; plural молодцы́
У́мница! | Clever you! Good girl, good boy! | warm; to either sex
Ничего́ себе́! | Wow! |
Не мо́жет быть! | It can't be! |

Како́й у́жас! | How awful! |

Коне́чно! Обяза́тельно! | Of course! Definitely! |

Посмо́трим. | We'll see. |
:::

## In shops, lines and on public transport

Public life has its own short scripts. Knowing them saves a lot of bewilderment.

:::ex
Вы выхо́дите? | Are you getting off? | on a crowded bus or metro: it means "let me through, I am"
Разреши́те пройти́. | Excuse me, may I get by? |
Переда́йте, пожа́луйста. | Pass it along, please. | handing your fare forward in a minibus
Останови́те здесь, пожа́луйста. | Stop here, please. |
Здесь свобо́дно? | Is this seat free? |
Сади́тесь, пожа́луйста. | Please, have a seat. | giving up your seat
Кто после́дний? | Who's last? | joining a line
Вы стои́те? | Are you in line? |
Вам помо́чь? | Can I help you? | offering a hand with a suitcase or a stroller
Вам подсказа́ть? | Can I help you find something? | a shop assistant
Спаси́бо, я про́сто смотрю́. | Thanks, I'm just looking. |
:::

**«Кто после́дний?»** deserves a comment. In a clinic, a post office or any place where the line isn't a visible row of people, you walk in and ask the room who is last. Someone says «Я», you answer «Я за ва́ми» (I'm after you), and your place is now held, even if you step away for a minute.

At the checkout you'll hear the same handful of questions every time:

:::dialogue
Касси́р: Здра́вствуйте. Паке́т ну́жен? | Hello. Do you need a bag?
Са́ша: Да, оди́н, пожа́луйста. | Yes, one, please.
Касси́р: Ка́рта магази́на есть? | Do you have a store card?
Са́ша: Нет. | No.
Касси́р: С вас семьсо́т два́дцать рубле́й. Ка́ртой? | That's seven hundred and twenty rubles. By card?
Са́ша: Ка́ртой. | By card.
Касси́р: Прикла́дывайте. Чек ну́жен? | Go ahead and tap. Do you need the receipt?
Са́ша: Нет, спаси́бо. До свида́ния! | No, thanks. Goodbye!
Касси́р: Всего́ до́брого! | All the best!
:::

In a café the equivalent question is «**С собо́й и́ли здесь?**» (To go or for here?).

## Putting it together

А́ня calls Ма́ша on her birthday. Nearly every line is a formula from this section, and that is exactly how such a call goes.

:::dialogue
Ма́ша: Алло́? | Hello?
А́ня: Ма́ша, приве́т! Э́то А́ня. Тебе́ удо́бно говори́ть? | Masha, hi! It's Anya. Is this a good time?
Ма́ша: О, Ань, приве́т! Да, коне́чно. | Oh, Anya, hi! Yes, of course.
А́ня: С днём рожде́ния! Жела́ю тебе́ сча́стья, здоро́вья и что́бы все мечты́ сбыва́лись! | Happy birthday! I wish you happiness, health, and may all your dreams come true!
Ма́ша: Спаси́бо большо́е! Мне о́чень прия́тно. Ты придёшь в суббо́ту? | Thank you so much! That's so nice. Are you coming on Saturday?
А́ня: Обяза́тельно! [[ns:Что́-нибудь]] принести́? | Definitely! Shall I bring anything?
Ма́ша: Да ничего́ не на́до, про́сто приходи́. | Oh, no need for anything, just come.
А́ня: Договори́лись. Ну всё, не бу́ду тебя́ отвлека́ть. Ещё раз поздравля́ю! | Deal. OK, I won't keep you. Congratulations once again!
Ма́ша: Спаси́бо! Дава́й, до суббо́ты. Целу́ю! | Thanks! OK, see you Saturday. Kisses!
А́ня: Пока́-пока́! | Bye-bye!
:::

:::summary
- «Как дела́?» is a real question: ask people you know, and answer with something truer than "great". **Норма́льно** is the default.
- An imperative plus **пожа́луйста** is already polite. For strangers: **Вы не подска́жете…?**
- Wishes are bare genitives after a silent жела́ю: Прия́тного аппети́та! Уда́чи! Всего́ до́брого!
- Congratulations are **с** + instrumental after a silent поздравля́ю: С днём рожде́ния! С пра́здником!
- Toasts are **за** + accusative: За тебя́! За встре́чу! And **на здоро́вье** is what a host says when thanked for food, never a toast.
:::
