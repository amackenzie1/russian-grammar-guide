# Hedging, seeming and hearsay

Nothing here is a new idea: English also says "probably", "it seems", "they say" and "it turns out". The section exists for three reasons. Several of the Russian words mislead English speakers (**наве́рное** looks like "surely" and means "probably"), Russian has dedicated little words for secondhand information that English has no compact match for, and the comma rules for all of them are specific.

Russian grammar calls most of these **вво́дные слова́**, "parenthetical words". They stand outside the structure of the sentence: they change no endings, they can usually move around freely, and in writing most of them are fenced off with commas.

:::vocab
наве́рное, наве́рно | probably |
мо́жет быть, мо́жет | maybe |
вряд ли | unlikely, hardly |
ка́жется | it seems; I think |
по-мо́ему | in my opinion, I think |
вро́де (бы) | seems like; as far as I know | colloquial
похо́же | it looks like |
ви́димо | evidently, apparently |
говоря́т | they say, people say |
я́кобы | supposedly, allegedly |
ока́зывается | it turns out |
:::

## How sure are you?

The scale of certainty, from the top:

| Word | Roughly | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **то́чно** | definitely, for sure | |
| **коне́чно** | of course | ч is pronounced "ш" |
| **наверняка́** | almost certainly, bound to | |
| **скоре́е всего́** | most likely | |
| **наве́рное**, **наве́рно** | probably | the default; the two spellings are equal |
| **пожа́луй** | I suppose, I guess | inclining toward yes |
| **мо́жет быть**, **мо́жет** | maybe | plain мо́жет is the spoken form |
| **вряд ли** | unlikely, I doubt it | |
| **едва́ ли** | hardly | bookish |

:::ex
Он, **наве́рное**, уже́ спит. | He's probably asleep already. |
Она́ **наверняка́** зна́ет. | She's bound to know. |
**Мо́жет**, не на́до? | Maybe we shouldn't? |
**Вряд ли** он согласи́тся. | He's unlikely to agree. |
**Пожа́луй**, я возьму́ ры́бу. | I think I'll have the fish. |
:::

:::warn Наве́рное is not "surely"
Наве́рное looks as if it ought to mean "for certain": it contains ве́рно, "true". A century ago it did. In modern Russian it means **probably**, no more. If you tell someone «Я, наве́рное, приду́», you have not promised to come. "For certain" is **наверняка́** or **то́чно**.
:::

Any of these works as a complete answer: «Ты придёшь?» — «Скоре́е всего́». One more belongs on the scale: colloquial **по иде́е** means "in theory, if things are as they should be": «По иде́е, он уже́ до́лжен быть до́ма» (By rights he should be home by now), which usually hints that things may not be as they should.

## It seems

### Ка́жется and мне ка́жется

Dropped into a sentence, **ка́жется**, from the verb **каза́ться** (to seem), means "I think" or "it seems", with a clear note of uncertainty:

:::ex
**Ка́жется**, дождь начина́ется. | I think it's starting to rain. |
Я, **ка́жется**, забы́л ключи́. | I seem to have forgotten my keys. |
:::

With a dative, **мне ка́жется, (что)…** is the standard gentle way to give an opinion. Russians use it where an English speaker would say "I think" or "I feel":

:::ex
**Мне ка́жется**, ты не прав. | I think you're wrong. |
**Тебе́ не ка́жется**, что здесь хо́лодно? | Don't you think it's cold in here? |
:::

The perfective **показа́ться** is for a single impression, often a mistaken one:

:::ex
**Мне показа́лось**, что кто-то стучи́т. | I thought I heard someone knocking. |
**Тебе́ показа́лось**. | You imagined it. |
:::

As a full verb with a subject, каза́ться takes the instrumental of what the subject seems to be, and so does **вы́глядеть** (to look):

:::ex
Он **ка́жется уста́лым**. | He seems tired. |
Ты **вы́глядишь уста́лой**. | You look tired. | to a woman
:::

Notice the difference between «Он ка́жется уста́лым» and «Он, ка́жется, уста́л». In the first, he gives an impression of tiredness. In the second, I believe he's tired but I'm not sure. The commas are the signal.

### По-мо́ему

**По-мо́ему** is "in my opinion", and it is much lighter than the English phrase. It's the casual "I think", "if you ask me". The other persons work too: **по-тво́ему**, **по-ва́шему**.

:::ex
**По-мо́ему**, э́то плоха́я иде́я. | I think that's a bad idea. |
А **по-тво́ему**, кто прав? | And who do you think is right? |
:::

### Вро́де

**Вро́де**, also **вро́де бы**, is the colloquial workhorse. It says: this is my impression, or what I seem to remember, and I won't swear to it.

:::ex
Он **вро́де** в Москве́ живёт. | He lives in Moscow, I think. |
**Вро́де бы** всё взя́ли. | We seem to have taken everything. |
— Он придёт? — **Вро́де** да. | "Is he coming?" "Seems so." |
:::

### Похо́же and ви́димо

When you are concluding something from evidence in front of you, the words are different. **Похо́же (, что)** is "it looks like", and **ви́димо** is "evidently": I'm working this out.

:::ex
**Похо́же**, бу́дет дождь. | Looks like rain. |
— Он оби́делся? — **Похо́же на то**. | "Is he offended?" "Looks that way." |
Он не отвеча́ет. **Ви́димо**, за́нят. | He's not answering. He must be busy. |
:::

**Как бу́дто** (as if) also works as a hedge inside the sentence: «Ты как бу́дто похуде́л» (You seem to have lost weight).

## I heard: marking hearsay

If the information isn't yours, Russian gives you ways to say so, and to say how far you trust it.

**Говоря́т** is neutral: "they say", "I hear". It is a verb without a subject, the [indefinite-personal](#s-06-09) pattern.

:::ex
**Говоря́т**, зима́ бу́дет холо́дная. | They say it's going to be a cold winter. |
:::

To name the source there is **по слова́м** plus the genitive, "according to", and for unnamed sources **по слу́хам**, "rumor has it":

:::ex
**По слова́м сосе́дей**, он уе́хал ме́сяц наза́д. | According to the neighbors, he left a month ago. |
:::

Now the skeptical ones. **Я́кобы** reports a claim and declines to vouch for it: "supposedly", "allegedly".

:::ex
Он **я́кобы** боле́л. | He was supposedly ill. | that's his story
:::

**Бу́дто бы** does the same job: «Говоря́т, бу́дто бы он уезжа́ет» (They say he's supposedly leaving). And **так называ́емый** is "so-called": «его́ так называ́емые друзья́» (his so-called friends).

Then there is a little quoting particle, **мол**. It shows that the words are someone else's and gives only their gist. English "he's like" and "as if to say" come close.

:::ex
Он отказа́лся: **мол**, вре́мени нет. | He said no: no time, he says. |
Она́ посмотре́ла на меня́ — **мол**, что ты тут де́лаешь? | She gave me a look, as if to say "what are you doing here?" |
Объясни́л, что, **мол**, не знал. | He explained that he hadn't known, or so he says. |
:::

**Де́скать** means the same and sounds old-fashioned or ironic.

## It turns out: ока́зывается

**Ока́зывается** marks information the speaker has just learned and didn't expect: "it turns out", "apparently", "what do you know". English needs a clause for this and usually puts it first; Russian has one word and drops it anywhere, including the middle of the sentence.

:::ex
**Ока́зывается**, он жена́т! | Turns out he's married! |
Она́, **ока́зывается**, говори́т по-ру́сски. | She speaks Russian, as it turns out. |
Ты, **ока́зывается**, уме́ешь гото́вить! | So you can cook, it turns out! |
:::

Ока́зывается is present tense and belongs to the moment of discovery. When you're telling a story in the past, use **оказа́лось (, что)**:

:::ex
**Оказа́лось**, что я перепу́тал день. | It turned out I'd mixed up the day. |
Мы ду́мали, что э́то далеко́, а **оказа́лось** — ря́дом. | We thought it was far, but it turned out to be right nearby. |
:::

The verb behind it, **ока́зываться / оказа́ться**, also takes a normal subject, with the instrumental for what it turns out to be: «Он оказа́лся врачо́м» (He turned out to be a doctor).

## Words for attitude and for steering

These work the way their English counterparts do, so a list is enough. The last column says whether the word is set off by commas.

| Word | Meaning | Commas? |
|---|---|---|
| **к сожале́нию** / **к сча́стью** | unfortunately / fortunately | yes |
| **че́стно говоря́**, **е́сли че́стно** | to be honest | yes |
| **кста́ти** | by the way, speaking of which | yes |
| **ме́жду про́чим** | by the way; I'll have you know | yes |
| **вообще́-то** | actually, as a matter of fact | no |
| **на са́мом де́ле** | in reality, actually | no |
| **в о́бщем** | all in all; anyway | yes |
| **в при́нципе** | basically; in principle | no |
| **коро́че** | in short; "so basically" | yes |
| **зна́чит** | so; that means | yes |
| **так сказа́ть** | so to speak | yes |
| **ска́жем**, **допу́стим** | say; let's suppose | yes |
| **наприме́р** | for example | yes |
| **во-пе́рвых**, **во-вторы́х**, **в-тре́тьих** | first, second, third | yes |
| **с одно́й стороны́… с друго́й стороны́** | on the one hand... on the other | yes |

:::ex
Я, **ме́жду про́чим**, то́же уста́ла. | I'm tired too, I'll have you know. |
**Вообще́-то** я вегетариа́нец. | Actually, I'm a vegetarian. | you seem to have missed this
**На са́мом де́ле** всё бы́ло не так. | In reality it wasn't like that at all. |
:::

Two pairs share one English translation. **Кста́ти** is the neutral "by the way", while ме́жду про́чим usually has a point to it, a fact the listener has been ignoring. Вообще́-то raises an objection, and на са́мом де́ле contrasts the truth with an appearance: «Вообще́-то я здесь рабо́таю» is what you say to someone treating you like an intruder, «На са́мом де́ле я здесь рабо́таю» when everyone thought you were a customer.

Several of these words, especially коро́че, зна́чит and в о́бщем, also serve as pure [fillers](#s-07-07).

## Как бы and ти́па

**Как бы** literally means "as it were", "in a way", and used with that meaning it is beyond reproach: «Он как бы шу́тит, но не совсе́м» (He's sort of joking, but not entirely). **Ти́па**, the genitive of тип (type), is colloquial for "kind of, like": «Он там ти́па нача́льник» (He's kind of the boss there).

The trouble is that both have become automatic filler, sprinkled into sentences where nothing is being compared to anything:

:::ex
Я **как бы** не про́тив. | I'm, like, not against it. |
Мы **ти́па** встреча́емся. | We're, like, dating. |
:::

Teachers, editors and parents call them **слова́-парази́ты** ("parasite words") and mock them without mercy. The situation is the same as with English "like": recognize them, and keep them out of anything formal. Neither takes commas.

## Commas

The written rule is simple to state: a parenthetical word is set off by commas, on both sides if it is in the middle of the sentence. The test is whether you can lift the word out without disturbing the grammar of what remains.

:::ex
Он, наве́рное, до́ма. | He's probably home. |
Ты, коне́чно, прав. | You are, of course, right. |
:::

The same word can be a parenthetical in one sentence and a working part of another, and then the commas come and go:

| Parenthetical, with commas | Part of the sentence, no commas |
|---|---|
| Он, **ка́жется**, уста́л. (I think he's tired.) | Он **ка́жется** уста́лым. (He seems tired.) |
| **Мо́жет быть**, он придёт. (Maybe he'll come.) | Э́то не **мо́жет быть** пра́вдой. (This can't be true.) |
| **Зна́чит**, ты согла́сен? (So you agree?) | Что **зна́чит** э́то сло́во? (What does this word mean?) |
| **Говоря́т**, он уе́хал. (They say he left.) | Все **говоря́т** об э́том. (Everyone's talking about it.) |

A number of hedging words are not parentheticals in the grammatical sense. They are adverbs and particles, and they take no commas: **наверняка́**, **то́чно**, **вряд ли**, **я́кобы**, **как бу́дто**, **вообще́-то**, **на са́мом де́ле**, **в при́нципе**, and usually **вро́де**. Russians get these wrong all the time, so you needn't lose sleep over them. In messages, people leave out half the commas anyway.

## Office gossip

Everything in this conversation is secondhand, guessed or just discovered, and every line says which.

:::dialogue
А: Слу́шай, а где И́горь? Его́ вро́де сего́дня [[ns:не́ было]]. | Listen, where's Igor? I don't think he was in today.
Б: Говоря́т, он увольня́ется. | They say he's quitting.
А: Да ла́дно! Он же, ка́жется, то́лько что получи́л повыше́ние. | No way! I thought he'd just gotten a promotion.
Б: Ока́зывается, нет. Повыше́ние, по слу́хам, да́ли Ле́не. А ему́ я́кобы сказа́ли: мол, подожди́ ещё год. | Turns out he didn't. Rumor has it the promotion went to Lena. And he was supposedly told to wait another year.
А: Поня́тно. Ви́димо, оби́делся. | I see. So he took offense, evidently.
Б: Похо́же на то. Хотя́, че́стно говоря́, я не уве́рена, что э́то пра́вда. Мо́жет, он про́сто в о́тпуске. | Looks that way. Though to be honest, I'm not sure it's true. Maybe he's just on vacation.
:::
